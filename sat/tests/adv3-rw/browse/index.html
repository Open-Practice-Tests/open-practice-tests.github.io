Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 1 of 27

The first telegraph cable laid across the Atlantic Ocean, completed in 1858, carried messages for only about three weeks before it failed, and a second cable snapped and was lost in midocean in 1865. Cyrus Field, the American businessman who had organized both efforts, nonetheless remained ______: he immediately began raising money for yet another attempt and assured investors that a stronger cable would succeed. In 1866 a new cable began operating, and it continued to work for years.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct choice: sanguine

Despite two failures, Field “immediately began raising money” and “assured investors that a stronger cable would succeed.” He remained sanguine—hopeful and confident about the outcome—before the 1866 success.

A: “Ambivalent” means having mixed feelings, but the text describes Field acting decisively and assuring investors of success.

B: Field was vindicated only when the 1866 cable worked; the blank describes his attitude after the 1865 failure, before any cable had lasted, so “remained vindicated” reverses the order of events.

C: “Complacent” means contentedly satisfied and so not inclined to act, but Field responded to the failures by immediately organizing another costly attempt.

Watch for: Place the blank in the story’s timeline: a word that fits the ending may not fit the moment the blank describes.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 2 of 27

The following text is from Charles Lamb’s essay “Poor Relations,” collected in his 1833 book The Last Essays of Elia. The narrator recalls W——, a proud schoolmate at Christ’s Hospital, a London charity school whose pupils wore long blue coats.

Poor W—— was of my own standing at Christ’s, a fine classic, and a youth of promise. If he had a blemish, it was too much pride; but its quality was inoffensive; it was not of that sort which hardens the heart, and serves to keep inferiors at a distance; it only sought to ward off derogation from itself. [...] Many a quarrel have I had with him, when we were rather older boys, and our tallness made us more Underlined: obnoxious End underlined. to observation in the blue clothes, because I would not thread the alleys and blind ways of the town with him to elude notice, when we have been out together on a holiday in the streets of this sneering and prying metropolis.

As used in the text, what does the word “obnoxious” most nearly mean?

Correct choice: exposed

The boys’ tallness made them easier to notice in their conspicuous uniforms, which is why W—— wanted to slip through “alleys and blind ways” to “elude notice.” Being “obnoxious to observation” here means being exposed, or liable, to it—an older sense of the word.

B: The quarrels arose because W—— cared a great deal about being noticed, so the boys were not indifferent to observation; the sentence describes how noticeable they were.

C: W—— disliked being watched, but the sentence says that the boys’ tallness made them more “obnoxious to observation”; height could not make them hostile to being seen, only more exposed to it.

D: “Offensive” is the modern sense of “obnoxious,” but the narrator is describing how easily the boys could be seen, not any offense they gave; being tall does not make anyone offensive to observation.

Watch for: In older prose, “obnoxious to” something often means “exposed” or “liable” to it; check what the context says actually happens to the people described.

To readers who know Spanish or Italian, the letter i in the English word bite seems to stand for the wrong sound, as does the ou in house to readers who know French. In Middle English, the vowel of bite sounded much like the vowel in modern beet, and the vowel of house much like the one in modern goose. Between about 1400 and 1700, English speakers gradually changed the pronunciation of these and other long vowels, a change linguists call the Great Vowel Shift. Across the same centuries, printers, beginning with William Caxton in the 1470s, were gradually settling on standard spellings for English words.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Correct choice: To suggest why some English letters seem to stand for the wrong sounds by placing two developments of the same period side by side

The text opens with a puzzle—English letters seem to stand for the “wrong” sounds—and then sets two developments side by side: the long vowels changed between 1400 and 1700 while spellings were being fixed. It leaves readers to infer that spellings settled on during or before the shift still record the older sounds, so its purpose is to account for the puzzle.

A: The text does not say that the vowel shift created a need for standard spellings; it presents the two developments as happening in the same period without making one the cause of the other.

B: The text never says that printers modeled their spellings on other languages; the comparison with Spanish, Italian and French only sets up the puzzle.

In his 1628 book on the motion of the heart, the English physician William Harvey multiplied the amount of blood that he estimated the heart sends out with each beat by the number of times it beats. Underlined: Even with cautious assumptions, he found that the heart sends out more blood in half an hour than the entire body contains. End underlined. Harvey concluded that the same blood must leave the heart and return to it over and over again.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

Correct choice: It gives a result implying that, if the first sentence were true, the liver would have to turn food into blood far faster than seems possible.

If the tissues used up the blood and the liver replaced it from food, the liver would have to make more than the body’s entire supply of blood every half hour. The underlined result shows how implausible that is, which is why Harvey concluded that the same blood must circulate.

A: The calculation concerns how much blood passes through the heart, but its role in the text is to undercut the view that the liver makes blood continually, not to explain the heart’s workload.

C: The sentence compares the heart’s output with the body’s total blood; it does not claim that the body contains less blood than anyone believed.

D: Harvey drew the opposite conclusion: the same blood returns again and again, so the tissues do not use it up.

Watch for: Ask what the number would mean if the first view were true; a calculation often works by showing that a rival view demands something impossible.

Text 1

When Nicaragua opened its first large schools for deaf children in the late 1970s, deaf students, most of whom had never met other signers, began creating a shared sign language. Over the following years the language became more systematic; for example, signers increasingly used a particular location in the space in front of them to refer back to the same person or thing. Some linguists have attributed such changes to the language’s longer use: the more years a community uses a language, the more time it has to refine it.

Text 2

Psychologists Ann Senghas and Marie Coppola compared the schools’ first signers with later entrants who learned from them. Among signers who had begun signing before about age ten, later entrants used locations to refer back to people and things more consistently than first-group signers did, even though the first group had been signing for longer. Among signers who had begun signing at older ages, entering later brought no such advantage.

Based on the texts, how would Senghas and Coppola (Text 2) most likely respond to the linguists’ explanation in Text 1?

Correct choice: They would argue that it cannot account for why the advantage appeared only among later entrants who began signing as young children.

If longer use alone refined the language, everyone signing in the community later on should have benefited, including later entrants who started as teenagers. Instead, the advantage appeared only in later entrants who started as young children—and those signers outdid first-group signers who had been signing longer. The change depended on young children learning the language, which elapsed time alone cannot explain.

A: Text 2 reports that the later entrants who began young used locations more consistently, so the language grew more systematic, not less.

B: It is true that later entrants learned an older language, but older-starting later entrants learned the same language and showed no advantage, so the language’s age cannot be what made the difference.

D: Neither text mentions spoken language, so nothing suggests that Senghas and Coppola would raise it.

Watch for: When a second text reports only a comparison, ask which explanation the comparison rules out: here, time in use is the same for groups that differ in result.

Text 1

Until the 1980s most physicians attributed stomach ulcers to stress and to excess stomach acid. Drugs that reduce acid eased patients’ pain and allowed ulcers to heal, and many physicians took this success as confirmation that acid was the underlying cause of the disease.

Text 2

Physicians Robin Warren and Barry Marshall found the bacterium Helicobacter pylori in the stomachs of most ulcer patients. In later trials, ulcers came back within about a year in most patients whose ulcers had been healed with acid-reducing drugs alone, but in far fewer patients who had also been given antibiotics that eliminated the bacterium.

Based on the texts, how would Warren and Marshall (Text 2) most likely respond to the reasoning in Text 1?

Correct choice: They would argue that the drugs’ success does not show that acid is the underlying cause, since ulcers usually returned if the bacterium remained.

Text 1 reasons that because reducing acid healed ulcers, acid must be the cause. Text 2 shows that ulcers healed that way usually returned, while eliminating the bacterium kept them away. Relief from treating a symptom does not show what caused the disease; Warren and Marshall would point to the bacterium.

A: Text 2 says the antibiotics were given in addition to acid-reducing drugs, so it offers no evidence that antibiotics alone healed ulcers or that acid plays no part.

B: Text 2 grants that the drugs healed ulcers, but its trials show that the ulcers usually came back if the bacterium remained, which undercuts the inference that acid was the underlying cause.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 9 of 27

In Homer’s epics, characters and things are regularly paired with fixed descriptive phrases: Dawn is “rosy-fingered,” ships are “swift,” and Achilles is “swift-footed,” even when he is sitting still and the ships lie beached on the shore. In the 1920s classicist Milman Parry showed that each such phrase fills a particular portion of the poem’s rhythmic line, and that for a given figure in a given position in the line, the poems almost always use one phrase and only one. Parry argued that the poems grew out of a tradition in which poets composed aloud as they performed.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct choice: Parry traced the fixed phrases in Homer’s epics to the needs of poets composing aloud, which explains why a phrase can appear where it does not suit the moment.

The text raises a puzzle (phrases that do not fit the moment), reports Parry’s finding that each phrase is tied to a position in the rhythmic line, and ends with his view that the poems come from a tradition of composing aloud. Together these explain the puzzle: a poet composing in performance reached for the phrase that fit the meter, whatever the character was doing.

A: The text attributes the mismatches to how the poems were composed, not to multiple authors; Parry’s finding that each phrase is tied to a metrical position points to the needs of composition.

B: This offers an explanation that the text does not give: Parry’s finding is that the choice of phrase depends on its position in the line, not on a quality the poet wished to stress.

D: The first part matches Parry’s finding, but the text says the poems grew out of a tradition of poets composing aloud, not that they prove a single poet working from memory.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 10 of 27

In Oliver Wendell Holmes’s 1858 poem “The Deacon’s Masterpiece,” a deacon builds a one-horse carriage, or “shay,” so that no part of it is weaker than any other, and the carriage runs for exactly a hundred years. Describing the carriage on the last morning of that century, the narrator suggests that no single part of it had worn out before the others, and he traces this to the way the deacon built it.

Which quotation from “The Deacon’s Masterpiece” most effectively illustrates the claim?

Correct choice: “But nothing local, as one may say. / There couldn’t be,—for the Deacon’s art / Had made it so like in every part / That there wasn’t a chance for one to start.”

The claim has two parts: no single part wore out first, and the narrator traces this to the deacon’s building. These lines say the decay was “nothing local” and explain why: the deacon’s art had made the carriage “so like in every part” that no part could fail first.

A: These lines state the general rule that ordinary carriages have a weakest spot somewhere; they say nothing about this carriage’s condition or why it had no weak spot.

C: These lines describe the carriage’s sudden collapse, not its condition that morning or the cause the narrator gives for it.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 11 of 27

In the 1770s and 1780s the physician Franz Anton Mesmer treated patients in Paris by passing his hands or specially prepared objects near them, claiming that an invisible fluid he called animal magnetism flowed into their bodies. Many patients reported unusual sensations, and some fell into convulsions. In 1784 a commission appointed by King Louis XVI tested Mesmer’s claim. The commissioners hypothesized that the patients’ reactions were produced by their own expectations rather than by any fluid.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the commissioners’ hypothesis?

Correct choice: Blindfolded patients reacted when told they were being treated even when no one was treating them, but did not react when treated without being told.

To support the hypothesis, expectation and treatment must be separated. Blindfolded patients reacted when they only believed they were being treated and did not react when they were treated without knowing it, so the reactions followed belief, not treatment.

A: Patients who expected effects also received the treatment, so their reactions fit a real fluid just as well as they fit expectation; this finding does not separate the two.

C: Mesmer might transmit more of the supposed fluid, or might inspire stronger expectations; this finding fits both explanations.

D: Improvement after repeated treatment fits either explanation and does not separate expectation from the supposed fluid.

Watch for: The strongest evidence separates two possible causes, showing the effect with one cause alone and its absence with the other alone.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 12 of 27

In experiments published in 1935, psychologist J. Ridley Stroop timed participants as they worked through lists of 100 items. In one experiment, participants read color words aloud, either printed in black or printed in a conflicting ink color (the word “red” in blue ink, for example). In another, participants named ink colors, either of solid squares or of color words printed in a conflicting color. Stroop concluded that a conflicting word disrupts color naming far more than a conflicting ink color disrupts word reading, citing the finding that ______

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the text?

Correct choice: conflict added 47.0 seconds to the time needed to name ink colors but only 2.3 seconds to the time needed to read color words.

The conclusion compares how much conflict slowed each task. Conflict raised color-naming time from 63.3 to 110.3 seconds, an increase of 47.0, but raised word-reading time only from 41.0 to 43.3 seconds, an increase of 2.3.

B: These figures are accurate, but they compare the two tasks under conflict only; without the times for each task without conflict, they cannot show how much conflict slowed either task.

C: This is accurate but gives only the small effect of conflict on word reading, without the large effect on color naming that the conclusion contrasts it with.

D: This has the right shape, but it misreads the table: 69.3 is the difference between color naming with conflict (110.3) and word reading without conflict (41.0); conflict added 47.0 seconds to color naming (63.3 to 110.3).

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 14 of 27

In the early 1890s physicist Lord Rayleigh prepared nitrogen in two ways: by removing the oxygen, water vapor, and carbon dioxide from ordinary air, and by releasing nitrogen from chemical compounds such as ammonia. Nitrogen obtained from air was consistently about half a percent denser than nitrogen obtained from compounds, while nitrogen released from several different compounds, by different chemical reactions, always had the same density. Because a gas’s density depends on the masses of its molecules, these results suggest that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: the gas obtained from air contained, in addition to nitrogen, a small amount of some heavier gas that had not been removed.

The gas from air was the denser one, so the difference points to something heavier in the air sample or something lighter in the chemical samples. Different reactions gave nitrogen of exactly the same density, which would be a remarkable coincidence if each reaction added its own impurity. The better explanation is that the air sample contained an unknown heavier gas. (Rayleigh and William Ramsay soon isolated it: argon.)

A: A heavier gas mixed into the chemical nitrogen would make it denser, but the chemical nitrogen was the lighter sample.

B: A lighter gas mixed into the air sample would make it less dense, but the air sample was the denser one.

C: This would explain the direction of the difference, but it is hard to square with the finding that several different reactions all gave nitrogen of exactly the same density.

Watch for: Check each explanation against both the direction of a difference and any result that stays constant across different methods.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 15 of 27

In a 1970 paper, economist George Akerlof considered a market for used cars in which each seller knows whether their own car is reliable but buyers cannot tell reliable cars from unreliable ones before buying. Unable to tell the difference, buyers will offer no more than a price that reflects the average quality of the cars for sale. Akerlof argued that in such a market, ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: owners of reliable cars, unwilling to sell below their cars’ worth, would withdraw them, lowering the average quality of cars for sale and so the price buyers offer.

An average price is too low for owners of reliable cars, so they keep their cars. That leaves a worse mix for sale, which lowers the average quality and so the price buyers will offer, which drives out still more good cars.

B: This reverses the incentives: an average price is more than an unreliable car is worth, so its owner is glad to sell; it is the owners of reliable cars who are shortchanged.

C: The text says buyers offer a price reflecting the average quality of cars for sale; if the mix stayed the same, that price would not keep falling, so this describes a spiral with no cause in the situation described.

D: Because buyers cannot tell reliable cars apart, cutting the price of a reliable car would not attract buyers to it; the decline comes from reliable cars leaving the market.

Watch for: For a process that “feeds on itself,” trace one step through: who gains or loses at the current price, what they do, and how that changes the next price.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 18 of 27

The Voynich manuscript, a book of about 240 vellum pages filled with drawings of unidentified plants and lines of an unknown script, names no ______ succeeded in reading its script since the bookseller Wilfrid Voynich acquired it in 1912.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: author, nor has anyone

“Nor” joins two independent clauses here, so a comma goes before it, and the clause it introduces inverts its subject and helping verb: “names no author, nor has anyone succeeded.”

A: When “nor” joins two independent clauses, a comma is needed before it; without one, the two clauses run together.

C: A clause that begins with “nor” must invert its subject and helping verb (“nor has anyone”); “nor anyone has” keeps ordinary word order.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 19 of 27

Soon after the Hubble Space Telescope was launched in 1990, engineers found that its main mirror had been ground to a slightly wrong shape, and they knew that unless they ______ the flaw, the telescope’s images would remain blurred; corrective optics installed by astronauts in 1993 solved the problem.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: corrected

The sentence reports in the past what the engineers knew: “unless they ______ the flaw, the … images would remain blurred.” The result clause takes “would,” and the condition takes the simple past: “unless they corrected the flaw.”

A: “Would” belongs in the result (“the images would remain blurred”), not in the “unless” clause that states the condition; the condition takes the simple past, “corrected.”

B: “Will correct” is future tense, but the sentence reports, in the past, what the engineers knew in 1990; the condition must shift to the past.

B: A semicolon cannot follow a single introductory word; outside of complex lists, a semicolon joins two independent clauses.

C: With a comma, “However” becomes a transition, and “the builders lifted so many of these blocks into place” becomes an independent clause joined to the next one by only a comma—a comma splice.
