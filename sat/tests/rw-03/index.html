Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 1 of 27

Under the old harbor system, a merchants' guild set inspection schedules and appointed inspectors. A new charter transferred both powers to an elected council and gave the guild no formal role in either task. The council would therefore ______ the guild as the authority responsible for administering harbor inspections.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

Correct choice: supplant

"Supplant" means to replace someone or something in a role. The charter transfers both scheduling and appointment powers from the guild to the council, leaving the guild with no formal role in either task. The council therefore replaces the guild as the authority administering inspections. It does not merely add support to an ongoing guild responsibility, formally approve the guild, or resolve a disagreement with it. The decisive information is the complete transfer of the two specified powers.

A: "Supplement" would mean adding to the guild's work or authority. The charter instead removes the guild's formal role in both tasks.

B: "Reconcile" would involve resolving a disagreement or making things consistent. No such disagreement or reconciliation is described.

C: "Ratify" would mean formally approve. The council replaces the guild's authority rather than confirming it.

Key takeaway: Distinguish complete replacement from addition or support; a passage's stated allocation of responsibilities can determine the required verb.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 2 of 27

When Nera designed a grain depot, neighboring growers harvested within the same two-week period. She nevertheless divided its unloading area into bays that could operate independently throughout the year. Two decades later, the introduction of staggered planting schedules made year-round deliveries common. What had seemed an unnecessary complication in Nera's design now appeared remarkably ______.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

Correct choice: prescient

"Prescient" means showing apparent knowledge of, or foresight about, future developments. The independent bays seemed unnecessary when deliveries clustered within two weeks, yet the same feature suited the later shift to year-round deliveries. The contrast between the original criticism and the later usefulness makes anticipation the essential idea. Although the design could also be called judicious in a broad sense, that word does not capture the explicit relationship between an earlier decision and a much later change.

A: A conciliatory design would ease disagreement. The passage describes a feature suited to a later development, not a compromise among growers.

C: The design proves useful after conditions change, but the passage emphasizes its original anticipation of those conditions, not its ability to be altered.

D: "Judicious" broadly means showing good judgment. "Prescient" more precisely captures a design that anticipated a development occurring two decades later.

Key takeaway: When two words are broadly positive, prefer the one that captures the passage's specific time relationship or causal relationship.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 3 of 27

To learn how readers understood an unfamiliar honorific, translator Imani Vale asked them to describe the relationship between two speakers in a short dialogue. Rather than ask whether the honorific conveyed deference, Vale used an open-ended question to elicit readers' impressions without supplying the interpretation she wanted to investigate.

As used in the text, what does the word "elicit" most nearly mean?

Correct choice: draw out

Vale asks an open-ended question so that readers will express their own impressions. In this setting, "elicit" means to draw out or bring forth a response. The sentence distinguishes obtaining readers' impressions from influencing those impressions: Vale does not want the wording of her question to plant the idea of deference. The action may precede later evaluation, but the highlighted word describes prompting the responses themselves, not assessing them or predicting what they will be.

B: The study investigates readers' impressions rather than predicting them in advance. The question prompts readers to state their impressions.

C: Vale could evaluate the impressions after receiving them, but the highlighted verb describes what her question was designed to obtain.

D: Vale specifically avoids supplying an interpretation, so "shape" conflicts with her effort to obtain responses without directing their content.

Key takeaway: Locate the word's role in the sequence of actions; gathering a response is different from interpreting or influencing it.

A feature that had threatened the instrument's usefulness became the basis of a new measurement technique.

Correct choice: It identifies the observation that led Rios to investigate a supposed defect as a useful source of information.

The sentence sits between Rios's initial classification of the second image as a defect and her eventual use of it for precise measurement. Its key observation is that image separation changes predictably with lamp position. That relationship gives Rios a reason to record the separation and explore its usefulness. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 5 of 27

A proverb in the fictional language Talic uses the same word for a riverbank and a promise. A literal translation into English preserves the river image but loses the connection to trust; a translation using "pledge" preserves that connection but makes the river image puzzling. Translator Noor Amadi instead adds a brief reference to "holding firm" while retaining the riverbank. Her version does not reproduce the original wordplay, but it allows the image and the ethical advice to reinforce each other. The example suggests that preserving a work's effects may require changing the means by which it produces them.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

Correct choice: It presents a translation problem, examines limitations of two direct solutions, and discusses an alternative that supports a broader principle.

The opening explains why the proverb presents a problem: one Talic word performs two jobs that an obvious English equivalent cannot perform together. The next sentence tests two direct solutions and shows what each loses. Amadi's alternative then retains the image while adding wording that reconnects it to the advice. Finally, the text generalizes from this example. The structure is therefore problem, limitations of direct approaches, alternative, and principle, rather than a history of revisions that eventually recreates the same verbal device.

A: Only one translator is named, and no historical context resolves a disagreement. The contrast concerns possible translation choices.

C: The general principle appears at the end. The earlier alternatives establish the problem; they are not an objection considered after a theory's application.

D: The text explicitly says Amadi's version does not reproduce the wordplay. Its success concerns the interaction of image and advice instead.

Key takeaway: Distinguish reproducing an original device from preserving its effect; structure questions often depend on that difference in the conclusion.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 6 of 27

The following passage is from an original work of fiction.

Every evening, Tessa saw the man across the courtyard seated beneath a lamp, his head bent over the same large book. She had supplied him with an occupation, a temperament, even a reason for living alone. One evening the window stood open, and she heard him counting aloud while a child corrected him. The book, she noticed now, was upside down. Tessa drew her curtain, though her own lamp had not yet been lit.

Which choice best describes the function of the bolded sentence in the passage?

Correct choice: It introduces a concrete detail that exposes how much Tessa's earlier account of the man depended on assumptions.

Tessa has built an elaborate account from the limited sight of a man bending over a book. Hearing a child correct him and noticing the book's orientation reveal that the scene does not securely support that account. The highlighted detail is therefore a check on her interpretation, not a complete explanation of the man's activity. The passage gives no basis for deciding his occupation or claiming that he intends to mislead anyone. Its focus remains the gap between what Tessa saw and what she supplied.

B: The child corrects the man's counting; no difficulty understanding his reading is stated. The detail primarily changes Tessa's perspective.

C: No occupation is confirmed. The new detail challenges Tessa's imagined account, and the passage supplies no reliable account of the man's temperament.

D: The passage does not establish the man's intentions toward observers. Tessa's assumptions are exposed without evidence of a deception aimed at her.

Key takeaway: A literary detail may undermine an observer's certainty without establishing a complete alternative explanation of the observed person's behavior.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 7 of 27

TEXT 1

Historian Mira Chen argues that the six copies of the Orlan harbor charter produced in its first year indicate that officials expected to consult it frequently. Creating so many copies, she reasons, made the charter available in several offices and reduced the risk that a dispute would arise while the only copy was elsewhere.

TEXT 2

Historian Darius Fen notes that four of the six copies remained enclosed in ceremonial cases whose seals were intact when the cases were opened a century later. The other two copies show extensive handling. Fen argues that distributing the charter served both to display the council's authority and to support officials' daily work.

Based on the texts, how would Fen (Text 2) most likely respond to Chen's argument (Text 1)?

Correct choice: The physical condition of the copies suggests that practical consultation explains the production of some copies more convincingly than it explains the production of all six.

Chen explains the production of six copies through access for practical consultation. Fen's evidence divides those copies into two groups: two were handled extensively, while four remained sealed in ceremonial cases. The handled copies fit Chen's account, but the sealed copies suggest another purpose for much of the duplication. Fen would therefore qualify the scope of Chen's explanation rather than reject practical consultation altogether. His account does not rank the importance of the two purposes or estimate how often particular disputes occurred.

A: Neither text connects survival rates to consultation frequency. Fen distinguishes uses among the six copies rather than estimating unrecorded consultation.

B: The numerical difference between sealed and handled copies does not establish the relative importance of ceremonial and practical purposes to officials.

C: Chen uses all six copies as evidence. The four sealed copies directly matter because they complicate a practical-use explanation for the full set.

Key takeaway: When a second text divides evidence into groups, check whether it limits the first text's explanation to only one group.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 8 of 27

TEXT 1

Astronomer Leena Das proposes that gas is steadily flowing outward from a distant star. In spectra collected on one night, light from two regions of the star showed the same shift toward shorter wavelengths. Das argues that agreement between the regions favors a steady outflow over a surface oscillation.

TEXT 2

Astronomer Owen Pell notes that some surface oscillations can make two regions move in the same direction during part of a cycle. Later in that cycle, however, their motions diverge. A single observation of matching wavelength shifts would therefore be insufficient to distinguish such an oscillation from a steady outflow.

Based on Text 2, which additional information would Pell most likely consider necessary for assessing Das's conclusion?

Correct choice: Whether the two regions continue to show matching wavelength shifts in observations covering a possible oscillation cycle.

Pell identifies a temporal ambiguity. Both steady outflow and part of an oscillation cycle can produce matching shifts in the two regions. Their predictions differ later: an oscillation would make the regions' motions diverge, whereas the proposed steady outflow predicts continued agreement. Observations spanning a possible cycle would therefore test the distinction Pell says the single-night evidence cannot settle. Confirming that outflow can produce the observed pattern would not suffice, because an alternative can produce that pattern at one moment as well.

A: Pell distinguishes proposed motions using their timing. Equal light output at other wavelengths does not address that specific ambiguity.

B: Another same-night match would still sample one phase. Pell's objection concerns changes across a cycle, which more simultaneous locations may not resolve.

D: Pell allows that a steady outflow could produce the shifts. The unresolved issue is whether oscillation could explain the same single-night observation.

Key takeaway: To distinguish competing explanations, seek evidence from the condition or time when their predictions differ, not another shared prediction.

Adding a temperature sensor reduces the operating burden but does not remove the need for inspection. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 10 of 27

The following passage is from an original work of fiction.

Leon had remembered the courthouse clock as enormous. Returning after twenty years, he found a modest dial above a narrow door. He was prepared to blame the building's restoration until he noticed that the chipped numeral he used to stare at was still there, exactly where it had been. As a boy waiting for his mother to finish work, he had measured whole afternoons by that numeral. He stood across the street now, late for an appointment, and wondered how a clock so small had once contained so much time.

Which choice best states the main idea of the passage?

Correct choice: A familiar object prompts Leon to recognize a difference between its physical dimensions and the importance it held in his childhood experience.

The clock appears small in the present but was enormous in Leon's memory. The unchanged chipped numeral links the present object to the one he watched as a child, while the memory of long afternoons explains its former significance. His final thought connects the clock's perceived size to the time and attention he invested in it. The passage therefore examines the relationship between an object's physical scale and its remembered importance, rather than documenting restoration or claiming that Leon can regain his childhood experience.

A: Restoration is an explanation Leon considers, not an established fact. The chipped numeral redirects him toward his earlier experience of time.

B: He is late for an appointment, but the text does not show that attachment makes him unwilling to go. The focus is perception and remembered waiting.

C: Leon notices a contrast in experience; he does not recover or feel reassured about regaining his childhood sense of time.

Key takeaway: In literary main-idea questions, distinguish a character's provisional explanation from the understanding toward which the passage moves.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 11 of 27

In her English translation of a fictional island's administrative code, translator Eda Marr retained the island-language term "saven" rather than replacing it with "lease." Scholar Tomi Venn argues that Marr's choice was intended to preserve a legal distinction: a saven granted temporary use of land but included obligations that an ordinary English lease did not necessarily include.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support Venn's interpretation?

Correct choice: In a note accompanying the translation, Marr explained that using "lease" would conceal the holder's obligation to maintain a public footpath across the land.

Venn makes a claim about intention and a particular semantic distinction. Marr's own note directly addresses both: she rejected "lease" because that word would obscure an obligation attached to a saven, namely maintaining a public footpath. This is substantially stronger evidence than the general fact that translators sometimes retain original-language terms. It also goes beyond demonstrating that "saven" concerns land use. The finding links the specific translation choice to the specific additional duty that Venn says Marr wanted to preserve.

B: Consistent usage establishes the term's subject matter, but it does not identify an obligation that would be lost by translating it as "lease."

C: A general practice among translators does not identify Marr's reason for retaining this particular term or establish the legal distinction Venn proposes.

D: This concerns readers' behavior after encountering the term, not Marr's intended distinction between a saven and an ordinary lease.

Key takeaway: Evidence for a claimed intention should connect the person's choice to the proposed reason, not merely document similar choices elsewhere.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 12 of 27

In an experiment with young orchard trees, removing large surface stones increased growth. The investigators proposed that the increase occurred mainly because stone removal reduced physical obstruction of the trees' roots. Other conditions, including irrigation and nutrient supply, were held constant. A second team questioned the proposed mechanism, noting that surface stones could also affect soil temperature.

Which finding from a follow-up experiment would most directly weaken the investigators' proposed explanation?

Correct choice: When shallow tiles recreated the temperature conditions of the original stony plots without entering the root zone, the growth advantage of stone removal disappeared.

The proposed mechanism predicts that trees should benefit while the root zone remains less obstructed. In the follow-up, shallow tiles restore the original temperature conditions without putting obstacles back among the roots. If the growth advantage disappears under those conditions, reduced obstruction alone does not explain the benefit. The result instead links the benefit to the temperature change that stone removal produced. This weakens the claim that reduced physical obstruction was the main cause; it need not establish that obstruction had no effect whatsoever.

A: Similar moisture rules out one possible difference but does not distinguish reduced root obstruction from altered soil temperature as the main mechanism.

C: The added removal could further reduce obstruction or alter temperature. Without separating those changes, the finding does not directly challenge the proposed mechanism.

D: Greater root extension after removal is consistent with reduced obstruction. It does not undermine that proposed explanation for increased growth.

Key takeaway: To test a proposed mechanism, separate it from another change that originally occurred at the same time.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 14 of 27

Until 1849, the town of Belven permitted only property owners to sign council petitions. Beginning in 1850, any adult resident could sign. Clerks checked eligibility whenever a signature was added, including signatures added to older petitions. An original petition dated 1847 bears Mara Vey's signature. Complete property records show that Vey never owned property, and residency records establish that she was an adult resident from 1845 onward.

Which conclusion is most strongly supported by the text?

Correct choice: Vey's signature was added to the petition after the year in which the petition was originally dated.

Before 1850, Vey was ineligible because she never owned property. From 1850 onward, she qualified as an adult resident. Since clerks checked eligibility whenever a signature was added, her accepted signature must have been added under the later rule, in 1850 or afterward. The petition itself is dated 1847, so the signature is later than its original date. This establishes a sequence without revealing when the council considered the petition or what its first signers expected to happen.

A: Clerks checked eligibility, and the rule changed in 1850. Nothing supports an exception or reinterpretation of the earlier ownership requirement.

C: Older petitions could receive later signatures, but the original signers' expectations and intentions are not described.

D: The text supplies no consideration date or evidence that the council delayed action. Only the signature's permissible timing can be inferred.

Key takeaway: Apply eligibility conditions to the moment of the relevant action, rather than assuming every part of a document shares its original date.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 15 of 27

A telescope sends light through a shared lens and then separates it into two paths, each ending at a detector. Investigators found that images from the left path had become blurred, whereas images from the right path remained sharp. They exchanged the two detectors without changing the paths. The left-path images remained blurred. Earlier calibration had established that a defect in the shared lens would blur images from both paths under these conditions.

Which conclusion is most strongly supported by the text?

Correct choice: The source of the blur is more likely in a component specific to the left light path than in either the shared lens or the original left detector.

Two observations narrow the likely source. Exchanging detectors leaves the blur in the left path, so the problem does not follow the original left detector. Calibration also says a shared-lens defect would blur both outputs, yet the right-path images remain sharp. Together these findings point toward something specific to the left path after the light separates. The conclusion appropriately identifies a more likely location; it does not claim that the investigators have already identified the precise faulty component or measured differences in detector sensitivity.

B: Blur existed before the exchange and stayed with the left path after detectors moved. Damage during the exchange does not explain that pattern.

C: Calibration says a shared-lens defect would blur both paths under these conditions. The continued one-path blur weighs against that explanation.

D: The text does not compare detector sensitivity. Its calibration and exchange evidence locate the likely fault rather than measuring sensitivity.

Key takeaway: Follow which feature stays with a problem after components are exchanged, then use controls to rule out shared upstream causes.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 16 of 27

The observatory's first star chart plotted positions measured on clear winter nights. Its later chart incorporated summer observations ______ the two charts used the same system of coordinates, allowing astronomers to compare them directly.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: as well; however,

The first relevant independent clause is "Its later chart incorporated summer observations as well." The next is "the two charts used the same system of coordinates." A semicolon can connect these related independent clauses. "However" introduces the second clause and is followed by a comma. The completed sequence, "as well; however, the two charts," therefore supplies the required clause boundary. A comma alone creates a comma splice, and the absence of punctuation produces a run-on.

A: This places a semicolon after the introductory conjunctive adverb, separating "however" from its clause rather than correctly introducing that clause.

B: Without punctuation after "well," two independent clauses run together. "However" cannot join them by itself.

D: A comma alone cannot join the independent clauses ending with "as well" and beginning with "however, the two charts."

Key takeaway: With "however" between independent clauses, check for a complete clause boundary before it and a comma after it.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 20 of 27

How the brass instruments recovered from the council chamber reached that location ______ uncertain, even though markings on several pieces identify the workshop that manufactured them.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: remains

The subject is the entire clause "How the brass instruments recovered from the council chamber reached that location." A clause functioning as a subject takes a singular verb here, so "remains" is correct. The plural "instruments" is the subject of the verb "reached" inside that larger clause; it does not control the main verb after the blank. Likewise, "markings" controls only "identify" in the later clause. Tracking those separate clause structures prevents nearby plural nouns from misleading the agreement choice.

A: The subject of the main verb is the entire "How" clause, which takes singular agreement here. "Instruments" belongs inside that subject clause.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 21 of 27

The experimental grain elevator opened in 1882. By the time an enlarged rail depot began serving the district in 1904, the elevator ______ for more than two decades, and local growers had come to rely on its storage capacity.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: had been operating

The phrase "By the time" establishes a past reference point: the rail depot began service in 1904. The elevator's operation began earlier, in 1882, and continued through the intervening period. "Had been operating" uses the past perfect progressive to describe that ongoing activity before and up to another past event. It also fits the stated duration, "for more than two decades." The future and present forms shift the time frame away from 1904, and "have" would additionally disagree with the singular subject.

A: Present progressive places the operation in the present instead of expressing its duration before a specified past event.

B: Future perfect progressive is incompatible with the stated past reference point, the depot's opening in 1904.

D: The present perfect auxiliary "have" neither matches the singular subject nor places the duration before the stated 1904 reference point.

Key takeaway: When an ongoing activity precedes a specified past event, past perfect progressive can express its duration up to that event.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 22 of 27

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The opening participial phrase, "Comparing the handwritten draft with the printed decree," describes an action that must be performed by the subject immediately following it. Mentioning Pell elsewhere in those phrases does not make Pell the subject or correctly attach the modifier. The sentence needs the person performing the comparison in the subject position.

B: A discovery cannot compare documents. The introductory participial phrase must modify the person performing the comparison.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 23 of 27

A crop-drying system originally directed warm air through one long chamber. Measurements showed that air near the exit had already absorbed enough moisture to dry additional grain only slowly. ______, the engineers divided the chamber into shorter sections, each supplied with fresh warm air, to avoid repeatedly exposing grain to moisture-laden air.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Correct choice: Accordingly

The measurements reveal a problem: air loses drying effectiveness as it acquires moisture while traveling through the long chamber. Dividing the chamber and adding fresh air supplies directly addresses that problem. "Accordingly" signals an action taken in response to the preceding finding. The relationship is therefore diagnosis followed by a suitable consequence, not contrast, illustration, or similarity between independent cases. The purpose phrase "to avoid repeatedly exposing grain to moisture-laden air" makes the connection between evidence and redesign especially explicit.

A: The redesign is a response to the measurement, not an example of air near the exit already containing moisture.

The translator retained the narrator's repeated questions — a feature some early reviewers had called distracting ______ she removed explanations that answered those questions before readers could consider them independently.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: — but

The phrase "a feature some early reviewers had called distracting" interrupts the main sentence and begins with a dash. The correct choice closes that interruption with a matching dash and uses "but" to coordinate two independent clauses: the translator retained the questions, but she removed premature answers. The comma and semicolon choices fail to close the dash pair. "But although" closes the interruption but begins a dependent clause after the coordinating conjunction, without the independent clause needed to complete the resulting structure.

A: The semicolon does not close the inserted description and incorrectly places a clause boundary inside the dash-marked interruption.

B: The inserted description begins with a dash. A comma cannot close that dash-marked interruption before the coordinating conjunction.

C: After "but," "although" begins a dependent clause without the independent clause needed to complete the coordinated structure.

Key takeaway: When a choice must both close an interruption and connect clauses, check the matching boundary and the connector separately.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 20 of 27

Of the eight surviving diagrams that show the observatory's original roof mechanism, not one ______ the angle at which the shutters were intended to rest when fully open, a detail essential to an accurate reconstruction.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: specifies

The opening "Of the eight surviving diagrams ..." identifies a group. The actual subject of the main clause is "not one," a singular expression meaning no individual member of that group. The singular verb "specifies" agrees with it. The plural verb "show" earlier in the sentence correctly belongs to "diagrams" inside the introductory phrase, but that separate relationship does not make the later subject plural. The words after the blank supply the object of "specifies" and an appositive explaining its importance.

B: The plural auxiliary "are" does not agree with the singular subject "not one," despite the nearby reference to eight diagrams.

C: The subject is "not one," which is singular. "Diagrams" belongs to the introductory "Of" phrase and does not determine the main verb.

D: The plural auxiliary "have" disagrees with "not one." The introductory group of eight diagrams does not make the grammatical subject plural.

Key takeaway: In a sentence beginning "Of the ...," find the subject that follows the introductory group rather than letting the group's number control agreement.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 21 of 27

Unlike the earlier seed separator, which sorted mainly by size, ______ differences in the time seeds take to fall through a moving stream of air, allowing seeds of similar size but different density to be separated.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: the new apparatus uses

The introductory "Unlike" phrase names an earlier seed separator, so the subject immediately following the phrase should identify the corresponding thing being contrasted with it. "The new apparatus" supplies that parallel entity. The remaining sentence then explains its different sorting basis: fall time in moving air rather than mainly size. The other choices all mention the apparatus, but their grammatical subjects are its designers, its operation, or its use. Those subjects produce mismatched comparisons with the earlier machine itself.

B: The introductory "Unlike" phrase compares an earlier separator with the main subject. Designers are people, not a comparable apparatus.

C: This compares the earlier separator itself with an operation, rather than comparing the two separators. The introductory comparison is improperly matched.

D: The main subject would be "the use," producing a mismatched comparison with "the earlier seed separator."

Key takeaway: For "unlike" comparisons, compare corresponding entities in the grammatical subject positions, not merely related words mentioned somewhere in the sentence.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 22 of 27

Preparing the bilingual edition involved identifying changes made in the author's handwritten corrections, distinguishing those changes from marks added by a later reader, and, after the two kinds of marks had been separated, ______ each revised passage against the first printed edition.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: checking

The verb "involved" takes a three-part series describing the work of preparing the edition: identifying changes, distinguishing kinds of marks, and checking revised passages. Each item is a gerund phrase. The dependent clause beginning "after" interrupts the third item's introduction but does not change the required parallel form. "Checking" therefore continues the pattern. The other choices supply a finite clause, an infinitive, or a past-tense verb, none of which fits the same coordinated structure governed by "involved."

A: The clause "they checked" cannot serve as the third parallel object of "involved" alongside the gerunds "identifying" and "distinguishing."

B: The past-tense form "checked" does not match the gerunds that function as the first two coordinated objects of "involved."

C: The infinitive "to check" breaks the parallel series of gerund phrases governed by "involved": identifying, distinguishing, and checking.

Key takeaway: Remove an interruption temporarily to reveal the structure of a list: involved identifying, distinguishing, and checking.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 23 of 27

The new star atlas recorded several kinds of information that an earlier edition had omitted. ______, it marked pairs of stars that appear close together from Earth but are known to lie at very different distances. Those markings helped readers avoid treating every apparent pair as a physically associated system.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Correct choice: For example

The opening makes a general claim that the new atlas includes previously omitted kinds of information. The next sentence names a particular addition: distinguishing apparent pairs whose members lie at very different distances. "For example" correctly connects the specific case to the broader claim. The final sentence explains the value of that example. No opposing expectation or replacement is introduced, and treating the specific marking as a causal consequence of the general description would reverse the explanatory relationship.

B: The fact that the atlas added information does not itself cause this particular marking practice; the practice illustrates what information was added.

C: The later information is one instance of the newly included material, not an alternative replacing the claim that the atlas added information.

D: Nothing about marking apparent pairs conflicts with or persists despite the atlas's inclusion of new information.

Key takeaway: When a general statement is followed by a concrete instance of it, choose an example transition rather than a result transition.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 24 of 27

In a new irrigation layout, a pump fills an elevated reservoir during the afternoon. At night, gravity carries the stored water to field channels while the pump is switched off. ______, the system allows the time when water reaches the crops to be separated from the time when electricity is used to raise that water, even though the supply still depends on pumping.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Correct choice: Thus

The reservoir stores water that was pumped upward earlier, and gravity delivers it later without the pump operating. The last sentence draws the consequence of that arrangement: raising water and delivering it to crops can occur at different times. "Thus" signals this supported conclusion. The final qualification preserves an important fact - pumping is still needed to provide the stored supply - without changing the inferential relationship between the sentences. The sentence is not a simultaneous event, a conceded objection, or another particular example.

A: The last sentence draws a general implication from the stated operating sequence. It does not add another example of that sequence.

C: The last sentence states what follows from the design; it does not concede a point before presenting an opposing position.

D: The last sentence explains a consequence of the storage arrangement rather than introducing another event occurring at the same time.

Key takeaway: A stored resource can separate the timing of supply and use; select a conclusion transition when a sentence explains that consequence.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 25 of 27

The provincial assembly first authorized a survey of the disputed boundary. Surveyors then spent two seasons comparing landmarks with the descriptions in older agreements. Only after their report had been circulated to every district did the assembly schedule a final debate. ______, representatives proposed amendments to the survey's recommendations, and the assembly voted on each amendment before adopting the boundary.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Correct choice: During that debate

The passage traces a sequence: authorization, fieldwork, circulation of the report, and a final debate. The last sentence tells what occurred in that debate, namely proposing and voting on amendments before adopting the boundary. "During that debate" locates those actions in the established sequence. The other choices either put the events at the wrong time or impose a causal or comparative relationship that the passage does not supply. The repeated reference to "that debate" provides a precise connection to the immediately preceding event.

A: The text describes different stages of a process, not a second method or action analogous to the surveyors' comparison.

C: The text places amendments after the report was circulated and the final debate was scheduled, not before the survey began.

D: The sentence supplies the next stage and actions within the debate. It does not identify a causal reason that led representatives to propose particular amendments.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 26 of 27

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

• A research team compared two hypothetical methods for aligning a telescope's mirror segments.

• Method R required 50 minutes for the initial alignment and 5 minutes after a routine temperature change.

• Method S required 20 minutes for the initial alignment and 18 minutes after the same kind of temperature change.

• Each completed alignment met the team's stated accuracy requirement.

• The team cautioned that the faster choice over an observing season would depend on how often realignment was needed.

The student wants to explain why the notes do not establish a single method as faster for every observing schedule. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Correct choice: Method S is faster for initial alignment, but Method R is faster for later realignments, so the time advantage depends on how often realignment is required.

The notes establish a tradeoff: S takes less time initially, while R takes less time on each later realignment. The total time over a schedule therefore depends on how often the later task occurs. The correct sentence states both sides of that tradeoff and the resulting dependence. Accuracy is held adequate for both methods, so it does not resolve the timing comparison. Declaring either method universally faster would require a frequency assumption that the notes deliberately do not provide.

A: This concerns suitability and accuracy. It omits the contrasting setup and realignment times needed to explain why overall speed depends on the schedule.

B: The conclusion ignores that R needs only 5 minutes for realignment while S needs 18. Initial speed does not establish speed for every schedule.

D: This assumes enough realignments to offset R's longer initial setup. The notes explicitly leave their frequency open.

Key takeaway: When initial and repeated costs favor different options, preserve the dependence on repetition frequency instead of declaring an unconditional winner.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 27 of 27

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

• A translator tested two versions of an original short story with different groups of readers.

• Version P retained an unusual sentence order and included a brief introduction explaining that the narrator often postpones naming people.

• Version Q used conventional sentence order and had no explanatory introduction.

• Readers of P identified the narrator's evasiveness more often than readers of Q did.

• The translator concluded that the tested package of sentence order plus introduction could make the narrator's evasiveness clearer.

• The comparison did not isolate the contribution of sentence order from that of the introduction.

The student wants to report the finding while preserving the limitation on what caused the difference. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Correct choice: Readers identified evasiveness more often with the unusual-order version and its introduction, but the comparison did not establish how much either feature contributed to that difference.

The observed finding is a higher rate of identifying evasiveness for P, which combines unusual order with an introduction. Because Q differs on both features, the comparison cannot separate their contributions. The correct sentence reports the actual package-level difference while preserving that attribution limit. It neither assigns effects to individual features nor discards the observed result altogether. Both errors are tempting: a limitation on causal explanation does not erase a measured difference, but a measured difference between packages does not identify which component produced it.

B: The design compares packages, not the introduction against sentence order. It cannot establish this feature-by-feature causal ranking.

C: This assigns separate causal contributions to both features, although the comparison did not isolate either contribution.

D: The limitation concerns attribution to individual features, not the existence of a difference between the tested packages.

Key takeaway: Report a measured difference at the level actually compared, and keep a limitation on component attribution separate from denial of the finding.
