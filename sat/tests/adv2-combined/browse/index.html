Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 1 of 27

The following text is from Theodore Dreiser’s 1900 novel Sister Carrie. Hurstwood, who tricked Carrie into leaving Chicago with him, has just asked her to marry him.

Carrie had never been ill-disposed toward him. Only a moment before she had been listening with some Underlined: complacency End underlined., remembering her old affection for him. He was so handsome, so daring!

Now, however, this feeling had changed to one of opposition, which rose feebly.

As used in the text, what does the word “complacency” most nearly mean?

Correct choice: gratification

Carrie was “remembering her old affection” for Hurstwood and thinking him “so handsome, so daring!” She had been listening with pleasure—an older sense of “complacency” meaning quiet satisfaction or gratification.

A: The modern word “complacent” suggests unconcern, but Carrie was not unconcerned: she was recalling her affection and admiring Hurstwood, which is pleasure, not indifference.

C: Her opposition “rose feebly” afterward, but before that she was listening with pleasure; nothing suggests she had passively given up.

D: Carrie “had never been ill-disposed toward him,” and she was remembering her affection, so she was not listening with suspicion.

Watch for: In older texts, “complacency” often means simple pleasure or satisfaction, without the modern sense of self-satisfied unconcern.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 2 of 27

A 1993 study reported that college students scored slightly higher on a spatial-reasoning task after listening to ten minutes of a Mozart sonata than after sitting in silence, an improvement that lasted only about fifteen minutes. Within a few years, the finding had been recast in popular accounts as evidence that playing classical music to infants makes them smarter. Psychologists who reviewed the research judged this popular version ______: it offered an appealingly simple lesson only by ignoring how small, brief, and specific the original effect had been.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct choice: facile

The colon explains the judgment: the popular version was appealingly simple, but only because it ignored the original effect’s limits. “Facile” means easily achieved or stated but superficial, which captures both the appeal and the criticism.

B: “Straightforward” captures the simplicity but carries no criticism; the text says the simplicity was achieved “only by ignoring” the evidence, a fault the word does not express.

C: The popular version was indeed derived from the study, but “derivative” means unoriginal or imitative, and the colon explains a different fault: false simplicity, not lack of originality.

D: “Succinct” praises brevity; the psychologists were faulting the popular version for oversimplifying, not complimenting it for being brief.

Watch for: When a colon explains a judgment, the word must match every part of the explanation—here both “appealingly simple” and “only by ignoring.”

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 3 of 27

The following text is from Charles Dickens’s 1857 novel Little Dorrit. The Circumlocution Office is a fictional government department.

This glorious establishment had been early in the field, when the one sublime principle involving the difficult art of governing a country, was first distinctly revealed to statesmen. It had been foremost to study that bright revelation and to carry its shining influence through the whole of the official proceedings. Underlined: Whatever was required to be done, the Circumlocution Office was beforehand with all the public departments in the art of perceiving—HOW NOT TO DO IT. End underlined.

Through this delicate perception, through the tact with which it invariably seized it, and through the genius with which it always acted on it, the Circumlocution Office had risen to overtop all the public departments; and the public condition had risen to be—what it was.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

Correct choice: It reveals that the accomplishment the narrator has been lavishly praising is in fact a talent for avoiding action.

The first two sentences praise the office’s “sublime principle” and “bright revelation” without saying what it is. The underlined sentence names it—“HOW NOT TO DO IT”—exposing the praise as ironic: the office’s celebrated skill is avoiding action. The closing “the public condition had risen to be—what it was” continues the irony.

A: This takes the praise literally. The principle is “HOW NOT TO DO IT,” and the final clause (“the public condition had risen to be—what it was”) signals that the public was not well served.

B: The later praise of the office’s “tact” and “genius” continues the same irony rather than withdrawing a criticism; it applies the mock praise to the office’s skill at not acting.

D: The text says the office was “beforehand with all the public departments,” but it never mentions resentment; the other departments are simply outdone in the same pursuit.

Watch for: In satire, a sentence that finally names what has been praised often reveals that the praise was ironic all along.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 4 of 27

In the 1790s the naturalist Lazzaro Spallanzani found that bats whose eyes had been covered could still fly through a darkened room without striking threads strung across it. Soon afterward, Louis Jurine reported that bats whose ears had been tightly plugged collided with objects. Underlined: Spallanzani then fitted bats with small tubes inserted into their ears; when the tubes were left open, the bats flew normally, but when the tubes were closed, the bats blundered into obstacles. End underlined. Because neither man could hear any sound from the flying bats, the idea that bats find their way by hearing was widely doubted until the late 1930s, when researchers recorded the animals’ high-pitched calls, which lie beyond the range of human hearing.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

Correct choice: It rules out the possibility that Jurine’s bats collided with objects merely because something was placed in their ears.

Jurine’s plugged bats collided with objects, but plugging an ear might disturb a bat in ways unrelated to hearing. Spallanzani’s tubes were in the bats’ ears in both conditions; only whether sound could pass through changed. Because the bats flew normally with open tubes, the collisions depended on blocking sound, not on the presence of something in the ear.

B: Bats with closed tubes “blundered into obstacles,” which agrees with Jurine’s report; the experiment confirms and strengthens his finding rather than challenging it.

C: The covered-eye experiment succeeded in showing that bats do not need sight; the underlined sentence does not revisit it or explain any failure.

D: The tube experiment shows that navigation depends on hearing, but nothing in it shows that the bats produce sounds or listen for echoes; that was established only when researchers recorded the calls in the 1930s.

Watch for: When an experiment keeps an intervention in place but changes one feature of it, ask which alternative explanation it is designed to exclude.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 5 of 27

In 1991 psychologists Diane Halpern and Stanley Coren reported that among recently deceased people whose handedness their relatives could recall, left-handers had died about nine years younger, on average, than right-handers, and they suggested that left-handedness shortens life. Underlined: People born in the early twentieth century, however, were far less likely to be identified as left-handed than people born later, since many children of that era were made to write with their right hands. End underlined. A later study that followed a large group of older adults for several years found no difference in survival between left- and right-handers.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

Correct choice: It points out a difference between generations that could produce Halpern and Coren’s result even if handedness does not affect how long people live.

If older generations include few left-handers, then left-handers among recent deaths will be mostly younger people, and their average age at death will be lower even if handedness has no effect on life span. The sentence supplies this alternative explanation, and the later study’s finding of no difference fits it.

B: The sentence concerns how common left-handedness was in different generations, not whether relatives remembered it correctly.

C: The sentence does imply that less discouragement made left-handedness more common, but its role in the text is to explain Halpern and Coren’s result without any effect on life span, not to explain trends in handedness for their own sake.

D: The sentence says that forced switching made older people less likely to be (recorded as) left-handed; it says nothing about the health or life span of the children who were switched.

Watch for: When a group’s makeup differs by birth year, its average age at death can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with the trait being studied.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 6 of 27

Text 1

Astronomer Ivo Pell attributes a star’s periodic dimming to a planet passing in front of it. The star’s color remains nearly constant during each dimming event. Pell notes that a cool spot rotating across the star’s visible surface would make the observed light redder, so the color measurements favor his interpretation over the cool-spot explanation.

Text 2

Astronomer Mina Orr agrees that the color measurements weigh against a cool spot. She notes, however, that two stars of nearly the same color could eclipse one another without appreciably changing the combined light’s color. Measurements of the light sources’ motions would be needed to distinguish that possibility from Pell’s planet interpretation.

How would Orr (Text 2) most likely characterize the reasoning in Text 1?

Correct choice: It uses evidence that distinguishes the planet interpretation from one alternative but does not yet distinguish it from another viable alternative.

Orr accepts Pell’s comparison with the cool-spot explanation: the nearly unchanged color is unfavorable to that alternative. Her qualification is that this success does not establish the planet interpretation uniquely. An eclipsing pair of similar-colored stars could also produce dimming without much color change. Pell’s evidence therefore separates two explanations but leaves a third unresolved. Orr’s proposed motion measurements would address the remaining comparison. She is not rejecting the color evidence itself or reporting results from measurements that have not yet been made.

A: This reverses the evidence: color weighs against a cool spot but leaves the similar-colored stellar pair unresolved.

B: No motion measurements have been reported, and Orr does not claim they support a cool spot. She recommends obtaining them.

C: Orr explicitly agrees that the color measurements weigh against a cool spot. Her objection concerns a different alternative.

Watch for: Eliminating one alternative does not establish a favored explanation when another alternative predicts the same evidence.

Text 1

Temple inscriptions from the reign of the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses III describe invaders, whom historians call the Sea Peoples, overrunning lands from Anatolia to Cyprus before an attack on Egypt around 1177 BCE. Many historians concluded that these raiders alone had destroyed the great cities of the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Bronze Age.

Excavations show that the region’s cities were destroyed over several decades rather than in a single wave, and some destruction layers show damage consistent with earthquakes rather than attack. Historian Eric Cline argues that earthquakes, drought and famine, and disrupted trade, together with the raids of the Sea Peoples, weakened the region’s interconnected kingdoms until they collapsed one after another.

Based on the texts, how would Cline (Text 2) most likely respond to the conclusion of the historians described in Text 1?

Correct choice: He would accept that the Sea Peoples contributed to the destruction but reject the idea that their raids alone brought the kingdoms down.

The historians in Text 1 held the Sea Peoples alone responsible for the destruction. Cline lists their raids among several causes—earthquakes, drought and famine, and disrupted trade—that together weakened the kingdoms. He would grant the raids a part while rejecting them as the sole cause.

A: Cline includes “the raids of the Sea Peoples” among the causes of collapse, so he would not deny them any part.

B: Cline accepts that the raids played a part, but he would not agree that they were responsible for the destruction, since he attributes it to several causes acting together.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 8 of 27

Text 1

In a 1985 study, psychologists Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone, and Amos Tversky had college basketball players each take 100 shots from spots where the player made about half of their attempts. For each player, the researchers compared the percentage of shots made immediately after three consecutive makes with the player’s overall percentage. Finding no meaningful difference, they concluded that the widespread belief in a “hot hand”—that players become more likely to score after a string of successes—is an illusion.

Text 2

Economists Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo have identified a subtle bias in such comparisons. Consider a player whose chance of making each shot never changes and who takes 100 shots, as in the 1985 study. If one calculates the percentage of shots made immediately after three consecutive makes, that percentage will, on average, be noticeably lower than the player’s overall percentage.

Based on the texts, how would Miller and Sanjurjo (Text 2) most likely respond to the conclusion in Text 1?

Correct choice: They would argue that finding no difference suggests that the players shot better after three makes than players with unchanging chances would be expected to.

Text 2 shows that for a player whose chances never change, the percentage made after three straight makes is expected to fall below the overall percentage. Text 1’s players, however, made shots after three makes at about their overall rate—better than that expected shortfall. So the data Text 1 took as showing no hot hand actually point toward one.

B: Text 2 describes a bias in short series; nothing suggests that more shots would push the players’ post-streak percentages below their overall percentages.

C: This reverses the bias: the calculation Text 2 describes makes post-streak shooting look worse, not better, than a constant chance would produce, so correcting for it strengthens the case for a hot hand.

D: The spots were chosen so that each player made about half of their attempts; Text 2 raises a problem with the calculation, not with the difficulty of the shots.

Watch for: When Text 2 changes what a result should be compared with, recompute what Text 1’s result means against the new benchmark.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 9 of 27

The following text is from Sherwood Anderson’s 1919 book Winesburg, Ohio. The narrator describes the central thought of an unpublished book written by an old man.

The thought was involved but a simple statement of it would be something like this:

That in the beginning when the world was young there were a great many thoughts but no such thing as a truth. Man made the truths himself and each truth was a composite of a great many vague thoughts. All about in the world were the truths and they were all beautiful. [...] And then the people came along. Each as he appeared snatched up one of the truths and some who were quite strong snatched up a dozen of them.

It was the truths that made the people grotesques. [...] It was his notion that the moment one of the people took one of the truths to himself, called it his truth, and tried to live his life by it, he became a grotesque and the truth he embraced became a falsehood.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct choice: According to the old man’s theory, truths that are beautiful in themselves become falsehoods, and distort people, once someone claims one as his own and lives by it.

The truths are “all beautiful,” yet “it was the truths that made the people grotesques”: the moment a person takes a truth “to himself, called it his truth, and tried to live his life by it,” the person becomes a grotesque and the truth becomes a falsehood. The harm lies in appropriating a truth, not in the truth itself.

A: The text reverses this causation: “It was the truths that made the people grotesques.” People become grotesques by taking up a truth, not because they are grotesque already.

B: The truths “were all beautiful,” and a truth “became a falsehood” only when someone embraced it as his own; it was not false from the beginning.

C: The text notes that some strong people “snatched up a dozen” truths but never says that this spared them; the theory says anyone who takes a truth as his own becomes a grotesque.

Watch for: When a text states a paradox (beautiful truths make people grotesque), the main idea must explain how both halves can hold.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 10 of 27

In his 1877 essay “An Apology for Idlers,” Robert Louis Stevenson inverts a common judgment of character, treating relentless industry as a sign of inner poverty and a gift for leisure as the mark of a rich, many-sided nature.

Which quotation from “An Apology for Idlers” most effectively illustrates the claim?

Correct choice: “Extreme busyness, whether at school or college, kirk or market, is a symptom of deficient vitality; and a faculty for idleness implies a catholic appetite and a strong sense of personal identity.”

The claim has two parts: relentless industry signals inner poverty, and a gift for leisure marks a rich, many-sided nature. This quotation makes both points: busyness is “a symptom of deficient vitality,” and a faculty for idleness implies “a catholic appetite”—wide-ranging interests—and “a strong sense of personal identity.”

A: This recommends idleness for the young but says nothing about what busyness or idleness reveals about a person’s vitality or interests.

B: This portrays busy people as incurious, which fits the idea of inner poverty, but it says nothing about what a gift for leisure reveals, the claim’s second part.

D: This criticizes constant work for its cost—the neglect of other things—and questions the importance of business, rather than presenting it as a sign of deficient vitality, and it says nothing about idlers’ interests.

Watch for: With a two-part claim, eliminate quotations that support only one part, however apt that part is.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 11 of 27

Before the 1860s, defenders of spontaneous generation—the idea that living things can arise from nonliving matter—argued that boiled broth stayed free of microbes in a sealed flask only because sealing kept out fresh air, which they believed life requires. Louis Pasteur therefore boiled broth in flasks whose necks he had drawn out into long, downward-bending curves, leaving the ends open. Air could reach the broth, but dust settled in the curves, and the broth remained free of microbes for months. Some critics then proposed that the boiling itself had altered the broth so that it could no longer support microbial life of any kind.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the critics’ proposal?

Correct choice: When a curved-neck flask was tipped so that the boiled broth washed into the curve of the neck and back, microbes soon appeared in the broth.

The critics claim the boiled broth can no longer support microbial life. Tipping the flask exposes the same boiled broth to the dust trapped in the neck, and microbes then appear—so the broth could still support microbes, and what it had lacked was contact with dust-borne microbes.

A: This answers the earlier objection about fresh air, but it fits the critics’ new proposal perfectly well: broth that could no longer support life would also stay free of microbes.

B: If longer boiling altered the broth more thoroughly, broth boiled longer would stay sterile longer; this finding is what the critics’ proposal predicts.

D: Unboiled broth has not been altered by boiling, so microbes appearing in it says nothing about whether boiled broth can still support life.

Watch for: When critics shift to a new objection, the finding that weakens it must address that objection, not the one already answered.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 12 of 27

In a 1974 study, psychologists Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed 150 participants a short film of a car accident. Afterward, 50 participants were asked, “About how fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?” Another 50 were asked the same question with “hit” in place of “smashed,” and the remaining 50 were not asked about speed. A week later, all participants were asked whether they had seen any broken glass in the film; there had been none. A student claims that the verb in the speed question changed what participants remembered about the accident itself, not merely the speeds they estimated.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student’s claim?

Correct choice: Participants asked with “smashed” were more than twice as likely to report seeing broken glass as participants in either of the other two groups were.

The claim concerns memory of the accident, so the relevant measure is the report of broken glass, which the film did not show. Sixteen of 50 “smashed” participants reported it, compared with 7 of 50 “hit” participants and 6 of 50 participants not asked about speed—more than twice either other group.

A: The figures are accurate (10.46 is about 31 percent above 8.00), but a higher speed estimate could reflect only how participants answered the speed question, which is the alternative the claim sets aside.

B: This has the right shape but misstates the table: the “hit” group reported broken glass 7 times, only slightly more often than the 6 reports from participants not asked about speed.

C: This is accurate, but it shows only that “hit” did not change reports of broken glass; it says nothing about the effect of “smashed,” which is what the claim concerns.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 13 of 27

The number of farms in the United States reached its peak in the 1930s and then fell steeply as farms grew larger and more mechanized. A student claims that the number of farms per person in the country fell in the first half of the twentieth century—even during the years when the number of farms was still rising.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student’s claim?

Correct choice: The number of farms rose from 5.7 million in 1900 to 6.8 million in 1935, but in the same years the population grew from 76.2 million to 127.3 million, a far larger proportional increase.

The claim concerns the years when farms were still increasing, 1900–1935. Farms rose by about 19 percent (5.7 to 6.8 million) while the population rose by about 67 percent (76.2 to 127.3 million), so farms per person fell even as the number of farms grew.

A: This is accurate, but it concerns 1950–2000, when the number of farms was falling; it says nothing about the years when farms were still increasing, which is the part of the claim that needs support.

B: This is accurate, but it describes only the count of farms; without population figures it cannot show that farms per person fell.

C: This has the right shape but overstates the table: population grew by about 67 percent and farms by about 19 percent, so population grew about 3.5 times as fast, not more than five times.

Watch for: A claim about a rate (“per person”) needs both the count and the base; check each figure a sweeping choice relies on.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 14 of 27

In 1823 the astronomer Heinrich Olbers described a puzzle. Suppose the universe were infinitely large, had existed unchanged forever, and were filled throughout with stars much like the Sun. Then every line of sight from Earth would eventually end at the surface of a star, and the whole night sky would glow as brightly as the surface of the Sun. Dust between the stars cannot remove the difficulty: over endless time, dust absorbing starlight would heat up until it glowed as brightly as the stars themselves. Because the night sky is dark, ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: at least one of the three suppositions about the universe’s size, age, and contents must be false.

The three suppositions together imply a sky as bright as the Sun’s surface, and dust cannot rescue them. Since the sky is dark, the suppositions cannot all be true—but the argument does not show which one fails.

A: A finite universe would be one way out, but the argument shows only that the three suppositions cannot all hold; nothing in it singles out size as the one that fails.

B: A universe of finite age is the explanation astronomers now favor, but the text’s reasoning does not single out age; it shows only that at least one supposition must fail.

D: The text explains that dust cannot remove the difficulty, because over endless time it would heat up and glow as brightly as the stars.

Watch for: When a set of assumptions leads to a false result, logic shows that at least one assumption fails—not which one.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 15 of 27

In sealed-bid auctions for leases to drill for oil in the Gulf of Mexico during the 1950s and 1960s, no company knew exactly how much oil a lease held. Each company estimated a lease’s value from its own surveys and bid accordingly, and the lease went to the highest bidder. In 1971 petroleum engineers Ed Capen, Robert Clapp, and William Campbell reported that winning companies had often earned disappointing returns on their leases, even though, the engineers argued, the companies’ estimates had not been too high on average. The engineers explained this apparent paradox by pointing out that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: the winning bid usually came from the company whose estimate was highest, and the highest of many estimates tends to exceed a lease’s true value.

Even if estimates are right on average, they scatter above and below a lease’s true value. The auction awards each lease to the highest bid, which usually reflects the highest estimate—one of the ones that overshoot. Winners therefore tend to have overestimated, and disappointing returns follow.

B: The text says nothing about which companies had the most accurate surveys or how cautiously they bid, and this would not explain why winners’ estimates tended to be too high.

C: Competition can raise prices, but each company bid according to its own estimate; if estimates were accurate on average, competition alone would not make winners’ estimates too high, which is the puzzle the engineers explained.

D: Nothing in the text compares drilling costs, and the paradox concerns estimates that were accurate on average yet led to disappointment, which drilling costs would not explain.

Watch for: Selecting the highest of many uncertain estimates picks out the ones most likely to be too high, even when the estimates are unbiased.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 16 of 27

In 1774 Antoine Lavoisier heated a piece of tin in a sealed glass vessel until part of the tin had turned into a powdery substance called a calx. As others had found, the calx weighed more than the metal it came from, yet the sealed vessel and its contents weighed the same after heating as before. When Lavoisier broke the seal, air rushed into the vessel, which then weighed more than it had. Many chemists of the time held that a metal turns into a calx by giving off a substance called phlogiston. Lavoisier’s results suggest that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: the tin gained weight by combining with part of the air sealed inside the vessel, and outside air rushed in to replace the air that had been used up.

The calx is heavier than the tin, yet the sealed vessel’s total weight did not change, so the gain was matched by a loss from something else inside. Air rushing in when the seal was broken shows that some of the air inside had been used up. The tin combined with part of the air, and fresh air replaced it.

B: Absorbing a substance from the glass would keep the sealed weight constant, but it would not explain why air rushed in when the seal was broken, which shows that air inside the vessel had been used up.

C: Phlogiston leaving the tin would not use up any of the air in the vessel, so this cannot explain why air rushed in when the seal was broken.

D: The text says part of the tin had turned into calx while the vessel was sealed and heated, before the seal was broken, so the calx did not form only after air rushed in.

Watch for: In a sealed system, an unchanged total weight means any gain in one part is a loss in another; find what was lost.

Benjamin Franklin filled the yearly editions of Poor Richard’s Almanack with short sayings that praised thrift, industry, and plain dealing. “Well done,” ______ “is better than well said.”

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: he wrote in 1737,

The attribution “he wrote in 1737” interrupts a single quoted sentence (“Well done … is better than well said”). An interrupting tag is set off by commas on both sides, so a comma must follow it before the quotation resumes.

A: A semicolon would treat “is better than well said” as a separate independent clause, but it has no subject; it completes the quoted sentence that the tag interrupts.

B: A period would end the sentence and leave “is better than well said” as a fragment beginning with a lowercase verb.

C: Without a comma, the tag runs directly into the resumed quotation; an interrupting tag needs punctuation on both sides.

Correct choice: who

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 21 of 27

When the Swedish warship Vasa was raised from Stockholm harbor in 1961, it ______ on the harbor floor for 333 years, preserved in part because the harbor’s brackish water was inhospitable to the shipworms that devour submerged wood elsewhere.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: had lain

The ship rested on the harbor floor, so the verb is “lie” (to recline), whose past participle is “lain.” The past perfect “had lain” places the resting before the 1961 raising.

A: “Laid” is the past participle of “lay” (to put something down), which takes an object; the ship did not lay anything on the harbor floor.

B: “Lay” is the simple past of “lie,” not its past participle, so it cannot follow “had.”

C: “Lied” is the past participle of “lie” meaning to tell a falsehood, which does not fit a sunken ship.

Watch for: “Lie, lay, lain” (recline) and “lay, laid, laid” (put) overlap at “lay”; after “had,” reclining is “lain.”
