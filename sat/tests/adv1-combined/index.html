Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 1 of 27

The following text is from Jane Austen’s 1817 novel Persuasion. Lady Russell is a friend and adviser of Sir Walter Elliot, a baronet whose family is in financial difficulty.

She was of strict integrity herself, with a delicate sense of honour; but she was as desirous of saving Sir Walter’s feelings, as solicitous for the credit of the family, as aristocratic in her ideas of what was due to them, as anybody of sense and honesty could well be. She was a benevolent, charitable, good woman, and capable of strong attachments, most correct in her conduct, strict in her notions of decorum, and with manners that were held a standard of good-breeding. She had a cultivated mind, and was, generally speaking, rational and consistent—but she had prejudices on the side of ancestry; she had a value for rank and consequence, which blinded her a little to the faults of those who possessed them.

As used in the text, what does the word “consequence” most nearly mean?

Correct choice: standing

“Consequence” is paired with “rank” as something people can possess and that Lady Russell values because of her “prejudices on the side of ancestry.” In this context it means social importance, or standing.

A: This is the common modern sense (a result), but a person cannot possess an outcome in the way the text says people possess rank and consequence.

B: Seriousness is a sense related to importance (a matter “of consequence”), but it describes the gravity of an issue, not a person’s social position.

C: The text calls Lady Russell “rational and consistent,” but “consequence” names something she values in others, not reasoning.

Watch for: Words paired in a phrase (“rank and consequence”) often share a meaning; let the partner word guide you.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 2 of 27

The following text is from Thomas Hardy’s 1878 novel The Return of the Native. The narrator describes Egdon Heath, a large expanse of dark, uncultivated land, at dusk beneath a pale, overcast sky.

In such contrast the heath wore the appearance of an instalment of night which had taken up its place before its astronomical hour was come: darkness had to a great extent arrived hereon, while day stood distinct in the sky. Looking upwards, a furze-cutter would have been inclined to continue work; looking down, he would have decided to finish his faggot and go home. The distant rims of the world and of the firmament seemed to be a division in time no less than a division in matter. The face of the heath by its mere complexion added half an hour to evening; it could in like manner retard the dawn, sadden noon, anticipate the frowning of storms scarcely generated, and intensify the opacity of a moonless midnight to a cause of shaking and dread.

As used in the text, what does the word “complexion” most nearly mean?

Correct choice: coloring

The text contrasts the dark heath with the pale sky: darkness has arrived on the land while day remains in the sky, so a worker looking down would think it later than a worker looking up. The heath adds “half an hour to evening” by its “mere complexion,” that is, simply by its dark coloring.

B: Because the heath is personified (it can “sadden noon”), temperament is tempting, and it is an older sense of complexion. But the effect described is visual: the heath looks darker than the sky and so makes the evening seem to arrive earlier.

C: The text mentions “the distant rims of the world and of the firmament,” but that boundary is not what makes the land seem darker than the hour; the effect comes from the heath’s dark color.

D: The text says nothing about how the heath is regarded by others; its effect comes from its appearance.

Watch for: When a familiar word appears in older prose, test the modern sense and the older senses against what the sentence says the thing actually does.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 3 of 27

The story of six-year-old George Washington confessing that he had damaged his father’s cherry tree first appeared in 1806, in the fifth edition of a biography by Mason Locke Weems, more than six years after Washington’s death. Weems attributed the anecdote to an unnamed elderly woman who had known the family when she was a girl, and no earlier account of the episode has ever been found. For these reasons, most historians regard the story as ______, even though it remains one of the best-known episodes of Washington’s childhood.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct choice: apocryphal

The reasons given all concern where the story came from: it surfaced years after Washington’s death, rests on an unnamed source, and has no earlier record, yet it is widely retold. A story of doubtful authenticity that nonetheless circulates widely is apocryphal.

A: Specious describes something, usually an argument, that seems plausible but is actually false or misleading; the text’s evidence concerns the story’s uncertain origin, not a deceptive plausibility.

B: Anachronistic means placed in the wrong period, but nothing suggests the story is set in the wrong time; the problem is that it first appeared long after the events it describes.

C: Tendentious means written to promote a particular point of view; the text says nothing about Weems’s purpose, only about the lack of any reliable source for the story.

Watch for: Match the word to the reason the text gives: evidence about a story’s source points to doubtful authenticity, not to bias or misleading reasoning.

Defenders of deliberate settlement cited oral traditions of planned voyages, but skeptics replied that such traditions might have been composed long after the events they describe. In 1976 the Hōkūleʻa, a voyaging canoe modeled on traditional designs, sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti with navigator Mau Piailug, who used no instruments. The voyage could not show that ancient navigators had made the same trip, but it undercut a premise of the drift view: that such navigation was impossible.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Correct choice: To explain how a voyage bore on a scholarly dispute about Polynesian settlement without settling it

The text sets out a dispute (accidental drift versus deliberate settlement), notes why one kind of evidence for deliberate settlement was contested, and then presents the 1976 voyage. Its final sentence states both what the voyage could not show and what it did accomplish: it undercut the premise that such navigation was impossible. The purpose is to explain the voyage’s bearing on the dispute without claiming that it resolved it.

The following text is Paul Laurence Dunbar’s poem “The Poet,” from his 1903 collection Lyrics of Love and Laughter.

He sang of life, serenely sweet,
With, now and then, a deeper note.
From some high peak, nigh yet remote,
He voiced the world's absorbing beat.

He sang of love when earth was young,

Underlined: But ah, the world, it turned to praise
A jingle in a broken tongue. End underlined.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined portion in the text as a whole?

Correct choice: It sets the poet’s ambitious work against the slighter verse that the world chose to praise.

The first six lines describe the poet’s elevated subjects: life, love, and “the world's absorbing beat,” voiced “from some high peak.” The underlined lines turn with “But ah”: the world praised instead “a jingle in a broken tongue,” a dismissive phrase for slighter verse. The lines therefore set the poet’s serious work against what the public chose to admire.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 6 of 27

The following text is from Virginia Woolf’s 1922 novel Jacob’s Room. The narrator is considering the character of Jacob Flanders, a young man.

It is no use trying to sum people up. One must follow hints, not exactly what is said, nor yet entirely what is done. Some, it is true, take ineffaceable impressions of character at once. Others dally, loiter, and get blown this way and that. Kind old ladies assure us that cats are often the best judges of character. A cat will always go to a good man, they say; Underlined: but then, Mrs. Whitehorn, Jacob's landlady, loathed cats. End underlined.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined portion in the text as a whole?

Correct choice: It humorously denies the reader any verdict from the folk method just described, in keeping with the narrator’s opening claim.

The narrator begins by saying that “it is no use trying to sum people up,” then reports a folk method for doing so: cats go to good men. The underlined portion deflates that method at once. Jacob’s landlady loathed cats, so no cat’s verdict on Jacob is available. The comic dead end supports the opening claim that character cannot easily be summed up.

B: The landlady’s dislike is of cats, not a judgment of Jacob; the text reports no opinion of hers about his character.

C: The text never says that cats avoided Jacob. Their absence results from the landlady’s dislike of cats, not from anything about Jacob.

D: The narrator reports the test with gentle irony (“Kind old ladies assure us”) after declaring that “it is no use trying to sum people up,” so the text does not endorse the test as sound.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 7 of 27

Text 1

In 2018 a team led by Dirk Hoffmann dated thin mineral crusts that had formed over red paintings in three Spanish caves, using the slow radioactive decay of uranium in the crusts. Because a crust can form only after the paint beneath it, the dates, more than 64,000 years ago, set minimum ages for the paintings. The team concluded that Neanderthals must have made them, since modern humans had not yet reached Europe.

Text 2

Some archaeologists have questioned the dating. Water flowing over a cave wall can dissolve and carry away uranium from a crust after the crust has formed, and the dating method can mistake uranium lost in this way for uranium that has decayed.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the conclusion in Text 1?

Correct choice: By suggesting that the crusts may be younger than their dates indicate, so that the dates may not show the paintings to be old enough to rule out modern humans

If uranium lost from a crust is mistaken for uranium that has decayed, the method will overestimate how much time has passed, making a crust seem older than it is. Text 2’s author would therefore say the minimum ages might be too old, so the dates may not place the paintings before modern humans arrived.

A: Text 2 does not question the order of paint and crust, which Text 1 explains (a crust can form only over paint already there); it questions the dates themselves.

B: Text 2 says only that the method can overestimate ages; it does not give any new age for the crusts or show that Neanderthals could not have made the paintings.

D: Text 2 questions whether the dates are reliable, so its author would not accept them as reliable minimum ages; it says nothing about when modern humans arrived.

Watch for: Text 2 never says “too old”; work out what mistaking lost uranium for decayed uranium would do to the calculated age.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 8 of 27

The following text is from Zitkála-Šá’s 1921 book American Indian Stories. The narrator, a Dakota girl, describes her first breakfast at a boarding school far from her home.

A small bell was tapped, and each of the pupils drew a chair from under the table. Supposing this act meant they were to be seated, I pulled out mine and at once slipped into it from one side. But when I turned my head, I saw that I was the only one seated, and all the rest at our table remained standing. Just as I began to rise, looking shyly around to see how chairs were to be used, a second bell was sounded. All were seated at last, and I had to crawl back into my chair again. Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct choice: Unfamiliar signals repeatedly leave the narrator out of step with the others, until she is afraid to act.

Each signal catches the narrator out. She sits when the others stand, begins to rise just as the second bell seats everyone, and has to crawl back into her chair. When the others finally begin eating, she cries “for by this time I was afraid to venture anything more.” The text is about unexplained signals leaving her repeatedly out of step until she is afraid to act.

A: No punishment is described; the narrator’s fear comes from repeatedly misreading the signals.

C: She looks around “shyly,” but the text is about misreading the bells, not about being unable to ask for help.

D: She does watch the others, but the passage ends with her too afraid to act, not with her mastering the routine.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 9 of 27

In parts of Africa, greater honeyguides, birds that eat beeswax, lead people to wild bees’ nests; the people open the nests and take the honey, and the birds feed on the wax left behind. Yao honey hunters in northern Mozambique summon honeyguides with a distinctive call, a loud trill followed by a grunt. In 2016, zoologist Claire Spottiswoode and her colleagues reported evidence that the birds treat this particular call as a sign that a person is ready to follow them, rather than responding simply to any person making noise.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers’ conclusion?

Correct choice: Recordings of the trill-and-grunt call produced guiding far more often than recordings of spoken words or of another bird’s call.

The conclusion is specific: birds respond to this call, not merely to any human noise. The finding that supports it must compare the call with other sounds. Choice D does this, using recordings of spoken words and of another species’ call as controls. The trill-and-grunt call produced far more guiding.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 10 of 27

When touched or jolted, the leaves of the sensitive plant Mimosa pudica fold shut, a response thought to discourage plant-eating animals. In a 2014 study, ecologist Monica Gagliano and colleagues repeatedly dropped potted Mimosa plants a short distance onto a cushioned surface. At first the leaves folded after each drop, but after several drops many plants stopped folding in response. The researchers concluded that the plants had learned to ignore a harmless disturbance rather than simply having exhausted the energy needed to keep folding.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers’ conclusion?

Correct choice: Plants that had stopped folding after repeated drops folded their leaves fully when they were then shaken, a disturbance they had not experienced before.

The rival explanation is exhaustion: a plant that had used up the energy needed to fold could not fold in response to anything. If plants that ignored drops still folded fully when shaken, they clearly still had that energy; they had stopped responding specifically to the harmless drops, as the researchers concluded.

B: Folding again after a long rest is what exhaustion would predict, since an exhausted plant would recover its energy over time; this finding does not favor learning.

C: Plants with less energy giving up sooner fits the exhaustion explanation at least as well as the learning explanation, so it cannot show that the plants learned rather than tired.

D: How quickly plants fold after drops of different heights says nothing about why the plants eventually stopped responding to repeated drops.

Watch for: To support one explanation over a rival, look for a finding the rival cannot explain; a new disturbance that still triggers folding shows that the plants were still able to fold.

The Southwark and Vauxhall Company drew its water from a stretch of the Thames polluted by sewage; the Lambeth Company had moved its intake upstream. A student claims that, relative to the number of houses each company supplied, Southwark and Vauxhall households suffered cholera deaths at more than eight times the rate of Lambeth households.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student’s claim?

Correct choice: Southwark and Vauxhall households had about 315 cholera deaths per 10,000 houses, compared with about 38 per 10,000 houses for Lambeth households.

The claim compares rates, so deaths must be divided by houses: 1,263 ÷ 40,046 ≈ 315 per 10,000 houses for Southwark and Vauxhall and 98 ÷ 26,107 ≈ 38 per 10,000 for Lambeth. Since 315 is about 8.4 times 38, this choice supports the claim.

A: These counts are accurate, but the companies supplied different numbers of houses, so a ratio of deaths is not a ratio of rates; the claim is about rates.

C: These rates are accurate (1,422 ÷ 256,423 ≈ 55 per 10,000), but they compare Southwark and Vauxhall with the rest of London rather than with Lambeth.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 13 of 27

Clark’s nutcrackers bury thousands of seeds in autumn and dig many of them up months later. In a 1982 experiment, biologist Stephen Vander Wall let captive nutcrackers bury seeds in the sand floor of an aviary scattered with rocks and logs. After the birds had cached their seeds, he shifted every object on one side of the aviary about 20 centimeters in the same direction, leaving the other side undisturbed. When the birds later searched, their probes on the undisturbed side landed on or near their caches, while their probes on the altered side missed by roughly 20 centimeters, in the direction the objects had been moved. These results suggest that the nutcrackers ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: locate each cache by its position relative to objects near it, rather than by the arrangement of objects across the aviary as a whole.

The misses on the altered side matched the shift in the objects, so the birds were guided by objects, not by the seeds themselves. And because searches on the undisturbed side stayed accurate even though half of the aviary’s objects had moved, the birds must have used the objects near each cache rather than the layout as a whole.

B: If the birds relied on the arrangement of all the objects, moving half of them would have disturbed searches on both sides; instead, searches on the undisturbed side stayed accurate.

C: If the birds found seeds by scent, their probes on the altered side would still have landed on the caches; instead they missed by the distance the objects had moved.

D: If the birds measured from the entrance, which did not move, shifting the objects would not have displaced their probes on the altered side.

Watch for: Use both halves of the experiment: the altered side shows that objects guide the search, and the undisturbed side shows which objects.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 14 of 27

The following text is from Stephen Crane’s 1895 novel The Red Badge of Courage. The youth, a young soldier, has fled from his first battle and is walking alone through a forest.

He threw a pine cone at a jovial squirrel, and he ran with chattering fear. High in a treetop he stopped, and, poking his head cautiously from behind a branch, looked down with an air of trepidation.

The youth felt triumphant at this exhibition. There was the law, he said. Nature had given him a sign. The squirrel, immediately upon recognizing danger, had taken to his legs without ado. He did not stand stolidly baring his furry belly to the missile, and die with an upward glance at the sympathetic heavens. On the contrary, he had fled as fast as his legs could carry him; and he was but an ordinary squirrel, too—doubtless no philosopher of his race. The youth wended, feeling that Nature was of his mind. She re-enforced his argument with proofs that lived where the sun shone.

Based on the text, what can most reasonably be inferred about the youth’s conclusion that “Nature had given him a sign”?

Correct choice: It turns an ordinary animal’s reaction to a threat the youth himself created into a law that excuses his own flight.

The youth provokes the squirrel’s flight himself by throwing a pine cone, then treats that reaction as “the law” and “a sign” that fleeing danger is natural, which excuses his own flight from battle. The narrator’s phrasing is ironic: the youth feels “triumphant,” and Nature “re-enforced his argument,” implying that he wanted a conclusion and found one. The inference is that the conclusion is a self-serving generalization from a trivial event he caused.

A: The narrator frames the conclusion ironically, as a “triumphant” discovery that “re-enforced his argument,” not as genuine self-recognition about his own flight.

D: The excerpt ends with the youth still “feeling that Nature was of his mind”; he does not abandon the conclusion.

Watch for: In ironic narration, separate what a character concludes from how the narrator frames that conclusion.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 16 of 27

Many species of Hawaiian honeycreeper, a group of birds descended from a single finch-like ancestor, have already disappeared. Several surviving ______ are threatened by avian malaria, a disease carried by mosquitoes that people introduced to the islands.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: species, moreover,

“Moreover” interrupts a single independent clause, “Several surviving species … are threatened by avian malaria.” An interrupting word within a clause is set off by a pair of commas.

A: A semicolon would separate “Several surviving species” from its verb, leaving a fragment before it.

B: Without a closing comma, “moreover” is not fully set off from the rest of the clause.

C: Without an opening comma, “moreover” is not fully set off from the subject.

Watch for: A conjunctive adverb takes a semicolon only when it begins a new independent clause; inside one clause, set it off with two commas.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 18 of 27

Of the more than 600 instruments by Antonio Stradivari that survive today, the ______ in 1716 is widely considered the best preserved, having rarely been played in the three centuries since.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: violin that he completed

“That he completed in 1716” is a restrictive clause: it identifies which of Stradivari’s many violins is meant. A clause introduced by “that” is always essential and is never set off with commas, and no punctuation should separate the subject from its verb, “is.”

A: A single comma after “completed” would split the clause (“that he completed … in 1716”) and wrongly separate part of the subject from the rest.

C: Paired commas would treat the “that” clause as nonessential, but a clause introduced by “that” cannot be set off with commas.

D: A comma before “that” wrongly separates “the violin” from the clause that identifies it, and the phrase is left half enclosed.

Watch for: A clause beginning with “that” is always essential; do not set it off with commas.

Lyrical Ballads, the 1798 collection that opened with Samuel Taylor Coleridge’s “The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere” and closed with William Wordsworth’s “Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey,” ______ published anonymously; neither poet’s name appeared on its title page.

In an 1859 essay, physicist James Clerk Maxwell showed mathematically that a solid ring of that size would be torn apart by the forces acting on it, and that a liquid ring would break up as well. ______, he argued, the rings must consist of countless separate particles, each orbiting the planet on its own.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 23 of 27

After decades of work, scholars of ancient Maya writing can now read the great majority of surviving inscriptions. ______, some signs remain undeciphered, and the readings of others are still debated. Those uncertainties, however, usually affect individual words rather than the overall meaning of a text.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?
