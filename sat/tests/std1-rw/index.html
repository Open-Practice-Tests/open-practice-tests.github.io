Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 1 of 27

Every twenty years, the main buildings of the Ise Grand Shrine in Japan are taken down and rebuilt on an adjacent site using the same traditional methods. Because each reconstruction requires younger carpenters to learn techniques from workers who took part in the previous one, the practice ______ building skills that might otherwise disappear during the long intervals between major projects.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct choice: perpetuates

The text says that each rebuilding requires younger carpenters to learn from workers who took part in the previous one, so the techniques are handed on rather than lost. Perpetuates, meaning causes to continue, describes this effect precisely.

A: To codify is to set down in a systematic written form, but the text describes skills passed on by working alongside experienced carpenters, not written rules.

B: The rebuilding may honor tradition, but “commemorates building skills that might otherwise disappear” misses the point that the practice keeps those skills in use.

D: The text stresses that the same traditional methods are used each time, not that the skills are made easier.

Watch for: Predict a word from the clause that follows the blank (skills that “might otherwise disappear”) before reading the choices.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 2 of 27

The following text is from E. M. Forster’s 1910 novel Howards End. Margaret Schlegel is thinking about the Wilcoxes, a family of brisk, practical businesspeople whose way of life differs from that of Margaret and her younger siblings, Helen and Tibby.

To Margaret this life was to remain a real force. She could not despise it, as Helen and Tibby Underlined: affected End underlined. to do. It fostered such virtues as neatness, decision, and obedience, virtues of the second rank, no doubt, but they have formed our civilisation. They form character, too; Margaret could not doubt it; they keep the soul from becoming sloppy.

As used in the text, what does the word “affected” most nearly mean?

Correct choice: feigned

In an older sense, to affect to do something is to make a show of doing it. Helen and Tibby profess to despise the Wilcoxes’ way of life, and the word implies that their contempt is partly a pose, whereas Margaret cannot share it at all. Feigned captures this meaning.

A: Resolved would mean that Helen and Tibby had firmly decided to despise the Wilcoxes’ life; affect does not carry this meaning, and the word suggests a show of contempt rather than a determination.

C: Influenced is the most familiar meaning of affect, but it does not fit the sentence: Helen and Tibby are not influencing anyone; they are the ones who display contempt.

D: Declined would mean that Helen and Tibby refused to despise the Wilcoxes’ life, which erases the contrast the sentence draws between them and Margaret.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 3 of 27

The following text is Edna St. Vincent Millay’s poem “Kin to Sorrow,” from her 1917 collection Renascence and Other Poems.

Am I kin to Sorrow,
That so oft
Falls the knocker of my door—
Neither loud nor soft,
But as long accustomed,
Under Sorrow's hand?
Marigolds around the step
And rosemary stand,
And then comes Sorrow—
And what does Sorrow care
For the rosemary
Or the marigolds there?
Am I kin to Sorrow?
Are we kin?
That so oft upon my door—
Underlined: Oh, come in! End underlined.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined portion in the text as a whole?

Correct choice: It answers her repeated question indirectly, as she greets Sorrow the way one greets a frequent visitor.

The speaker asks whether she is “kin” to Sorrow because Sorrow knocks so often and “as long accustomed,” and she notes that the flowers at her step do not keep Sorrow away. The final line breaks off her repeated question with a greeting: she simply invites Sorrow in. This tacitly concedes the familiarity she has been questioning and treats Sorrow as a regular visitor rather than giving a direct answer.

A: The invitation lets Sorrow in; the flowers that might have kept Sorrow out are said to be ignored.

B: The poem identifies the knocking with Sorrow throughout and gives no sign of another visitor.

C: The greeting implies the opposite: the speaker treats Sorrow as familiar, as kin might be treated.

Watch for: An abrupt final line can answer a poem’s question by action rather than by statement.

Text 1

New Zealand’s moa, flightless birds that included species taller than a person, disappeared within about two centuries after Polynesians settled the islands around 1300 CE. Some researchers have suggested that hunting merely finished off moa populations that were already shrinking before people arrived, perhaps because of a changing climate or volcanic eruptions.

Text 2

Geneticist Morten Allentoft and colleagues analyzed ancient DNA from hundreds of moa bones dated to the four thousand years before the birds’ extinction. Populations that shrink over many generations tend to lose genetic diversity, yet the team found no such loss: the genetic diversity of all four moa species they studied remained stable until the birds vanished.

Based on the texts, how would Allentoft and colleagues (Text 2) most likely respond to the suggestion described in Text 1?

Correct choice: They would argue that their findings give little support to the idea of a long decline before people arrived, since moa populations showed no sign of shrinking.

Text 1’s suggestion requires populations that were shrinking before people arrived. Text 2 notes that shrinking populations tend to lose genetic diversity and reports that the moa’s diversity stayed stable until extinction, so its authors would say the evidence gives little support to a pre-human decline.

B: Text 2’s finding of stable diversity argues against any decline before people arrived, whatever its cause, and the text mentions no earlier visitors.

Text 1

In 2003 researchers excavating Liang Bua cave on the Indonesian island of Flores found the skeleton of an adult hominin, known as LB1, that lived roughly 60,000 to 100,000 years ago, stood only about a meter tall and had a brain roughly a third the size of a modern human’s. Its discoverers assigned it to a new species, Homo floresiensis. Some researchers objected that LB1 was more likely a modern human with a disorder that stunts growth, an argument that rested partly on the fact that LB1 was the only skull ever found.

Text 2

In 2016 a team working at Mata Menge, about 70 kilometers from Liang Bua, reported a jaw fragment and teeth roughly 700,000 years old. The fossils resemble those of LB1 and come from individuals as small as LB1 or even smaller.

Based on the texts, how would the researchers who studied the Mata Menge fossils (Text 2) most likely respond to the objection described in Text 1?

Correct choice: They would see the objection as weakened, since small, LB1-like hominins living hundreds of thousands of years earlier are hard to explain as one person’s disorder.

The objection depends partly on LB1 being a lone individual, which makes a personal disorder plausible. Text 2 reports similar small hominins on Flores some 700,000 years ago, which suggests a long-lived population of small people rather than one unusual person, so the Mata Menge team would see the objection as weakened.

B: The Mata Menge fossils belong to other individuals, so they cannot show directly whether LB1 itself had a disorder; they weaken the objection rather than refute it.

C: Small size shared across individuals separated by hundreds of thousands of years points to a population trait, not a disorder that happened to recur; the Mata Menge team would not take it as support for the objection.

D: Text 1’s objection concerns whether LB1 was a modern human with a disorder, not its age, and Text 2 dates the Mata Menge fossils, not LB1.

Text 1

Rocks on Racetrack Playa, a dry lakebed in California’s Death Valley, leave long trails in its surface, but for decades no one saw them move. Some researchers proposed that ice carried the rocks, yet ice thick enough to hold heavy rocks rarely forms in this desert. A more plausible explanation, other researchers argued, is that strong gusts pushed the rocks directly across mud made slick by rare rains.

During the winter of 2013–2014, a research team using GPS-equipped rocks, cameras, and a weather station recorded the rocks moving. After rain left a shallow pond on the lakebed, ice only a few millimeters thick formed overnight. As the sheet melted and broke into large floating panels, light winds drove the panels against the rocks, shoving them across the soft ground beneath the water.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the reasoning presented in Text 1?

Correct choice: By pointing out that Text 1 dismisses ice on the assumption that it would have to hold the rocks, whereas thin ice driven by wind can push them

Text 1 rejects an ice explanation because ice thick enough to hold heavy rocks rarely forms, and it favors strong gusts acting directly on the rocks. Text 2 reports that ice only a few millimeters thick, broken into panels and driven by light winds, shoved the rocks. The author of Text 2 would say that Text 1’s objection to ice assumes the wrong role for it: the ice did not have to hold the rocks, only push them.

A: Text 2 does not describe wind sliding the rocks directly. Light winds moved ice panels, and the panels shoved the rocks.

B: Text 2 gives wind an essential role: it “drove the panels against the rocks.”

D: Text 2 does not accept Text 1’s rejection of ice; its observations make ice central to the motion.

Watch for: When a second text partly agrees with the first (here, wind matters), find the exact point where it departs (the role of ice).

The following text is from Henry Adams’s 1918 autobiography The Education of Henry Adams. Adams attended Harvard College in the 1850s.

Harvard College, as far as it educated at all, was a mild and liberal school, which sent young men into the world with all they needed to make respectable citizens, and something of what they wanted to make useful ones. Leaders of men it never tried to make. [...] In effect, the school created a type but not a will. Four years of Harvard College, if successful, resulted in an autobiographical blank, a mind on which only a water-mark had been stamped. The stamp, as such things went, was a good one. [...] It taught little, and that little ill, but it left the mind open, free from bias, ignorant of facts, but docile. The graduate had few strong prejudices. He knew little, but his mind remained supple, ready to receive knowledge.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct choice: Adams judges that Harvard gave its students a respectable character and receptive minds but little knowledge and no strong sense of purpose.

Adams says Harvard made “respectable citizens” of a recognizable “type” but “not a will,” and that it “taught little, and that little ill”; yet it left minds “open,” “free from bias,” and “ready to receive knowledge,” and its stamp “was a good one.” His judgment is mixed: a sound but thin education.

A: Adams does credit Harvard with open minds, but he says “Leaders of men it never tried to make,” so he does not claim it prepared graduates to lead.

C: Adams does credit Harvard with forming character, but he says the graduate’s mind “remained supple, ready to receive knowledge,” so the teaching did not reduce students’ ability to learn.

D: Adams presents the lack of strong prejudices as a merit (“free from bias”), and he calls Harvard’s stamp “a good one,” so he does not treat its moderation as its chief failing.

The claim compares two ratios. For Saturn and Jupiter, the distance ratio is 9.54 ÷ 5.20, or about 1.8, while the period ratio is 29.46 ÷ 11.86, or about 2.5. The period ratio is larger than the distance ratio, as the claim requires.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 13 of 27

In 1676 astronomer Ole Rømer studied eclipses of Io, a moon of Jupiter that regularly passes into the planet’s shadow. Predicting eclipses from the average interval between them, Rømer found that the eclipses were seen later than predicted when Earth, in its orbit, was far from Jupiter and earlier than predicted when Earth was near Jupiter, although nothing in Io’s own motion accounted for the difference. He reasoned that light from Io needed time to cross the distance to Earth. Rømer’s reasoning implies that for predictions to match every observed eclipse, they would have to ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: include, for each eclipse, the time light takes to cross the distance between Jupiter and Earth.

On Rømer’s account, each eclipse is seen when its light arrives, which depends on how far that light must travel. Because the Earth–Jupiter distance changes through the year, an accurate prediction must add, for each eclipse, the travel time across the distance at that moment.

A: Light’s constant speed does not produce a constant delay: the distance changes, so the delay changes with Earth’s position. A single delay cannot match both the late and the early eclipses.

C: The text says nothing in Io’s own motion accounted for the difference, so treating its intervals as irregular contradicts Rømer’s explanation.

D: Using only near-Jupiter observations would ignore the late eclipses rather than predict them; the claim concerns every observed eclipse.

Watch for: A constant speed does not imply a constant delay when the distance varies; check which quantity actually changes.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 14 of 27

Most planetary scientists think that the Moon formed from debris thrown out when a body about the size of Mars struck the young Earth. Computer simulations of such a collision typically predict that most of the Moon’s material came from the impacting body rather than from Earth. Yet rocks brought back by the Apollo missions have almost exactly the same proportions of oxygen isotopes as rocks on Earth, whereas meteorites from Mars and most meteorites from the asteroid belt have proportions measurably different from Earth’s. If the impact explanation is correct, the Apollo results suggest that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: the impacting body resembled Earth in composition, or the collision mixed the two bodies’ material more thoroughly than simulations suggest.

Because other bodies in the solar system differ from Earth in their isotope proportions, a Moon made mostly of impactor material should differ from Earth too. It does not. Keeping the impact explanation therefore requires either an impactor that happened to resemble Earth or far more mixing of material than the typical simulations assume.

A: Nothing in the text suggests that the Apollo samples came from an unusual region; this proposes an untested sampling problem instead of drawing a conclusion from the evidence and the impact explanation.

B: The Apollo results are surprising precisely because a Moon made mostly of impactor material would be expected to differ from Earth; the results do not suggest that the simulations are right.

C: Nothing in the text suggests the samples were contaminated, and such a claim would dismiss the results rather than draw a conclusion from them.

Watch for: When a completion begins “If the explanation is correct,” keep the explanation and ask what else must be true for it to fit the new evidence.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 16 of 27

Although the coelacanth was known to scientists only from fossils more than 66 million years old, ______ living specimen was caught off the coast of South Africa in 1938.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct choice: a

The sentence begins with the subordinate clause Although the coelacanth was known …, which must be joined to a main clause: a living specimen was caught …. Because although already expresses the contrast, no second conjunction belongs before the main clause.

B: But doubles the connection already made by although: the clauses may be joined by a subordinating conjunction or by a coordinating conjunction, but not by both.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 21 of 27

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 22 of 27

On islands, mammals often evolve body sizes very different from those of their mainland relatives. Large species, which may face limited food and few predators, tend to become smaller: on Sicily and Malta, extinct elephants evolved to stand only about a meter tall at the shoulder. ______ small species on islands tend to become larger, perhaps because the absence of predators removes the advantage of a small, easily hidden body; the same islands were once home to a dormouse far larger than its mainland relatives.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Correct choice: Conversely,

The sentence after the blank describes the reverse of the pattern just given: large species shrink on islands, while small species grow. Conversely signals that reversal.

A: Similarly would present the small species’ change as like the large species’ change, but the two changes run in opposite directions.

B: Thus would present the growth of small species as a result of the shrinking of large ones, a causal link the text does not make.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 23 of 27

In the third century BCE, the scholar Eratosthenes learned that at noon on the summer solstice, the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene, in southern Egypt, while at Alexandria, to the north, a vertical rod cast a shadow at an angle of about one-fiftieth of a full circle. Eratosthenes assumed that the Sun is so far away that its rays reach Earth along parallel lines. ______ he reasoned, the difference in the angle of sunlight at the two cities had to be caused by the curvature of Earth’s surface between them, so Earth’s circumference must be about fifty times the distance separating the cities.

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct choice: mutations that had arisen in that population by about generation 20,000, though they did not themselves allow citrate use, made that ability possible later.

Every restarted culture had the same conditions and time, yet only those begun from later samples produced citrate users. Something in the population’s history by about generation 20,000 must have made the ability possible—earlier mutations that set the stage without themselves allowing citrate use, since no earlier sample could use citrate.

A: The result concerns the history of one population; nothing suggests that every population reaches the same point after 20,000 generations, and the other eleven had not evolved the ability.

B: The restarted cultures began from single cells, and the text says samples taken before generation 30,000 contained no bacteria that could use citrate, so citrate use had not already arisen and gone undetected; it evolved again in the new cultures.

C: A chance event unrelated to the population’s earlier history could have happened in cultures started from any generation, yet citrate users arose only from cells taken after about generation 20,000.

Watch for: When an outcome can be repeated only from later starting points, look for something that changed in the population’s history, not in the conditions or the outcome itself.

Correct choice: deduce

A: To deduce would follow verbs such as ask or encourage (“encourage his students to deduce”), but after active make the infinitive drops to.

C: Deducing is a participle; “make his students deducing” is not a standard construction.

The last sentence describes the other group in the comparison—people exposed early rather than late—and their different outcome. On the other hand introduces this contrasting case.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 24 of 27

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Correct choice: In turn,

The ancient seed produced a tree, and that tree then supplied the pollen that let another ancient palm bear fruit. In turn marks this next step, in which the result of one step becomes the means of the next.

A: Meanwhile signals that two things happened at the same time, but the pollination happened “later,” after Methuselah had grown into a tree.

B: For instance introduces an example of a general point, but the pollination is a later step in Methuselah’s story, not an example of anything stated before it.

C: In short introduces a summary, but the final sentence reports a new event rather than summarizing the previous ones.

Watch for: In turn fits when each step makes the next possible: seed, tree, pollen, fruit.
