Review and reference

Identify what a passage establishes, then preserve its scope when you choose an answer.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish a topic, a central idea, and a supporting detail.
  • Summarize the relationship between a passage's major parts without adding a claim.
  • Track qualifiers, comparisons, and references in detail questions.
  • Explain why a plausible alternative is too narrow, too broad, or inconsistent with the text.

1.1 From topic to central idea

A topic names the subject: community gardens, a musician, or an experiment. A central idea states what the passage establishes about that subject. 'Community gardens' is a label; 'Community gardens can serve social needs as well as provide food' is a claim. Your first draft need not sound polished. Write or think: This passage says that... Then finish the sentence with a relationship, finding, or observation.

Separate three levels: the subject, the passage's overall claim, and the examples supporting that claim. A central idea does not have to mention every example. It must explain why the important information belongs together. Do not choose an answer merely because all its words appear in the passage. An accurate supporting fact may cover only a fraction of the text.

W1.1: A use beyond the obvious

The ovens in Mara's neighborhood bakery stopped producing bread at noon, but the shop remained busy. Older residents came for help reading municipal notices. Children used the long table to finish homework while their parents returned from work. Mara had never advertised these services. Over time, a place designed to sell bread had become a place where neighbors could depend on one another.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A. Mara's bakery developed an important community role beyond selling bread.

B. Mara extended baking hours to meet the needs of neighborhood families.

C. Neighborhood residents preferred Mara's assistance to municipal services.

D. Children's homework sessions became the bakery's principal activity.

Practice this lesson: 1.01, 1.05.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.1

Answer: A

  1. Name the topic: the bakery's place in its neighborhood.
  2. Group the notice-reading and homework details as examples of assistance and connection.
  3. State the shared claim: the bakery acquired a community role beyond its commercial use. A captures that claim.

Why B fails: The ovens stopped at noon; the passage does not say baking hours increased.

Why C fails: Residents receive help with notices, but no preference between providers is established.

Why D fails: Homework is one example. Nothing identifies it as the principal activity.

Key takeaway: A central statement often names the common meaning of several different examples.

1.2 Map what the passage adds or changes

Long sentences become more manageable when you track their contributions. Compress a short passage into two or three notes: earlier belief → new observation → qualified conclusion, or general claim → two examples. These are flexible reading aids, not formulas every passage must follow. Notice whether a later sentence supports, limits, revises, or explains an earlier one.

A contrast word tells you to inspect a relationship; it does not announce that everything before it is false. Likewise, a final sentence may supply an example rather than the central idea. Build your summary from the whole passage. Central-idea questions ask what the text establishes. Questions asking why an author includes a particular sentence test function, usually under Craft and Structure; do not substitute that task here.

W1.2: A revision with a limit

In a hypothetical restoration project, managers expected newly planted dune grass to spread most rapidly where fertilizer was added. After a year, fertilized plots had taller stems but no more ground coverage than untreated plots. The managers therefore kept fertilizer for sites needing taller wind barriers while dropping it from sites where their priority was covering bare sand.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A. Fertilizer proved ineffective at changing the growth of dune grass.

B. Managers found that untreated grass provided the most effective wind barriers.

C. Managers adjusted fertilizer use because it benefited one restoration goal but not another.

D. Ground coverage became more important to managers than protecting sites from wind.

Practice this lesson: 1.02, 1.13.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.2

Answer: C

  1. Map the passage: expected faster spread → taller stems without additional coverage → different uses by goal.
  2. Keep both findings. Calling fertilizer simply useful or useless loses the distinction.
  3. Choose C, which connects the mixed result to the managers' decision.

Why A fails: Taller stems show a growth difference associated with fertilizer, even though fertilized plots had no extra ground coverage relative to untreated plots.

Why B fails: The passage does not report that untreated grass provided better barriers.

Why D fails: Different sites have different goals; the text does not rank those goals overall.

Key takeaway: When evidence changes a decision, summarize both the evidence's distinction and its consequence.

1.3 Preserve scope and qualification

Every claim has boundaries. Track who or what it concerns, where and when it applies, what is compared, and how strongly it is stated. 'Some first-time visitors returned more often' does not mean 'all visitors became more satisfied.' The second claim changes the population, the measured outcome, and the strength of the finding.

Treat an answer as a set of claims joined together. Check each part. An attractive first half cannot rescue an unsupported second half. Words such as only, all, more, and primarily deserve attention

because they specify scope, but they are not automatically wrong. A strong claim is correct when the text supports it. A vague claim can be wrong if it omits the passage's defining distinction.

W1.3: Which outcome improved?

A hypothetical survey compared two versions of a transit map. Among residents unfamiliar with the bus network, those viewing a simplified map identified direct routes more accurately. They did not identify routes requiring a transfer more accurately. Residents who already knew the network performed similarly with either map. The designers retained the simplified map for introductory materials but did not replace the detailed map entirely.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A. A simplified map improved route planning for residents throughout the transit network.

B. A simplified map offered a specific benefit for newcomers without improving every navigation task.

C. Experienced residents needed the detailed map to identify routes involving transfers.

D. Transit designers found that familiarity mattered more than map design on every task.

Practice this lesson: 1.03, 1.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.3

Answer: B

  1. Identify the relevant group: residents unfamiliar with the network.
  2. Identify the improvement and its limit: better direct-route identification, without better transfer-route identification.
  3. Select B, which preserves both the benefit and the limit.

Why A fails: It generalizes beyond the specific group and task that improved.

Why C fails: Experienced residents performed similarly with either map; necessity is not shown.

Why D fails: The text does not compare the size of familiarity's influence with design's influence on every task.

Key takeaway: Check population, outcome, and comparison before accepting a summary of research.

1.4 Retrieve details through faithful paraphrase

For a detail question, locate the statement that answers the precise question. Read enough surrounding text to resolve references such as this difference or the latter group. Then paraphrase the answer before comparing choices. Your paraphrase should preserve the actor, action, condition, and comparison. It may use entirely different words from the passage.

Distinguish the reported observation from an explanation offered for it. If listeners rated a passage as faster, the recording did not necessarily become faster. If an author argues that a policy helped, that is not automatically a fact the narrator endorses. Often the trap is a small substitution: actual performance for perceived performance, later for earlier, or one participant group for another.

W1.4: Sound versus judgment

In a hypothetical listening study, all participants heard the same piano recording. One group was told that the performer was a student; another was told that the performer was an established professional. The second group rated the playing as more confident, but the groups gave similar ratings for speed. The researchers measured listeners' judgments, not changes in the recording itself.

According to the text, how did the groups' judgments differ?

A. The student-labeled recording was judged faster but less confident.

B. The professional-labeled recording contained more confident playing.

C. The groups differed in speed ratings but agreed on confidence.

D. The professional-labeled recording received higher confidence ratings, with similar speed ratings.

Practice this lesson: 1.06, 1.18.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.4

Answer: D

  1. Identify the requested detail: the difference between groups' judgments.
  2. Resolve 'the second group' as the group given the professional label.
  3. Keep perception separate from the recording: confidence ratings differed; speed ratings were similar. D preserves both.

Why A fails: It invents a speed difference.

Why B fails: It turns a difference in judgment into a difference in the identical recording.

Why C fails: It reverses which rating differed and which remained similar.

Key takeaway: A faithful paraphrase changes wording without changing what was actually observed.

1.5 Read scientific explanations without outside knowledge

For a scientific passage, identify the question, the result, and any explanation or limitation. Translate unfamiliar labels into stable roles: organism, treatment, measured response. Keep the labels distinct, but do not assume you need a biology or physics lesson to answer a reading question. The text must supply the relevant relationship.

Separate what happened from why it may have happened. A proposed mechanism is not a demonstrated mechanism unless the passage establishes it. Similarly, an explanation can remain plausible even when the researchers acknowledge another possibility. A central idea may report a finding plus uncertainty about its cause. Retain that uncertainty without weakening the finding itself.

W1.5: A result with an unresolved mechanism

In a hypothetical experiment, algae exposed to intermittent light produced more biomass than algae receiving the same total light continuously. Researchers suggested that dark intervals allowed a light-processing system to recover. However, they had measured biomass, not that system's recovery. They planned a second experiment to test the proposed explanation while holding the total light exposure constant again.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A. Intermittent light increased algal biomass, but the suggested explanation required further testing.

B. Dark intervals were shown to restore an algal system responsible for processing light.

C. The researchers could not compare biomass because total light exposure differed.

D. The experiment established that light exposure matters less than recovery time for algae.

Practice this lesson: 1.09, 1.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.5

Answer: A

  1. Find the established result: more biomass under intermittent light despite equal total light.
  2. Identify the explanation's status: recovery is suggested but was not measured.
  3. Select A, which reports the result firmly and the mechanism cautiously.

Why B fails: It treats the proposed mechanism as directly demonstrated.

Why C fails: The text explicitly says total light exposure was the same.

Why D fails: The study does not establish a general ranking of the importance of these factors.

Key takeaway: Match the text's certainty separately for the finding and for its explanation.

1.6 Synthesize history and humanities passages

History and humanities passages often complicate an easy story: a practice may be older than expected, an apparent break may preserve a tradition, or a surviving record may represent only part of a community. Track dates, sources, and whose interpretation is presented. Ask whether new evidence replaces an earlier account or merely makes it more precise.

Do not import familiar historical narratives. Words such as modern, traditional, formal, and popular do not by themselves establish superiority or conflict. For central ideas, identify the relationship between the evidence and the broader account. For details, keep source limitations attached to the information they limit. A partial record does not necessarily make every fact in that record unreliable.

W1.6: New form, older practice

In an imagined coastal town, a museum labels a 1930 festival as the beginning of public storytelling. A historian finds earlier shopkeepers' diaries describing stories exchanged at open evening gatherings. Unlike the later festival, those gatherings had no fixed program or official sponsor. The historian argues that the festival organized an existing public practice rather than creating it.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A. Official sponsorship made the town's storytelling more popular than it had previously been.

B. Shopkeepers' diaries reveal that the town's earliest stories were performed in 1930.

C. Evidence of earlier gatherings suggests the festival formalized an existing storytelling practice.

D. The festival reproduced every feature of the town's earlier evening gatherings.

Practice this lesson: 1.15.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.6

Answer: C

  1. Identify the original account: public storytelling began with the festival.
  2. Connect the earlier diaries to the revision: public gatherings already included storytelling.
  3. Choose C. The new event changed the practice's organization, not its existence.

Why A fails: No attendance or popularity comparison is provided.

Why B fails: The diaries describe gatherings earlier than 1930.

Why D fails: The text identifies differences in programming and sponsorship.

Key takeaway: Preserve the difference between beginning a practice and formalizing one that already exists.

1.7 Find central ideas in literary prose and poetry

Literary passages often communicate a central idea through a change in attention, feeling, or behavior. Establish the literal situation first: who is present, what happens, and what the speaker notices. Then ask what the details collectively reveal. A modest emotional shift may be more accurate than a sweeping lesson about humanity.

Interpret figurative language in its local context. If a house 'holds its breath,' ask what the surrounding actions suggest: suspense, quiet, anticipation, or something else. Do not assume every object is a symbol or every speaker is the author. A detail question may require only the literal action. An answer about theme must explain the text's pattern, not merely sound profound.

W1.7: A small change in attention

The bench was wet; I stood beneath the tree. I checked the road, then checked my watch again. A sparrow shook the water from a leaf, and drops rang softly on a metal drain. The bus came late. I climbed aboard and found a place beside the window, looking back: the tree was small, the road was much the same, yet I had heard what haste had hidden there.

Which choice best states the main idea of the poem?

A. A delayed bus causes the speaker to reject the demands of daily life.

B. An unwanted delay allows the speaker to notice something usually overlooked.

C. The speaker prefers familiar urban sounds to the sounds of nature.

D. The speaker realizes that careful planning can prevent everyday frustration.

Practice this lesson: 1.11, 1.19.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.7

Answer: B

  1. Establish the literal situation: the speaker waits impatiently for a late bus.
  2. Track the shift from checking the watch to noticing the sparrow and falling water.
  3. Read the ending with that shift: the surroundings are familiar, but the speaker's attention changes. B fits.

Why A fails: The speaker boards the bus; no rejection of daily life is stated.

Why C fails: The poem attends to interacting natural and built sounds without ranking them.

Why D fails: No planning solution is offered; the insight comes from an unplanned wait.

Key takeaway: Anchor a literary central idea in the passage's actual movement of attention or feeling.

1.8 Choose between true but narrow and adequately central

When two answers seem correct, compare coverage and fidelity. Coverage asks whether the answer accounts for the major parts of the passage. Fidelity asks whether every claim stays within the text. The best central idea needs both. An answer listing many facts can still miss their relationship; a broad statement can cover the topic while inventing a conclusion.

Compare specifically: one choice may capture an example; another may capture the example and its qualification. Preserve a balanced distinction when the passage makes one, or a strong claim when the passage supports it. Answer length, positive tone, and cautious wording are unreliable guides.

W1.8: Two benefits with different reach

A theater began projecting translated dialogue above the stage. Visitors unfamiliar with the performance language reported following the plot more easily. Some fluent visitors also used the projections when actors spoke softly. Yet several attendees found that looking upward interrupted their attention to facial expressions. The theater continued the projections while testing a lower position for the text.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A. Projected translations helped visitors unfamiliar with the performance language follow the plot.

B. Projected text prevented audiences from attending to actors' facial expressions.

C. The theater's translation project mainly benefited visitors already fluent in the performance language.

D. Projected text aided comprehension but created an attention problem the theater sought to reduce.

Practice this lesson: 1.20.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.8

Answer: D

  1. List the major parts: comprehension benefits, a divided-attention problem, and an attempted adjustment.
  2. Compare A and D: A is true but covers only one benefit for one group.
  3. Select D because it represents the passage's overall trade-off and the theater's response.

Why A fails: It is a supported detail but omits the problem and the resulting adjustment.

Why B fails: It turns a reported interruption for several attendees into an absolute prevention.

Why C fails: The passage does not establish that fluent visitors were the main beneficiaries.

Key takeaway: A true statement can lose because another accurate choice explains more of the passage's central relationship.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

  • Central idea: state what the passage establishes about its topic, including its defining relationship.
  • Detail: preserve the actor, action, condition, comparison, and reference exactly.
  • Scope: check the population, outcome, time window, and strength of every claim in the answer.
  • Close choices: prefer adequate coverage with full fidelity; distinguish observations, explanations, and intended outcomes.

Begin with four brief reasoning drills, then complete sixteen multiple-choice questions. Later items generally demand closer distinctions. Identify the evidence for each answer and the specific flaw in its nearest competitor.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 1.01

A school replaced some lawn with a vegetable garden. The harvest supplied only a small part of cafeteria meals, but classes used the beds for experiments, drawing, and measuring. Teachers considered these uses the garden's greatest contribution.

Give the topic in a few words. Then write one sentence stating the central idea.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 1.01

Topic versus central idea

Answer: Topic: a school vegetable garden. Central idea: teachers valued the garden more for its educational uses than for the food it supplied.

The central idea connects the small harvest with the educational contribution that teachers considered greatest. It preserves whose evaluation is reported. "The school grows vegetables" names an activity but misses the comparison.

If you missed it: Add what the passage says about the topic, especially its explicit comparison.

Drill 1.02

An editor expected audio editions to attract only existing readers. Instead, many listeners reported never having read the magazine. Existing readers also subscribed to the audio version, especially for use while commuting.

Complete this map in your own words: expectation → finding → additional finding.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 1.02

Passage mapping

Answer: Only existing readers expected → many listeners were new to the magazine → some existing readers also used audio while commuting.

The result expands the anticipated audience without excluding the original audience. 'Audio replaced print' would add a claim about behavior the text does not report.

If you missed it: Check whether new information replaces an earlier group or adds another group.

Drill 1.03

In a hypothetical trial, some novice jugglers who watched slow demonstrations made fewer errors in their first practice session than novices who watched regular-speed demonstrations. The trial did not track later performance.

Repair this summary: 'Slow demonstrations make all jugglers more skilled in the long term.'

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 1.03

Repairing an overstated paraphrase

Answer: Some novice jugglers who watched slow demonstrations made fewer first-session errors than novices who watched regular-speed demonstrations.

The original summary expands some novices to all jugglers, changes a specific error comparison into general skill, and adds a long-term conclusion. The repair preserves the comparison group and removes all three overstatements.

If you missed it: Underline the population, measured outcome, and time window before paraphrasing.

Drill 1.04

The first mural used mineral pigments; the second used synthetic pigments. Conservators found fading only in the latter, though both had cracks near their lower edges.

Which mural had faded, and what condition affected both murals?

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 1.04

Resolving a reference

Answer: The second mural, with synthetic pigments, had faded. Both murals had cracks near their lower edges.

'The latter' refers to the second mural. The two observations concern different features: fading affected one mural, while cracking affected both.

If you missed it: Replace pronouns and reference phrases with their specific nouns before answering.
