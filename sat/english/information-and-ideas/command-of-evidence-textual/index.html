Review and reference

Choose the quotation or finding that most directly bears on the exact claim.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Turn a claim into a precise description of the evidence it needs.
  • Select quotations that support literary interpretations without adding unsupported motives.
  • Evaluate findings that strengthen or weaken research claims, including competing explanations.
  • Distinguish evidence of an effect from evidence of its cause, mechanism, or scope.

3.1 Isolate the claim and predict the needed evidence

Evidence questions supply a claim and ask which quotation or finding supports, strengthens, or weakens it. Ask: if this choice is true, what happens to the claim? Discussing the correct topic is not enough.

Identify the population, proposed relationship, and important limits. Predict the kind of observation that would matter before comparing choices. Read the task carefully: a weakening question requires evidence that makes the claim less convincing.

When a stem says "which finding, if true," accept each proposed finding for evaluation. Do not reject it because the passage has not already established that it occurred. The passage supplies the claim; the options supply possible evidence.

W3.1: Match the outcome in the claim

In a hypothetical museum study, a curator proposes that labels describing how a ceramic object was made direct visitors' attention to physical traces of its production. She predicts that these labels will be more effective for that purpose than labels describing the artist's childhood. Visitors are randomly assigned to read one of the two label types beside the same objects.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the curator's prediction?

A. Visitors reading production labels correctly recalled more dates from the labels.

B. Visitors reading childhood labels described the artists as more relatable.

C. Visitors reading production labels identified more tool marks on the objects.

D. Visitors in both groups considered the objects equally attractive.

Practice this lesson: 3.01, 3.06.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W3.1

Answer: C

  1. The target outcome is attention to physical traces of production, not enjoyment or general learning.
  2. Predict a comparison in which production-label readers notice more relevant physical features.
  3. C supplies that comparison: tool marks are visible traces of making the objects.

Why A fails: Remembering dates measures recall, not noticing physical traces.

Why B fails: Relatability concerns the other label's possible benefit, not the curator's prediction.

Why D fails: Equal attractiveness ratings do not tell us what visitors noticed.

Key takeaway: Name the measured outcome before comparing evidence choices.

3.2 Choose quotations that support an interpretation

Translate a literary interpretation into an observable feature: an action, contrast, statement, or image. If the interpretation has two connected parts, prefer a quotation establishing their relationship. Merely naming the character or subject supplies little support.

Read figurative language through context. A gesture can support an attitude without naming it, but do not invent a private motive. Track who speaks and whether actions differ from dialogue. Select evidence for the stated interpretation, not the line that sounds most important.

Some questions ask which quotation illustrates a stated technique or pattern. Use the definition supplied. Check both the feature itself and any relationship it is said to highlight; repetition alone may not establish the particular contrast described.

W3.2: Inheritance and independence in a poem

The following original poem describes a potter at work.

My mother's bowl stands near the wheel. Its glaze is dark as winter rain. I keep her curve beneath the rim, then turn the lip toward my own hand. Outside, the first cart shakes the lane. I set the finished bowl to dry.

Which quotation best supports the interpretation that the speaker respects an inherited design while adapting it?

A. "My mother's bowl stands near the wheel."

B. "I keep her curve beneath the rim, / then turn the lip toward my own hand."

C. "Its glaze is dark as winter rain."

D. "I set the finished bowl to dry."

Practice this lesson: 3.02, 3.05, 3.08.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W3.2

Answer: B

  1. The interpretation requires both preservation and modification.
  2. 'Keep her curve' supplies preservation; 'turn the lip' supplies a personal change.
  3. B joins the two actions in the same design, directly supporting the interpretation.

Why A fails: The mother's bowl is present, but the line does not establish how the speaker uses its design.

Why C fails: The description supplies a visual quality, not an act of preserving or adapting.

Why D fails: Finishing the bowl reveals neither its source nor its changes.

Key takeaway: For a two-part interpretation, look for evidence connecting both parts.

3.3 Find evidence that strengthens a hypothesis

A hypothesis proposes an explanation or relationship. Ask: what result would be more expected if this hypothesis were accurate? Such a result strengthens the hypothesis without necessarily proving it.

Useful evidence can show the predicted difference, connect a proposed cause to its outcome, or reduce a competing explanation. Preserve the prediction's direction. If scent supposedly guides insects, showing that flowers have a scent does not establish that insects use it.

W3.3: Test the proposed guidance signal

In a hypothetical study, researchers observe moths visiting pale flowers after dark. They hypothesize that the flowers' scent helps guide the moths to them even when the flowers cannot be seen. The researchers can release the scent at an empty location while controlling whether the location is visible.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the hypothesis?

A. In complete darkness, moths approached an outlet releasing the scent more often than an otherwise identical outlet releasing unscented air.

B. The pale flowers produced more scent after sunset than before sunset.

C. Moths visited pale flowers more frequently than dark flowers under bright light.

D. The flowers' scent contained several different chemical compounds.

Practice this lesson: 3.07, 3.19.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W3.3

Answer: A

  1. The claim concerns moth behavior guided by scent without a visible flower.
  2. Predict greater attraction to scent when visual guidance is unavailable.
  3. A supplies that behavior and an unscented comparison under the required condition.

Why B fails: The timing makes the hypothesis plausible but does not show that moths respond to the scent.

Why C fails: A difference under bright light does not isolate scent or remove visual guidance.

Why D fails: Chemical complexity does not establish a guiding effect on moths.

Key takeaway: Evidence that a proposed cause exists is weaker than evidence that it produces the predicted response.

3.4 Find evidence that weakens a claim

Weakening evidence makes an explanation or conclusion less convincing without necessarily proving it false. It may reveal an alternative cause, an unrepresentative sample, a faulty measurement, or a result contrary to the prediction.

Identify the argument's evidence and the connection it makes to the conclusion. Ask whether a finding damages that connection. Added complexity is not automatically a weakness. A second cause need not weaken a claim that the first contributes; the original wording determines what counts against it.

W3.4: When a count does not measure adoption

At an invented archaeological site, a layer dated to the town's later period contains three times as many ceramic fragments as an equally sized layer from its earlier period. An archaeologist argues that the greater fragment count indicates that ordinary households used a wider variety of ceramic cooking vessels during the later period.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the archaeologist's argument?

A. Some earlier-period fragments have decorative patterns also found in the later layer.

B. The town's population was approximately the same in both periods.

C. Both layers contain fragments of vessels made from locally available clay.

D. Most later-period fragments fit together into a few large ceremonial vessels rather than household cooking vessels.

Practice this lesson: 3.04, 3.09.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W3.4

Answer: D

  1. The conclusion concerns the variety of household cooking vessels.
  2. The argument uses more fragments as evidence of that variety.
  3. D shows that the increased count can come from a few vessels outside the claimed category.

Why A fails: Shared decoration does not establish how many kinds of cooking vessels households used.

Why B fails: A stable population does not undermine the inference and removes one possible explanation for a larger count.

Why C fails: The source of the clay does not resolve vessel type, variety, or use.

Key takeaway: Check whether the observation actually measures the thing claimed.

3.5 Compare the right groups under the right conditions

A comparison supports an explanation most clearly when groups differ in the proposed cause and are similar in other relevant ways. Check who entered each group, where they started, what changed, and when outcomes were measured. Random assignment helps address preexisting differences; it does not fix poor measurement.

Do not automatically dismiss observational findings with "correlation is not causation." Identify the specific competing explanation the evidence leaves open or addresses. For change claims, comparing improvements can matter more than comparing final levels.

W3.5: Separate improvement from a head start

In a hypothetical school study, students who choose an optional peer-feedback workshop produce stronger final essays than students who do not attend. The coordinator argues that the workshop improves revision skill. A reviewer notes that students already skilled at writing may be especially likely to enroll.

Which additional finding, if true, would most directly address the reviewer's concern while supporting the coordinator's argument?

A. Workshop participants report that they enjoyed exchanging suggestions.

B. Workshop participants submit their final essays earlier than nonparticipants.

C. Among students with similar initial revision scores, participants improve more on a new revision task than nonparticipants.

D. Several workshop participants had earned high grades in previous writing courses.

Practice this lesson: 3.13, 3.19.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W3.5

Answer: C

  1. The reviewer proposes prior skill as an explanation for the final difference.
  2. Look for evidence that compares improvement among students with similar starting skill.
  3. C supplies that comparison on a new task. It addresses the stated concern, although enrollment was still not random.

Why A fails: Enjoyment does not measure improvement or account for starting skill.

Why B fails: Submission time does not resolve whether the workshop improved revision.

Why D fails: Prior high achievement is compatible with the reviewer's concern.

Key takeaway: Match the comparison to the particular alternative explanation in the passage.

3.6 Distinguish an effect from its mechanism and scope

Related claims require different evidence. Existence: something occurs. Effect: one factor changes an outcome. Mechanism: it works through a particular process. Scope: the relationship holds for specified groups or conditions. Evidence at one level does not automatically establish the others.

For mechanisms, seek evidence about the intermediate process or an intervention separating competing pathways. For scope, preserve "only," "chiefly," or "during drought." Absolute claims can be correct when justified; they simply create stronger evidence requirements.

W3.6: Identify the route from cause to outcome

In a hypothetical gardening experiment, mulch increases seedling survival. Researchers propose that it does so primarily by slowing water loss from the soil rather than by protecting seedlings from insects. They can separately maintain equal soil moisture across mulched and unmulched beds or exclude insects from both kinds of bed.

Which finding, if true, would most strongly support the proposed mechanism?

A. Equalizing soil moisture eliminates the survival advantage of mulch, whereas excluding insects does not.

B. Mulched beds contain fewer insects than unmulched beds.

C. Seedlings survive more frequently with mulch than without it.

D. Gardeners use several different materials as mulch.

Practice this lesson: 3.03, 3.10, 3.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W3.6

Answer: A

  1. The effect is already given; the disputed issue is how it occurs.
  2. If moisture explains the advantage, removing the moisture difference should remove that advantage.
  3. A shows this pattern and indicates that the advantage persists when insect exposure is removed.

Why B fails: Fewer insects could support the competing pathway without establishing the moisture pathway.

Why C fails: This repeats the effect without distinguishing explanations.

Why D fails: The range of materials does not test either mechanism.

Key takeaway: When the claim specifies 'because,' evidence of the outcome alone is incomplete.

3.7 Reject true but insufficient evidence

An accurate quotation about a character may fail to support the stated interpretation. Does it establish the attitude, merely describe the setting, or fit several incompatible motives equally well?

Include context that changes a line's meaning: a stage direction can make behavior diverge from dialogue. For findings, check the relevant outcome and population. "True" and "useful evidence for this claim" are separate tests.

W3.7: Read words beside actions

The following is from an original play. Mira is about to show her father a machine she built.

MIRA: It works. I tested every gear. FATHER: Then why wait outside? MIRA: I am not waiting for permission. [She reaches for the door, then withdraws her hand when he looks away.] FATHER: Shall we go in? MIRA: If you have time.

Which quotation best supports the interpretation that Mira asserts independence while still seeking her father's attention?

A. "It works. I tested every gear."

B. "Then why wait outside?"

C. "Shall we go in?"

D. "I am not waiting for permission. [She reaches for the door, then withdraws her hand when he looks away.]"

Practice this lesson: 3.12, 3.17.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W3.7

Answer: D

  1. The interpretation joins an assertion of independence to behavior seeking attention.
  2. D includes Mira's denial that she needs permission and her hesitation when her father looks away.
  3. The contrast between dialogue and action supplies both parts of the interpretation.

Why A fails: Testing gears shows preparation but does not establish the relationship to her father's attention.

Why B fails: The father's question identifies a delay without showing Mira's assertion or its tension with her behavior.

Why C fails: The invitation is the father's action, not evidence of Mira's conflicting behavior.

Key takeaway: Do not discard stage directions when the interpretation concerns a character's behavior.

3.8 Resolve difficult claim-evidence comparisons

Hard questions often offer several relevant findings. Separate the claim's links: a policy changed behavior; it worked through a proposed cause; a named alternative does not explain the change. Strong evidence may distinguish explanations rather than report the largest improvement.

Complete this sentence: "This supports or weakens the claim because ..." Use only the passage and the choice. If you must invent a bridge, reconsider. If two choices seem strong, favor the one addressing the exact claim more directly.

W3.8: Distinguish two plausible explanations

In a hypothetical theater study, ticket holders who receive a reminder describing the play's story attend more often than those receiving no reminder. The theater proposes that increased interest in the story, rather than a simple reminder of the date, explains the difference. Researchers then randomly assign new ticket holders to receive either the story reminder or a date-only reminder, both sent at the same time.

Which finding, if true, would most strongly support the theater's proposed explanation?

A. Both reminder groups remember the date more accurately than a group receiving no reminder.

B. The groups recall the date equally well before the performance, but the story group reports greater interest and subsequently attends more often.

C. The story reminder is longer than the date-only reminder.

D. Within the story group, people who attend rate the performance more highly than people who leave early.

Practice this lesson: 3.19.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W3.8

Answer: B

  1. The question asks about increased story interest versus remembering the date.
  2. A useful result separates these explanations while preserving the attendance difference.
  3. B holds measured date recall equal and connects the story reminder with both greater interest and higher attendance.
  4. This is stronger support, not conclusive proof that no other feature of the reminders mattered.

Why A fails: Better date recall could support the alternative explanation and does not separate the reminder groups.

Why C fails: A length difference introduces another possible explanation without demonstrating the proposed one.

Why D fails: Postattendance ratings do not explain why ticket holders initially decided to attend.

Key takeaway: Prefer evidence that separates the competing explanations named in the claim.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

  • State the exact claim, including the outcome, comparison, mechanism, and scope that matter.
  • For quotations, connect specific words or actions to every essential part of the interpretation.
  • For findings, ask how the observation changes support for the claim and its named alternatives.
  • Choose the strongest direct connection; do not demand proof or supply missing facts yourself.

Begin with four brief reasoning drills, then complete sixteen multiple-choice questions. Later items generally demand closer distinctions. Identify the evidence for each answer and the specific flaw in its nearest competitor.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 3.01

A teacher claims that having students explain why wrong answers fail improves their ability to solve unfamiliar questions, even when total practice time remains unchanged.

State the proposed cause, outcome, and comparison needed for useful evidence.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 3.01

Identify the exact target

Answer: Cause: explaining why wrong answers fail. Outcome: performance on unfamiliar questions. Comparison: students practicing with and without that explanation step for equal total time.

Evidence about enjoyment or scores on repeated questions would not directly test transfer to unfamiliar questions. Equal time addresses the claim's stated condition.

If you missed it: Underline the outcome and any limiting phrase before imagining evidence.

Drill 3.02

A critic interprets an original story as showing a character who welcomes an opportunity but hesitates to leave familiar routines.

Describe a kind of action or quotation that would support both parts of the interpretation.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 3.02

Translate interpretation into evidence

Answer: For example: the character eagerly accepts an invitation to travel, then repeatedly delays packing while continuing the usual evening routine.

Acceptance supports welcome; delaying departure through familiar routines supports hesitation. Merely looking at a suitcase would not clearly establish either attitude.

If you missed it: Turn each part of the interpretation into behavior a reader could observe.

Drill 3.03

A town claims that a new reading room increases library visits because it offers quiet space. The number of visits increases after the room opens.

What part of the claim does the reported finding support, and what does it leave unresolved?

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 3.03

Separate effect from mechanism

Answer: It supports an association between the room's opening and more visits. It does not establish that quiet space caused the increase or rule out other simultaneous changes.

The finding concerns the outcome after the opening; it does not isolate the proposed reason for that outcome.

If you missed it: Separate 'what changed' from 'why it changed.'

Drill 3.04

A coach credits a new warm-up routine for faster race times. Runners using the old routine improved just as much during the same period under otherwise similar training conditions.

Explain precisely why the additional finding weakens the coach's explanation without proving that the new routine has no benefit.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 3.04

Recognize a weakening comparison

Answer: Similar improvement without the new routine suggests that another shared factor could explain the gains. The finding does not directly establish whether either routine has benefits compared with no warm-up.

The evidence challenges the claim that the new routine explains the observed improvement, not every possible benefit of warming up.

If you missed it: Name the particular explanation weakened; avoid claiming more than the comparison establishes.
