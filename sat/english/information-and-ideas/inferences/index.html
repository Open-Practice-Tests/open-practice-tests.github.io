By the end, you should be able to:

  • Complete a passage by following its argument and combining relevant clues.
  • Preserve limits involving groups, time, comparisons, and causation.

2.1 Move from what is stated to what is supported

An inference is a conclusion the text supports without necessarily stating it directly. The task is usually to choose the best-supported conclusion, not one that is certain under every imaginable circumstance. Evidence can support a reasonable explanation or prediction even when it does not prove it.

Use three moves: identify the relevant facts, explain their connection, and check the answer against both. Ask, What makes this conclusion more justified than the alternatives? An unsupported answer might be true in the world; the problem is that this passage does not establish it. Do not turn that distinction into a claim that the answer is false.

W2.1: The missing instructions

At a community cooking project, volunteers turned family recipes into printed instruction cards. Experienced cooks then prepared the dishes successfully from the cards. First-time cooks struggled, however. When the experienced cooks demonstrated the recipes, they repeatedly adjusted the heat or added water without consulting the cards. Several beginners succeeded after watching these demonstrations.

Which inference is best supported by the text?

A. The beginners preferred demonstrations to written instructions.

B. The cards omitted some knowledge the experienced cooks used.

C. The experienced cooks had memorized all the printed instructions.

D. The beginners were unfamiliar with every dish in the project.

Practice this lesson: 2.01, 2.04.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.1

Answer: B

  1. Locate the gap: experienced cooks succeeded from the cards, while beginners struggled.
  2. Connect the unrecorded adjustments with the improvement after demonstrations.
  3. Infer that some useful knowledge was available through experience or observation but absent from the cards.

Why A fails: Success after observing does not establish a preference.

Why C fails: The passage does not test memorization, and memorization would not explain unprinted adjustments.

Why D fails: Being new to preparing a recipe does not establish unfamiliarity with the dish.

Key takeaway: A strong inference explains the observed pattern using the specific clues provided.

2.2 Complete the passage in the direction it is moving

For a logical-completion question, summarize the unfinished reasoning before studying the options. A passage may introduce an expectation, report conflicting evidence, and need a revised conclusion. Or it may describe a mechanism and need its consequence. Words such as however, therefore, and although help identify the relationship, but they cannot replace reading the evidence.

Predict an idea, not exact wording. Then insert each serious candidate into the blank. Does it complete the whole argument, or merely repeat one earlier observation? A conclusion can qualify an explanation without rejecting it entirely.

W2.2: A preference that changed

In a hypothetical study, moths approached blue artificial flowers more often than white ones when both offered equal amounts of nectar. After several nights in which only the white flowers offered nectar, the same moths approached white flowers more often. Flower positions were changed each night, so location could not consistently signal a reward. These results suggest that the moths' flower choices ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

A. can be modified by experience with available rewards.

B. are determined primarily by where flowers are located.

C. reflect no preference when rewards are equal.

D. become independent of nectar after repeated feeding.

Practice this lesson: 2.05, 2.18.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.2

Answer: A

  1. Identify the initial pattern: with equal nectar, the moths favored blue.
  2. Track the change: rewarding white was followed by a preference for white.
  3. Complete the argument with a flexible preference shaped by reward experience.

Why B fails: Changing positions prevents a consistent location-reward relationship.

Why C fails: The first condition showed a blue preference despite equal rewards.

Why D fails: The changed choices followed a change in nectar availability; independence from nectar is unsupported.

Key takeaway: The needed conclusion often connects the first condition to the later change.

2.3 Combine clues instead of hunting for a matching sentence

Some inference answers are supported by two details that perform different jobs. One may establish a pattern; another may eliminate an explanation. Mark both before choosing. If you can justify an answer using only a sentence that also fits several competitors, you may have missed the decisive clue.

Avoid importing an extra step from your own knowledge. A useful bridge should follow from the passage: The material traveled, but the personnel did not; therefore, the material could spread independently of those personnel. It should not invent a motive, a funding source, or a historical event.

W2.3: How the play traveled

A fictional theater historian studies a nineteenth-century play performed in five coastal towns. The towns' scripts contain the same unusual handwritten correction. Their cast lists, however, name local residents rather than members of the touring company that first staged the play. Shipping records also list packages of theatrical scripts sent between these towns during the relevant months.

Which inference is best supported by the text?

A. The touring company secretly performed under local residents' names.

B. The local productions were more successful than the original production.

C. Each town independently discovered the same error in the script.

D. The play circulated through scripts used by local performers.

Practice this lesson: 2.10, 2.17.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.3

Answer: D

  1. The shared correction connects the scripts rather than merely their titles.
  2. The cast lists identify local performers, while shipping records support script circulation.
  3. Combine the clues: written materials helped transmit the play without requiring the original cast to travel.

Why A fails: Nothing indicates false names; this invents an explanation for contrary evidence.

Why B fails: No attendance, reviews, or other measures of success are given.

Why C fails: Independent corrections are possible, but circulation better explains all three clues.

Key takeaway: Prefer the conclusion that accounts for the clues together with the fewest added assumptions.

2.4 Preserve scope, quantity, and conditions

Before accepting an inference, check its boundaries: who, when, how many, and under what conditions? Evidence about sampled participants does not automatically describe everyone. A result observed once can establish that something is possible; it rarely establishes that it is typical. Conversely, do not reject a strong statement when the evidence actually warrants it.

Watch the direction of conditions. If a rule permits selection only for proposals with a budget, a budget is necessary for eligibility under that rule. It is not necessarily sufficient: other requirements may apply. An observed pattern alone does not establish such a rule. For negation, rewrite carefully: not all succeeded means at least one failed, not that none succeeded.

W2.4: The complete applications

A neighborhood arts fund reviewed forty applications in one funding round. All twelve applications that received grants included both a detailed budget and a proposed schedule. Of the twenty-eight applications that did not receive grants, seventeen also included both documents. The fund did not disclose its other evaluation criteria.

Which conclusion is best supported by the text?

A. Applications with both documents were more likely to receive grants than to be rejected.

B. The fund selected applications solely on artistic quality.

C. Both documents did not guarantee a grant in this round.

D. The fund will never support an application lacking either document.

Practice this lesson: 2.02, 2.08.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.4

Answer: C

  1. All funded applications had both documents, but seventeen unfunded applications did too.
  2. Those seventeen establish that the documents alone did not guarantee success.
  3. Keep the conclusion within this funding round; future policy is not given.

Why A fails: Only twelve of the twenty-nine applications with both documents received grants.

Why B fails: The other criteria are undisclosed, so sole reliance on artistic quality is unsupported.

Why D fails: An observed pattern in one round does not establish an exceptionless future rule.

Key takeaway: Do not reverse a condition or expand a sample result into a universal policy.

2.5 Make comparisons and predictions on the right basis

A prediction should extend an observed relationship to a situation where the relevant conditions still apply. Identify the observed difference and the conditions in which it appeared before predicting what happens next. Do not assume that a change improves everything: a material may gain strength but lose flexibility, and a strategy may work only in a particular environment.

For comparisons, name both sides and the measurement. More sensitive to moisture does not mean stronger overall. A change from each group's starting point also does not establish which group finishes higher unless the starting points are known.

W2.5: Packaging in humid air

In a hypothetical materials test, two films were equally transparent in dry air. In humid air, film P absorbed water and became cloudy, while film Q remained transparent. After both films dried completely, their original transparency returned. A manufacturer wants a window in a package to remain transparent throughout alternating dry and humid storage periods.

Which inference is best supported by the text?

A. Film P will become permanently less transparent after one humid period.

B. Film Q should better maintain visibility during humid periods.

C. Film Q will protect the package's contents from water more effectively.

D. Film P will be more transparent than film Q during dry periods.

Practice this lesson: 2.03, 2.09, 2.14.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.5

Answer: B

  1. The goal concerns visibility throughout storage, including humid periods.
  2. Q retained transparency in humidity; P regained it only after drying.
  3. Predict Q's advantage on the measured property, without inferring other packaging benefits.

Why A fails: P regained its original transparency after drying.

Why C fails: Water protection was not measured; transparency does not establish a moisture barrier.

Why D fails: The films were equally transparent in dry air.

Key takeaway: Extend the measured relationship, not an untested general impression of which option is better.

2.6 Reason about causes without overclaiming

An association can support a causal explanation, but its strength depends on the design and competing explanations. Ask what changed, what was measured, and what else could produce the pattern. A comparison group, random assignment, or a mechanism can strengthen causal reasoning. Their absence does not make all evidence useless.

Keep the outcome precise. Fewer insects counted at a lamp might mean fewer insects were attracted there; it does not necessarily mean fewer insects survived in the region. Choose a qualified causal conclusion when it fits the evidence, rather than either declaring proof or refusing every causal inference.

W2.6: Different lamps, different counts

In a hypothetical field study, two nearby parks initially had similar nightly insect counts around identical lamps. One park replaced its lamps with a different light spectrum. Counts around that park's lamps then fell, while counts in the unchanged park remained stable. Observers used the same sampling method, and both parks experienced similar weather during the study.

Which inference is best supported by the text?

A. The replacement lamps eliminated most insects living in the park.

B. Weather differences between the parks probably caused the decline.

C. All insect species avoid the replacement lamps equally.

D. The replacement lamps may have reduced attraction of insects.

Practice this lesson: 2.15.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.6

Answer: D

  1. The decline occurred after the replacement and only in the park with changed lamps.
  2. The stable comparison park and similar weather reduce some alternative explanations.
  3. Infer a plausible effect on local attraction, the outcome the counts most directly address.

Why A fails: Counts near lamps do not measure the entire park's population or survival.

Why B fails: The passage specifies similar weather; the comparison pattern favors the lamp explanation.

Why C fails: No species-by-species responses are reported.

Key takeaway: Match a causal inference to the design and to the outcome actually measured.

2.7 Infer attitudes from literary behavior and imagery

Literary passages often reveal an attitude through a contrast: what someone says versus does, an object kept versus dismissed, or a detail noticed versus ignored. Identify the behavior first, then ask which interpretation explains its context. Mixed feelings are common, but do not select a mixed-feelings answer merely because it sounds sophisticated.

Separate character, narrator, and speaker. A narrator's description is not automatically a character's belief, and a poem's speaker is not automatically its author. Infer no more biography than the passage supplies. Distinguish a reasonable emotional reading from a precise motive that would need additional evidence.

W2.7: The straight sign

The following original passage describes Leena's first day running her own shop.

When her brother arrived, Leena said that opening a shop was mostly paperwork. She turned the new sign toward the wall while he examined the shelves. But after he left, she carried it outside again. Three times she stepped across the road to see whether the lettering sat straight above the door.

Which inference about Leena is best supported by the text?

A. She cares about the opening more than she admits.

B. She believes the shop will fail unless her brother approves its sign.

C. She regrets choosing a sign instead of advertising in a newspaper.

D. She considers the shelves more important than the appearance of the entrance.

Practice this lesson: 2.07, 2.13.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.7

Answer: A

  1. Leena verbally minimizes the opening and hides its visible symbol from her brother.
  2. Her repeated checks afterward show private care about how the shop appears.
  3. Infer concealed investment in the opening, without deciding exactly why she conceals it.

Why B fails: Neither anticipated failure nor dependence on her brother's approval is established.

Why C fails: The passage mentions no newspaper advertising or regret about that choice.

Why D fails: The brother examines shelves; Leena's repeated attention goes to the sign.

Key takeaway: Use behavior to support an attitude; avoid inventing a detailed psychological explanation.

2.8 Resolve difficult inferences by testing the full chain

For a difficult question, build a short chain: evidence → permitted connection → conclusion. Then look for the vulnerable link. Does the answer turn surviving records into all past events, a minimum into an exact count, or evidence of use into evidence of origin? Close distractors often preserve most of the chain and alter one link.

Test the strongest competing answer explicitly. Name the extra fact it would need. Do not automatically prefer the weakest wording: choose the most informative conclusion supported by the full passage. Evidence can establish a firm lower bound while leaving the complete history uncertain.

W2.8: The earliest surviving sample

In a fictional archaeological investigation, excavators recover dyed cloth from a sealed burial dated to approximately 900 CE. Chemical analysis identifies a dye that could be produced from a local plant. No earlier cloth has survived at the site, where untreated fabric usually decays rapidly. The discovery establishes the dye's use there by approximately 900 CE, but ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

A. shows that the plant was first grown locally around that time.

B. makes earlier local use of the dye unlikely.

C. leaves the date of its first local use unresolved.

D. shows that the cloth itself was produced locally.

Practice this lesson: 2.12, 2.19.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.8

Answer: C

  1. The dated cloth establishes use by about 900 CE, a latest possible starting point.
  2. Earlier cloth is absent in conditions where fabric decays; absence cannot reliably date first use.
  3. Distinguish using the dye from inventing it, growing its source plant, or manufacturing the cloth locally.

Why A fails: An available plant source does not date cultivation.

Why B fails: Poor preservation makes the lack of earlier cloth weak evidence against earlier use.

Why D fails: A locally available dye source does not establish where this cloth was made.

Key takeaway: Separate what a discovery dates securely from the earlier history it leaves unresolved.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

  • Identify the conclusion the question needs: an implication, explanation, prediction, or logical completion.
  • Anchor the answer in the relevant clues and explain how those clues connect.
  • Preserve the people, conditions, time, quantity, comparison, and measured outcome.
  • Reject the closest alternative by naming its unsupported step; do not confuse unsupported with false.

Begin with four brief reasoning drills, then complete sixteen multiple-choice questions. Later items generally demand closer distinctions. Identify the evidence for each answer and the specific flaw in its nearest competitor.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 2.01

Ravi called the concert unimportant. Yet he saved the ticket and corrected a friend who named the wrong conductor.

Write one supported inference and identify the two clues supporting it.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 2.01

Stated fact versus supported inference

Answer: The concert mattered to Ravi more than his remark suggests: he saved a memento and cared about an accurate detail.

The actions support investment despite verbal dismissal. They do not establish that he enjoyed every performance or explain why he downplayed the event.

If you missed it: Pair the remark with the behavior before naming an attitude.

Drill 2.02

Every successful seedling in a trial received light. Some seedlings that received light did not survive.

Was receiving light sufficient to guarantee survival in this trial? Explain without generalizing beyond the trial.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 2.02

Direction of a condition

Answer: No. Some seedlings received light and still died, so light alone did not guarantee survival in this trial.

All survivors received light, but that relationship cannot be reversed. The text does not isolate what caused survival or failure.

If you missed it: Look for a case that satisfies the condition but lacks the outcome.

Drill 2.03

In a test, runners wearing fabric X stayed cooler than those wearing fabric Y in dry air. In humid air, their skin temperatures were similar.

Revise this overclaim: "Fabric X keeps runners cooler than fabric Y in any climate."

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 2.03

Prediction with a preserved condition

Answer: Fabric X kept runners cooler than fabric Y in the tested dry conditions; the test showed no comparable advantage in humid air.

The revision preserves both the measured outcome and the condition limiting the difference. It does not predict results in untested climates.

If you missed it: Attach the environmental condition to the comparison.

Drill 2.04

A town's surviving meeting minutes first mention a public garden in 1872. Minutes from the preceding decade are missing.

Explain why "The garden opened in 1872" is unsupported without claiming it is false.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 2.04

Unsupported versus disproved

Answer: The garden existed or was discussed by 1872, but missing earlier records prevent the first surviving mention from establishing its opening date.

Opening in 1872 remains possible. Earlier opening also fits the surviving evidence, so the text cannot choose between them.

If you missed it: Distinguish the first surviving record from the event's first occurrence.
