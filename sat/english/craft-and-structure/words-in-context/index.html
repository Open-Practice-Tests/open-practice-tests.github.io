1.1 Two task forms, one evidence standard

Words in Context questions take two main forms. A completion question asks which choice most logically and precisely fills a blank. A meaning question asks what an underlined word or phrase means as used in the text. For completion, build a description of the missing idea before comparing choices. For meaning, paraphrase the author's particular use of the marked expression. In both forms, the whole sentence and the relevant surrounding sentences decide the answer.

Read the prompt, then read the passage through once. Identify what the target describes or does: a result, an attitude, a relationship, or an action? Predict a short meaning such as "temporary until more data arrive." That prediction is a working hypothesis, not a commitment. Revise it when a choice captures the evidence more precisely. Finally, reread with the candidate in place. A familiar definition, a positive sound, or a smooth sentence is insufficient if the meaning conflicts with even one decisive clue.

W1.1: Predict a meaning before choosing a word

The first map of the underwater ridge was based on a single survey, which left several deep sections unmeasured. The research team described the map as ______. It could guide the next expedition, but its contours would be revised once measurements from the missing sections became available.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

A. provisional

B. rudimentary

C. inconclusive

D. speculative

Practice this lesson: 1.07, 1.08.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.1

Answer: A

  1. Identify the target: the blank characterizes the status of the map, not the sophistication of the equipment or the certainty of a scientific conclusion.
  2. Combine the clues: the map is usable now, and the team plans to revise it after collecting more measurements. Predict "accepted temporarily, pending further information."
  3. Choose A. "Provisional" means temporary or subject to revision; it captures both the map's current use and its expected replacement or adjustment.
  4. Substitute the word and check the contrast: a provisional map can guide an expedition while still requiring revision. Nothing in the passage makes those facts contradictory.

Why B fails: "Rudimentary" means basic or undeveloped. Unmeasured sections do not establish that the mapped sections are simple or technically primitive; the explicit distinction is temporary status.

Why C fails: "Inconclusive" means not settling a question. A map can leave questions open, but the sentence specifically emphasizes its use until revision, which "provisional" expresses more precisely.

Why D fails: "Speculative" means based substantially on conjecture. This map is based on an actual survey; missing measurements do not make its existing contours guesses.

Key takeaway: A useful prediction states the relationship established by the passage. Here it is "usable now, revisable later," not merely "imperfect."

1.2 Definitions, restatements, and examples

Writers often explain an unfamiliar word without announcing a formal definition. A colon, dash, appositive, or phrase such as "that is" may restate its meaning. Examples can also reveal a shared property: if a passage calls two schedules flexible and then shows that both can be changed, changeability is the relevant meaning. Use punctuation and signal phrases to locate a possible clue, then verify the actual relationship. A colon can introduce evidence or a list rather than a definition.

Translate the explanatory material into a compact description. Preserve its strength. "Made quieter" is not "eliminated"; "occurred occasionally" is not "rare enough to ignore." With examples, identify what they jointly establish instead of choosing a vivid detail from only one. If a technical-looking target is immediately explained in ordinary language, use that explanation before searching your memory for a scientific definition. The task tests the meaning supported here, not everything that the word might mean elsewhere.

  • Restatement clue: two expressions describe the same thing in different words.
  • Example clue: several instances reveal a common feature.
  • Degree clue: modifiers such as partly, nearly, slightly, and entirely limit the meaning.

W1.2: Use an explanation without overstating it

The concert hall's new lining Underlined: attenuated End underlined. the noise from the ventilation fans: the steady hum remained audible, but its volume was much lower than before. Engineers emphasized that the lining changed the strength of the sound, not the times at which the fans operated.

As used in the text, what does the word "attenuated" most nearly mean?

A. Interrupted

B. Diminished

C. Masked

D. Prevented

Practice this lesson: 1.09, 1.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.2

Answer: B

  1. Read beyond the marked word. The clause after the colon directly explains the effect: the hum remained audible at a much lower volume.
  2. Separate the measured change from adjacent possibilities. Sound strength decreased; operating times did not change, and sound did not disappear.
  3. Choose B. "Diminished" means reduced in amount, strength, or intensity. It fits a quieter but still audible hum.
  4. Check the limiting clue "remained audible." Any answer implying total removal goes beyond the explanation.

Why A fails: "Interrupted" means stopped temporarily or broken in continuity. The passage describes a steady hum and expressly excludes a change in fan operating times.

Why C fails: "Masked" means concealed or made harder to distinguish by something else. The text describes a reduction in the sound's strength, not another sound covering it.

Why D fails: "Prevented" means kept from occurring. The hum still occurred and remained audible, so this answer is too strong.

Key takeaway: A restatement often supplies both the meaning and its limit. Preserve "less," rather than turning it into "none."

1.3 Contrast, concession, and other logical relationships

A missing word can sit at the intersection of two ideas. Contrast presents a difference; concession acknowledges a fact that might create an expectation and then limits or reverses that expectation. In "Although the process is slow, its results are dependable," the speed and reliability differ, but "dependable" is not a dictionary opposite of "slow." Do not hunt for an antonym whenever you see although, yet, or however. Identify exactly which expectation the second statement changes.

Other relationships matter too. A cause can explain a result, an example can illustrate a claim, and a later sentence can qualify an earlier one. Write the relationship in ordinary language: "skeptical at first, willing to consider evidence later." Then test the target against both parts. A choice that fits the final sentence but erases the earlier contrast is incomplete. When a passage reports someone's changing position, also preserve the timeline: earlier resistance and later openness are compatible, not evidence of simultaneous indecision.

W1.3: Openness is not the same as agreement

Botanist Mira Sen initially dismissed a colleague's explanation for the vines' unusual growth. After examining the preliminary measurements, however, Sen became ______ to the proposal: she helped design a fair test and agreed that its outcome, whatever it was, should determine whether the explanation was accepted. She did not yet endorse the explanation itself.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

A. deferential

B. ambivalent

C. receptive

D. partial

Practice this lesson: 1.11, 1.12.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.3

Answer: C

  1. Locate the change signaled by "however": Sen moves away from dismissing the proposal.
  2. Use the later actions to define the new attitude. She will consider and fairly test the idea, but she has not accepted it. Predict "open to considering."
  3. Choose C. "Receptive to" means willing to consider or receive an idea; it does not require agreement.
  4. Check the last sentence. The best answer must allow openness without endorsement, which "receptive" does.

Why A fails: "Deferential" means yielding respectful regard to another's authority or judgment. Sen proposes an evidential test, not acceptance based on the colleague's standing.

Why B fails: "Ambivalent" means having mixed or conflicting attitudes. The passage establishes a clear commitment to fair consideration, not simultaneous attraction and aversion.

Why D fails: "Partial to" means favorably inclined toward or especially fond of something. The passage establishes willingness to consider the proposal fairly, not a special preference for it; acceptance will depend on the test.

Key takeaway: A change from rejection can lead to consideration rather than agreement. Let the passage specify the new position.

1.4 Familiar words with unfamiliar senses

Many difficult vocabulary questions use common words: bear, charge, check, command, register, reserve, or sustain. The challenge is selecting the sense activated by the sentence. A person can bear a burden, a tree can bear fruit, and a theory can bear on a dispute. The same written word does not have one substitute that works in every setting. First identify its grammatical role and the thing it acts on or describes.

Use a plain paraphrase instead of listing every dictionary definition. In "the evidence bears on the claim," ask what the evidence does in relation to the claim: it is relevant to it. Then verify that meaning in the surrounding argument. Pay special attention to a verb's object and to prepositions that accompany it. Do not choose a definition merely because it is the one you learned first. Conversely, do not reject an ordinary sense just because the question appears difficult; the local evidence decides.

W1.4: Let the sentence select the sense

Under daylight, the two fabric samples looked almost identical. Most visitors Underlined: registered End underlined. the difference only after an attendant placed the samples beneath a cooler lamp. Then the slight green tint in one sample became apparent, and visitors could consistently distinguish it from the other.

As used in the text, what does the word "registered" most nearly mean?

A. Recorded

B. Displayed

C. Approved

D. Noticed

Practice this lesson: 1.13, 1.14.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.4

Answer: D

  1. Identify who performs the action: visitors, rather than a measuring instrument or an official registry.
  2. Identify the object: a subtle visual difference. The following sentences describe that difference becoming apparent and distinguishable.
  3. Choose D. Here "registered" means noticed or consciously perceived. The new lighting allowed visitors to detect what they previously overlooked.
  4. Verify that the passage never requires a written entry, public display, or judgment of approval. Those are other possible associations with the word, not this use.

Why A fails: "Recorded" means preserved in a written, electronic, or other lasting form. Visitors perceive the difference; the text never describes making a record of it.

Why B fails: "Displayed" means showed or made visible. The lighting makes the tint apparent; the visitors are perceiving the difference, not exhibiting it.

Why C fails: "Approved" means accepted as satisfactory or authorized. Distinguishing the samples is a perceptual act, not a favorable evaluation.

Key takeaway: Determine who does what to what. That simple sentence frame often eliminates an attractive but irrelevant familiar definition.

1.5 Precision among near synonyms: degree and scope

Close choices may share a general direction while differing in exactly what they claim. Support is not always proof. A reduction is not necessarily elimination. A narrow exception does not necessarily overturn a rule. Translate each finalist into a distinct plain-language claim, then ask which claim the passage licenses. Avoid the vague test "they both sound positive." Direction is only the first filter.

Check degree, scope, certainty, and duration. Does the text concern all cases or the observed cases? A possibility or an established result? A temporary condition or a lasting one? These distinctions are contextual, not automatic reasons to reject strong words. "Eliminate" is correct when the text establishes complete removal. If two words both seem defensible, locate the extra claim each adds. The better choice is the one whose specific meaning is supported, not necessarily the milder or longer word. The passage must settle the distinction; do not manufacture a difference unsupported by normal usage.

W1.5: More support without a more detailed claim

Wear patterns on several stone tools suggested that the settlement's residents had worked plant fibers. A separate analysis later identified traces of fiber on some of the same tools. Archaeologists regarded the chemical finding as evidence that could ______ the interpretation drawn from the wear patterns: a different type of observation pointed toward the same activity.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

A. refine

B. corroborate

C. elaborate

D. qualify

Practice this lesson: 1.15, 1.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.5

Answer: B

  1. State the relationship between the findings: different observations independently support the same interpretation.
  2. Distinguish additional support from a change to the claim. The second analysis identifies material traces pointing toward the same activity; it does not supply a narrower or more detailed interpretation.
  3. Choose B. "Corroborate" means provide additional support for a statement or interpretation. That is the precise role assigned to the chemical finding.
  4. Check the colon's explanation. "A different type of observation" establishes an additional source of support, while "the same activity" preserves the original interpretation.

Why A fails: "Refine" means improve a claim's precision or adjust its details. The additional evidence strengthens the existing interpretation without changing what activity the interpretation identifies.

Why C fails: "Elaborate" means develop or explain in greater detail. The finding strengthens the proposed activity without providing a fuller account of how the fiber work occurred.

Why D fails: "Qualify" means limit or modify a claim. The finding supplies matching support, not a restriction or exception to the interpretation.

Key takeaway: Independent support can strengthen a claim without refining its details or limiting its scope.

Practice this lesson: 1.15 and 1.16.

1.6 Verbs, their objects, and natural combinations

A verb specifies an action or relationship, and its object helps determine which meaning fits. Researchers can reconcile estimates, reconcile people, or reconcile themselves to a setback; the surrounding words activate different uses. Ask what is being acted on and what result the action produces. With a blank, read the phrase on both sides, including any preposition. The right general idea still needs the right relationship: evidence can support a claim, while an explanation can account for evidence.

A collocation is a conventional combination of words, such as draw a distinction or raise an objection. Knowing these combinations helps you interpret fluent academic English, but sound alone should not decide an answer. First establish the intended meaning. Then use the combination as a check. On a well-formed vocabulary question, wrong choices can be grammatical and natural yet logically wrong. If you rely only on what "sounds academic," you may choose a familiar phrase that describes the wrong action.

W1.6: What action resolves the mismatch?

Two reports appeared to disagree about the town's annual water use. One included factories outside the residential district, whereas the other counted households alone. To ______ the estimates, analyst Jo Lin recalculated both totals using the same geographic boundary and categories of users. The adjusted estimates then agreed closely.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

A. validate

B. synthesize

C. reconcile

D. extrapolate

Practice this lesson: 1.17, 1.18.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.6

Answer: C

  1. Name the initial problem: the estimates appear inconsistent because their boundaries and included users differ.
  2. Name the operation and result: Lin makes the definitions comparable, after which the estimates agree closely. Predict "bring the apparent disagreement into alignment."
  3. Choose C. "Reconcile" here means make apparently conflicting figures consistent or explain their discrepancy.
  4. Read the complete phrase "reconcile the estimates." It fits both the object and the action; the sentence is not about projecting new values or blending numbers into a third estimate.

Why A fails: "Validate" means establish that something is sound or accurate. Lin makes the estimates comparable, but the passage gives no independent standard that verifies their accuracy. Agreement alone does not verify the estimates' accuracy.

Why B fails: "Synthesize" means combine elements into an integrated whole. Lin recalculates two separate estimates; the text never describes producing one combined estimate from them.

Why D fails: "Extrapolate" means estimate beyond known data by extending a relationship or trend. Lin changes definitions within existing reports, not a projection beyond the observations.

Key takeaway: Ask what changed between the beginning and end. A mismatch becoming agreement calls for a different verb than verification, combination, or projection.

Practice this lesson: 1.17 and 1.18.

1.7 Figurative language and phrase meaning

A phrase can transfer a physical idea to an abstract one: an argument may be threadbare, a promising plan may gain traction, and an old dispute may cast a shadow over a meeting. Convert the figurative expression into a literal statement about its actual subject. What does the phrase say the argument, plan, or dispute is doing? Do not select an answer that merely matches the image's physical details.

Context determines how much of an image carries over. A "thin" explanation may lack substance; a "thin" voice may be faint. The question does not require a sweeping account of the metaphor or a guess about hidden symbolism. Preserve the relevant feature and stop there. For an unfamiliar phrase, inspect the actions or consequences around it. Those details often show whether it expresses persistence, weakness, transition, restraint, or some other relationship. Be especially cautious about adding a cause: figurative wording may describe a condition without explaining why it exists.

W1.7: Translate the metaphor, not its material

At every budget meeting, Hale defended the unused platform by saying that it might someday be useful. Years passed, no use was proposed, and maintenance costs continued to rise. Even neighbors who had once repeated his defense now called the argument Underlined: threadbare End underlined.; hearing it yet again gave them no new reason to preserve the platform.

As used in the text, what does the word "threadbare" most nearly mean?

A. Weakened by repeated use

B. Poorly supported from the outset

C. Too complicated to follow

D. Applicable only in rare cases

Practice this lesson: 1.19, 1.20.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.7

Answer: A

  1. Identify the actual subject: an argument, not a piece of cloth. Its condition must be intellectual or persuasive rather than physical.
  2. Track the timeline: the defense once attracted supporters, but repeated use over years has supplied no new justification.
  3. Choose A. Figuratively, "threadbare" describes something worn out through repeated use; here the familiar argument has lost persuasive force.
  4. Keep the temporal distinction. The passage contrasts earlier acceptance with later dissatisfaction, so an answer claiming that the argument was weak from the beginning adds something unsupported.

Why B fails: This means the argument lacked support when first offered. The text emphasizes its repeated reuse and declining force; it does not establish its original evidential quality.

Why C fails: This means the argument is difficult to understand. Hale's defense is simple and familiar; the neighbors' problem is its exhausted usefulness, not its complexity.

Why D fails: This means the argument has narrow applicability. The text describes how repetition has weakened its force, not a limited category of situations in which it applies.

Key takeaway: A figurative meaning must fit both the subject and the surrounding timeline. Do not carry over irrelevant physical details.

1.8 Connotation and attitude in literary contexts

Words can express the same broad condition with different attitudes. Persistent and obstinate both involve continuing, but obstinate usually adds unreasonable resistance. Economical and stingy both concern restrained spending, but stingy adds criticism of unwillingness to give. A connotation is such an association or evaluative shade. Use the narrator's actual descriptions to decide whether praise, criticism, neutrality, affection, or irony is present.

Separate a character's observable behavior from the narrator's judgment and from your own reaction. Careful work does not automatically imply anxiety, vanity, or perfectionism; silence does not automatically imply hostility. A passage may support a specific attitude while leaving its motive unknown. When close choices describe different personalities, identify the behavior each would require. Then choose the word that describes the behavior actually given. Literary language can be subtle, but a defensible answer still rests on particular words and actions rather than imaginative mind-reading.

W1.8: Exact standards without an invented motive

The portraitist was Underlined: exacting End underlined. about the smallest changes in color. When an apprentice mixed a gray that seemed close enough to everyone else, she set it beside the sitter's sleeve and explained precisely where it differed. She would repeat the demonstration patiently, but she would not accept a merely approximate match.

As used in the text, what does the word "exacting" most nearly mean?

A. Difficult to satisfy through praise

B. Resourceful in solving problems

C. Anxious about making mistakes

D. Demanding a high degree of precision

Practice this lesson: 1.21, 1.22.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.8

Answer: D

  1. Find the behavior that explains the marked word: the portraitist identifies tiny differences and refuses an approximate match.
  2. Separate standards from manner. She is patient while insisting on precision, so impatience or anxiety is not necessary to explain her conduct.
  3. Choose D. "Exacting" means making strict or demanding requirements; here those requirements concern the precision of color matches.
  4. Check that the answer describes what the text establishes rather than why she supposedly acts that way. Her inner motive is not stated.

Why A fails: This describes someone who is not easily pleased by compliments. The passage concerns technical accuracy and contains no praise directed at the portraitist.

Why B fails: This describes an ability to find effective solutions. She provides a demonstration, but the marked word identifies the demanding standard she insists on, not general ingenuity.

Why C fails: This describes worry about errors. Her precision is clear, but the passage gives no evidence of anxiety; patient correction is compatible with confidence.

Key takeaway: Use the text to identify an attitude or standard. Do not convert careful conduct into an unsupported psychological diagnosis.

1.9 Multiple constraints and carefully unpacked negatives

Hard questions often require holding several conditions at once. An endorsement can be strong within a narrow scope; an explanation can be useful without being complete; a person can reject a method without rejecting its goal. Write two or three constraints separately before comparing close words. Otherwise, one vivid phrase may dominate your reading and hide a limiting sentence elsewhere in the passage.

Translate negatives into plain language one clause at a time. "Not inevitable" means not certain to occur, not certain to fail. "Not all" allows some exceptions; it does not mean none. "Not without merit" acknowledges some value without necessarily expressing enthusiasm. Keep track of what the negative modifies. Then reconnect the clauses: a committee may not doubt a result in one group while withholding a claim about another. Avoid mechanically canceling negative-looking words; conventional phrases, quantities, and the scope of each clause determine the meaning.

W1.9: A firm conclusion can still be limited

The committee did not doubt that the training helped the experienced operators studied in the trial. Its members endorsed using the training for that group without hesitation. Their endorsement was nevertheless ______: because the trial included no beginners, the committee explicitly withheld any recommendation for novices.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

A. tentative

B. qualified

C. equivocal

D. grudging

Practice this lesson: 1.23, 1.24.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.9

Answer: B

  1. Translate "did not doubt": the committee is confident about the observed benefit for experienced operators.
  2. Add the scope restriction: the endorsement covers that group and expressly excludes a recommendation for beginners. Confidence is high within a limited scope.
  3. Choose B. "Qualified" means modified or limited by a condition or reservation. The qualification concerns whom the recommendation covers.
  4. Reject the tempting equation "limited equals uncertain." The phrases "without hesitation" and "did not doubt" rule out uncertainty about the supported recommendation.

Why A fails: "Tentative" means provisional or not firmly settled. The committee's endorsement for experienced operators is firm; its scope, rather than its confidence, is limited.

Why C fails: "Equivocal" means ambiguous or open to conflicting interpretations. The committee explicitly separates the two groups, making its recommendation clear.

Why D fails: "Grudging" means given reluctantly or unwillingly. The committee endorses the training for the studied group without hesitation, and no resentment is described.

Key takeaway: Separate certainty from scope. A conclusion can be definite about one group while making no claim about another.

1.10 Learn vocabulary for flexible use

Vocabulary knowledge makes contextual reasoning faster, but a memorized one-word equivalence can be misleading. Record the meaning used in the passage, a common word partner, and a fresh sentence. Add a second sense when it matters: "a novel proposal" and "a historical novel" need different explanations. For close words, write the difference that would change an answer, such as "corroborate: add support" versus "qualify: limit a claim." This creates usable distinctions instead of an undifferentiated synonym list.

Word parts can suggest possibilities, but they cannot override context or established usage. A resemblance to a familiar word may be accidental or misleading: restive does not mean restful. Guess a possible meaning, then test it against the passage just as you would test an answer choice. If several choices are unfamiliar, eliminate only those whose meanings you know conflict with the clues; do not pretend that an unknown word is wrong. After practice, look up a reliable definition, verify the relevant sense, and retrieve it later from a new sentence.

  • Capture: word, relevant sense, original clue, and a natural phrase.
  • Distinguish: name one nearby word that would change the meaning.
  • Retrieve: cover the definition and explain the word from its sentence on a later day.
  • Transfer: write or solve a fresh context that uses the same sense, then test a different sense if the word has one.

W1.10: Context outranks a misleading resemblance

After the final vote, the chair began reviewing points the audience had already heard twice. The listeners grew Underlined: restive End underlined.: they shifted in their seats, checked the clock, and repeatedly reached for bags they had packed when the vote ended. Several asked when they would be allowed to leave.

As used in the text, what does the word "restive" most nearly mean?

A. Weary

B. Dismissive

C. Impatient

D. Apprehensive

Practice this lesson: 1.25, 1.26.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.10

Answer: C

  1. Ignore the tempting resemblance to "rest." Use the behavior after the colon to establish the relevant state.
  2. Combine shifting, clock-checking, packed bags, and requests to leave. The shared pattern is difficulty waiting through an unwanted delay.
  3. Choose C. "Restive" here means restless or impatient under restraint; the listeners want the meeting to end.
  4. Distinguish neighboring attitudes. The passage does not say the listeners are physically exhausted, contemptuous of the discussion, or fearful of what comes next.

Why A fails: "Weary" means tired or worn out. Repetition may bore listeners, but the explicit behavior centers on impatience to leave rather than fatigue.

Why B fails: "Dismissive" means treating something as unworthy of serious consideration. Wanting to leave after the vote does not show that the listeners disregard the issues themselves.

Why D fails: "Apprehensive" means anxious about something that may happen. The passage describes frustration with a delay, not fear of an anticipated event.

Key takeaway: Use roots and resemblance to generate a possibility, never to overrule a clear contextual explanation.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

  • Identify the target's role, then predict a meaning from the full context.
  • Use the exact clue: a restatement, example, contrast, action, or limiting phrase.
  • Compare close choices by their extra claims about degree, scope, certainty, and attitude.
  • Reread the winning meaning in the sentence; explain why the nearest competitor fails.
  • Record new words with their context, common partners, and a fresh example, then retrieve them later.

Begin with six brief reasoning drills, then complete twenty multiple-choice questions. Later items generally require closer distinctions. For every answer, identify the decisive evidence and the flaw in the nearest competitor.

These six written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Separating worsening from initiation | Review lesson 1.5

Answer: "Exacerbated" is better: the rain made an existing problem worse.

"Precipitated" would mean brought about or triggered the problem. The leak existed before the rain, so the stated effect is worsening rather than initiation. The choice depends on the temporal relationship, not on which verb sounds more forceful.

If you missed it: Ask whether the condition began with the event or was already present. Write "began" or "worsened" before selecting the verb.

Paraphrasing a figurative phrase | Review lesson 1.7

Answer: The club relied on the grant as its hoped-for way to obtain new equipment. Approval was not certain.

The phrase identifies the outcome on which the club's hopes depended. The competing applications explicitly preserve uncertainty. Hoping for or relying on a possible outcome does not establish confidence that it will occur.

If you missed it: Keep desire, reliance, and certainty separate. A figurative phrase can express one without implying all three.

Unpacking the scope of a negative | Review lesson 1.9

Answer: The letters have uneven reliability: some kinds of information are often accurate, whereas others are frequently distorted.

"Not uniformly" denies equal reliability across the letters' contents; it does not deny every useful fact. The passage explicitly supplies both a strength and a weakness, and "often" and "frequently" avoid claims about every instance.

If you missed it: Identify the word modified by "not." Preserve both categories and their frequency limits instead of replacing a mixed judgment with an absolute one.
