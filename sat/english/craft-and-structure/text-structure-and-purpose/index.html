Text Structure and Purpose

  • Chapter 2
Topic practiceReview and reference

Explain what a text is doing, how its parts work together, and whose ideas it presents.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish a text's subject and central claim from its rhetorical purpose.
  • Map the sequence of ideas without losing contrasts, qualifications, or shifts.
  • Explain a specific portion's contribution to the surrounding argument or narrative.
  • Choose between close descriptions by checking actions, attribution, scope, and sequence.

2.1 Topic, central idea, and rhetorical purpose

A topic names the subject: rooftop gardens. A central idea states what the text says about that subject: their benefits depend partly on maintenance. A rhetorical purpose describes the text's action: to qualify an optimistic claim about rooftop gardens. All three can be accurate, but they answer different questions. On a purpose question, find the author's main action and connect it to the important content.

Read for the relationship that makes the paragraph worth writing. Does it introduce an overlooked achievement, challenge an explanation, reconcile an apparent conflict, or explain a change? Summarize that action in ordinary language before studying the choices. A prediction is a starting point, not a commitment: revise it if the passage supports a more accurate description.

The main purpose must account for the paragraph as a whole. A correct statement about one sentence may be too narrow. Conversely, a broad claim about the entire subject may exceed the paragraph's task. Prefer the answer that explains why the major parts belong together.

Question

Useful answer form

What is the topic?

The subject or issue being discussed.

What is the central idea?

The principal claim or message.

What is the purpose?

An action the text performs concerning that subject.

W2.1: When a benefit needs a condition

A town promoted its new rain gardens as a way to reduce street flooding. During the first wet season, several gardens absorbed runoff effectively, but gardens with compacted soil overflowed. After workers loosened that soil, the previously overflowing gardens also retained more water. The results suggest that installing a rain garden is only the beginning: maintaining soil that water can enter is part of making the garden useful.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A. To explain why rain gardens are more effective than other ways of reducing street flooding

B. To qualify an expected benefit of rain gardens by identifying a condition that affects their performance

C. To describe how workers determined the original cause of compacted soil in rain gardens

D. To recommend replacing a town's rain gardens after comparing their performance during a wet season

Practice this lesson: 2.07, 2.17.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.1

Answer: B

  1. Identify the starting expectation: new rain gardens should reduce flooding.
  2. Notice the contrast and follow-up: compacted gardens overflowed, then improved when the soil was loosened.
  3. Describe the full rhetorical action: the text makes the promised benefit conditional on suitable maintenance. B captures that relationship.

Why A fails: The paragraph never compares rain gardens with other flood-control methods. The difference is among rain gardens with different soil conditions.

Why C fails: Workers changed the compacted soil, but the text does not investigate how the soil became compacted.

Why D fails: The later improvement supports maintaining the gardens, not replacing them. The choice mistakes a limitation for rejection of the entire approach.

Key takeaway: A purpose answer should state the text's main action, not merely name its subject or one event.

2.2 Map the overall sequence of rhetorical moves

Overall-structure questions ask how the text develops. Make a short sequence of moves, such as observation → possible explanation → evidence → qualified conclusion. Use these labels to describe what actually happens; a paragraph does not need to fit a fixed template. A question followed by an answer can be an explanation, while two answers offered by different sources can form a debate.

Map at the right level. Retelling every detail hides the relationships, but calling every sentence an example is too vague. Try one short label for each important change of role. Then check the proposed order: an answer that names the right moves in the wrong sequence is still wrong.

Watch for revisions within one position. A writer can accept a plan's goal, identify a problem, and modify the plan. That is different from rejecting the goal or comparing two unrelated proposals. Contrast words help locate changes, but only the surrounding ideas establish what changes.

  • A compact map should capture relationships, not list nouns.
  • Check all clauses of a structure answer; one accurate opening does not rescue an inaccurate ending.

W2.2: A proposal is adjusted

A neighborhood council proposed closing a shopping street to cars every Saturday to make walking easier. Shop owners supported the goal but noted that morning deliveries would become difficult. The council then proposed allowing delivery vehicles until 10 a.m. and closing the street afterward. This arrangement would preserve access for suppliers while giving pedestrians most of the day without car traffic.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

A. It contrasts two groups' goals and explains why neither can be achieved.

B. It describes a completed experiment and identifies a cause of its unexpected results.

C. It presents a proposal, identifies a practical concern, and explains a modification intended to address that concern.

D. It introduces two independently developed proposals and chooses one on the basis of its lower cost.

Practice this lesson: 2.08, 2.18.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.2

Answer: C

  1. Label the opening as a proposal: close the street on Saturdays.
  2. Label the shop owners' contribution as a practical concern within shared support for the goal.
  3. The revised schedule responds directly to that concern. C preserves both the sequence and the relationship.

Why A fails: The groups share the pedestrian-access goal, and the revision is designed to accommodate deliveries. The passage does not declare either goal impossible.

Why B fails: The schedule remains a proposal. No completed experiment or measured outcome appears.

Why D fails: The second arrangement modifies the first in response to a concern; it is not independently developed. No cost comparison is given.

Key takeaway: Map the passage's actual sequence, including whether a later idea replaces, modifies, or merely accompanies an earlier one.

Practice this lesson: 2.08 and 2.18.

2.3 Explain a focal portion through its connection to the whole

A function question isolates a sentence or underlined portion, but its answer usually depends on the surrounding text. Read the focal portion for literal meaning, then ask what it contributes to the nearby claim. Does it provide an instance, introduce a difficulty, explain a mechanism, rule out an interpretation, or establish a contrast?

Use a two-part explanation: the portion says X, and that matters because it does Y for the text. The first half is a paraphrase; the second half supplies the rhetorical function. For example, saying that a sentence reports a delayed train does not explain whether that delay illustrates a recurring problem or contradicts a claim of perfect reliability.

The removal test can help: if the portion vanished, what support, transition, restriction, or comparison would the passage lose? Treat this as a reasoning aid. The SAT asks for the function the words actually perform, not a guess about an author's private intentions.

W2.3: A limiting case

Some commentators describe electric bicycles as a complete solution to the steep hills that discourage cycling. Motors do make many climbs easier. Underlined: Riders carrying heavy cargo, however, can still struggle on the longest slopes. End underlined. Electric assistance therefore removes an important obstacle for many riders without eliminating that obstacle in every situation.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

A. It supplies a limiting case that helps the text qualify an otherwise useful benefit.

B. It identifies the reason electric bicycles are generally less suitable than conventional bicycles for carrying cargo.

C. It presents the main evidence that steep hills no longer discourage most people from cycling.

D. It explains the mechanical process by which electric motors make climbing hills easier.

Practice this lesson: 2.09, 2.19.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.3

Answer: A

  1. Read the broad claim: a complete solution.
  2. Connect the underlined sentence to that claim: one situation still creates difficulty.
  3. The conclusion keeps the benefit but limits its coverage. A describes the sentence's contribution to that qualification.

Why B fails: No comparison with conventional bicycles is made. Cargo is a condition under which a difficulty persists, not evidence of comparative unsuitability.

Why C fails: The sentence gives a remaining obstacle, not evidence that hills have ceased to discourage most people.

Why D fails: The text mentions electric assistance but explains no mechanical process. The focal sentence describes a limitation in use.

Key takeaway: A function answer connects the focal portion to another part of the text instead of merely restating its content.

2.4 Distinguish examples, evidence, context, and explanation

An example makes a general point concrete. Evidence gives a reason to accept a claim. Background prepares a reader to understand an issue. An explanation shows how or why something happens. One sentence can do more than one of these things, so classify it by its actual contribution in context rather than by a single keyword.

A date is not automatically background; it might establish that an event occurred before a supposed cause. A quotation is not automatically support for the author; it might state the view being challenged. Likewise, one vivid example can illustrate a pattern without establishing that the pattern holds everywhere.

When answers compete, ask what conclusion the passage uses the detail to support. Check the scale of that conclusion. Evidence from one case may support the possibility of an outcome, while an answer claiming proof of its universal prevalence adds an unsupported leap. Use the scope that the passage supplies.

W2.4: An example of a general distinction

Instructions can describe the same action from different users' perspectives. A sewing guide written for beginners might say, "Fold the fabric so that the printed sides face each other." Underlined: A guide for experienced sewers might compress that instruction to "Place right sides together." End underlined. The action has not changed; the amount of shared terminology the writer can assume has.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence?

A. It offers evidence that experienced sewers generally prefer shorter projects to longer ones.

B. It explains why a particular sewing technique produces a stronger finished seam.

C. It introduces an exception to the text's claim that all instructions require the same wording.

D. It provides a contrasting example that illustrates how assumed reader knowledge can affect an instruction's wording.

Practice this lesson: 2.10, 2.20.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.4

Answer: D

  1. Identify the opening generalization: instructions reflect users' perspectives.
  2. Compare the two instructions: they direct the same action but assume different familiarity with terminology.
  3. The underlined version completes that comparison. D identifies both the example and the broader point it illustrates.

Why A fails: A shorter instruction is not a shorter project. The text concerns wording and knowledge, not project preferences.

Why B fails: The sentence provides an alternative wording, not a mechanical explanation of seam strength.

Why C fails: The opening says wording can vary, not that it must remain the same. The example illustrates the opening claim instead of creating an exception to it.

Key takeaway: Identify both what a detail presents and which larger point that detail helps develop.

2.5 Separate concession, qualification, and refutation

A concession acknowledges a valid point that does not settle the whole issue. A qualification narrows a claim's scope or strength. A refutation gives a reason to reject a claim. These actions can occur together, but they are not interchangeable. "The method is costly, but its accuracy may justify the expense" concedes a drawback. "It works only indoors" qualifies its range. "The supposed improvement disappears when errors are corrected" challenges the claimed improvement itself.

To determine which action occurs, name the exact proposition under discussion. An author may reject "always effective" while continuing to endorse "often useful." Describing that as a total rejection loses the important distinction. Similarly, acknowledging a criticism does not mean adopting the critic's final conclusion.

Follow the text through its response. A sentence beginning with "although" often signals a concession, but the clause's role depends on what the writer ultimately argues. Check what is accepted, what is limited, and what is rejected.

Move

What changes

Concession

A relevant point is accepted, often before a response.

Qualification

A claim's range or degree becomes more limited.

Refutation

A reason is given to reject a specific claim.

W2.5: A drawback that remains real

Some schools avoid outdoor classes because street noise can interrupt instruction. That concern is reasonable: a passing truck can make a teacher difficult to hear. Yet short outdoor observation sessions need not rely on continuous spoken explanation. If instructions are given beforehand and observations are recorded individually, such sessions can retain educational value even where occasional noise makes a full outdoor lecture impractical.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A. To dismiss a practical objection to outdoor classes by showing that its factual basis is mistaken

B. To acknowledge a practical objection to outdoor classes while explaining why some forms of outdoor instruction may remain useful

C. To compare two objections to outdoor classes and establish which deserves more attention

D. To endorse a general objection to outdoor classes and show why proposed exceptions cannot address it

Practice this lesson: 2.11, 2.21.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.5

Answer: B

  1. The truck example accepts that noise can interrupt speech; the concern is not dismissed as imaginary.
  2. The response changes the instructional format from a continuous lecture to short observation sessions.
  3. B captures both the concession and the narrower claim of usefulness.

Why A fails: The noise problem remains real. The text responds by identifying activities less dependent on continuous speech, not by disproving the concern.

Why C fails: The text addresses one concern about noise. It compares instructional formats, not two distinct concerns.

Why D fails: The proposed observation sessions are presented as useful under specified conditions. They are not rejected as ineffective exceptions.

Key takeaway: Ask which part of the original objection survives after the writer's response.

Practice this lesson: 2.11 and 2.21.

2.6 Track the author and every quoted or reported view

A passage may contain several voices: the writer, a researcher, an earlier critic, and a contemporary commentator. Do not merge them into one position. Attach a name or role to each claim, then note how the writer treats it: reports, endorses, questions, or rejects. Reporting a claim does not automatically endorse it.

Attribution words matter. "Proposed" identifies a suggestion; "demonstrated" describes a claimed evidentiary success; "assumed" can mark a starting belief whose status must be checked in context. Contrast may occur between sources or between an earlier view and the author's response. Determine which before choosing a description of the disagreement.

For a quoted portion, distinguish the quotation's content from the writer's reason for including it. A quotation that sounds confident may be presented precisely because later evidence exposes its limitations. Read beyond the quoted voice to find the text's own direction.

W2.6: Confidence presented for revision

Early reviewers of a farming cooperative called its flexible work schedule "an arrangement certain to reduce output." They assumed that allowing members to choose work hours would leave crucial tasks unattended. Later records show that members coordinated their choices and maintained the cooperative's previous output. The reviewers correctly noticed that scheduling mattered, but their prediction overlooked the members' ability to organize the work themselves.

Which choice best describes the role of the quotation in the text?

A. It states a judgment that the text later treats as broadly confirmed by the records.

B. It expresses the writer's principal criticism of the cooperative's members.

C. It presents an earlier prediction that the text evaluates and finds based on an incomplete assumption.

D. It summarizes a disagreement among cooperative members that the records leave unresolved.

Practice this lesson: 2.12, 2.22.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.6

Answer: C

  1. Assign the quotation to the early reviewers, not the writer or members.
  2. Read the later evidence: output was maintained through coordination, contrary to the prediction of reduced output.
  3. The ending identifies the missed possibility. C accurately describes the writer's use of the quotation.

Why A fails: The records contradict the predicted reduction in output. Recognizing that scheduling mattered does not confirm the forecast.

Why B fails: The quotation belongs to the reviewers, and the writer emphasizes the members' successful coordination rather than criticizing them.

Why D fails: No disagreement among members is described. The conflict is between an earlier prediction and later records.

Key takeaway: A source's statement is not necessarily the passage's conclusion; check the author's treatment of it.

2.7 Read scientific structure as a chain of questions and tests

Scientific passages often move among an observation, a puzzle, possible explanations, a method, a result, and a limitation. Separate these roles even if the subject is unfamiliar. You can recognize "a test designed to distinguish two explanations" without knowing the technical vocabulary for the process being tested.

Methods describe what researchers do; results describe what they find. A prediction describes what should happen if an explanation is correct; a conclusion evaluates that explanation after considering evidence. Confusing these roles produces answers that sound scientific but misdescribe the paragraph. Pay attention to time and conditional language.

For an experimental detail, ask which comparison it makes possible. Holding temperature constant may help separate the influence of light from temperature; it does not by itself establish that light matters. Also preserve uncertainty. A method can investigate a mechanism, and a result can favor one explanation, without proving that every alternative has been eliminated.

W2.7: Why the test separates two conditions

Seedlings beneath a transparent shelter grew faster than uncovered seedlings. The shelter both retained warmth and reduced the wind reaching the plants, so either change might explain the difference. Underlined: To separate these possibilities, researchers planned a second comparison using shelters that blocked wind but allowed the enclosed air to remain at the outside temperature. End underlined. Growth under those conditions would help them assess whether reduced wind alone could account for the original advantage.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence?

A. It reports a result demonstrating that temperature had no effect on seedling growth.

B. It explains why the original observation cannot be measured accurately.

C. It rejects the possibility that reduced wind contributed to the original growth difference.

D. It describes a proposed test intended to distinguish between explanations of an observed difference.

Practice this lesson: 2.13, 2.23.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.7

Answer: D

  1. The original shelters changed two conditions at once, creating competing explanations.
  2. The focal sentence describes a planned comparison that changes wind exposure while preserving outside temperature.
  3. This is a method, not a reported result. D states its purpose without claiming the experiment has already settled the question.

Why A fails: The comparison is planned; no new result is reported. Even the proposed test is framed as assessing an explanation, not proving that temperature never matters.

Why B fails: The observed difference is treated as usable evidence. The difficulty concerns its explanation, not whether growth can be measured.

Why C fails: Reduced wind remains a live possibility that the test is designed to investigate. The sentence does not reject it.

Key takeaway: In science passages, distinguish what was observed, what might explain it, what will be tested, and what the evidence supports.

Practice this lesson: 2.13 and 2.23.

2.8 Follow narrative and poetic shifts without inventing motives

Literary texts can describe a place, reveal a character's attitude, develop a contrast, or trace a change in perception. Begin with concrete textual evidence: actions, selected details, comparisons, and changes in what the narrator notices. Then describe the supported function. You do not need a complicated psychological explanation to identify a shift from impatience to attention.

Distinguish a character's feelings from the narrator's presentation and from a claim about the real writer. First-person narration does not make a fictional speaker identical to the author. Similarly, one character's accusation does not establish that the narrator endorses it. Attribute judgments as carefully as you would in a research passage.

For an image or detail, ask what it contributes here. The same empty chair might mark absence, preserve a memory, or create space for a visitor. Let nearby language constrain the interpretation. A plausible symbol from outside the passage is weaker than an explanation anchored in the actual scene.

W2.8: Attention changes the scene

At first, Lena counted the minutes until her grandfather finished repairing the kite. His hands moved so slowly that she looked toward the field instead. Then she noticed how he turned a splintered piece of wood until its curve matched the frame. She pulled her chair closer. When he reached for another strip, she was already holding it out.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A. To depict a character's growing engagement in an activity through changes in what she notices and does

B. To explain how an earlier disagreement about a shared activity is eventually resolved

C. To compare two characters' methods and establish that the younger character's method is more effective

D. To show a character's initial confidence being undermined by recognition of a mistake

Practice this lesson: 2.14, 2.24.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.8

Answer: A

  1. The opening establishes impatience through counting minutes and looking away.
  2. The pivot is a new observation: Lena notices the careful adjustment of the wood.
  3. Moving closer and offering a strip reveal engagement without explicitly naming it. A traces that supported change.

Why B fails: No disagreement or resolution of a dispute appears. The central change is in Lena's engagement as the repair proceeds.

Why C fails: Only the grandfather's repair technique is described. Lena assists, but the passage does not compare two methods.

Why D fails: The opening shows impatience, not confidence, and no mistake causes the change. The ending shows participation.

Key takeaway: Use a character's specific actions to identify a supported change; do not add an unwritten motive.

2.9 Explain the work done by comparisons, questions, and imagery

Knowing a device's name is less useful than explaining its job. An analogy can clarify an unfamiliar process, make a comparison vivid, or expose a weakness in a claim. A rhetorical question can raise an objection, emphasize an implication, or lead into a proposed answer. Identify its contribution from the surrounding argument, not from the device alone.

For a comparison, identify the shared feature that matters. A writer who compares revising a map to adjusting a recipe may be emphasizing repeated correction, not claiming that geography resembles cooking in every respect. An answer that extends the analogy beyond that shared feature can sound imaginative while missing the text.

A rhetorical question need not express uncertainty: its expected answer may reinforce a position. But some written questions are genuine research questions. Check whether the passage is opening an unresolved investigation or making a point whose answer is supplied or strongly implied. The distinction depends on use, not punctuation.

W2.9: A question that presses an objection

A committee suggested judging community plays solely by ticket sales. Yet a production staged in a room with sixty seats cannot sell as many tickets in one evening as one staged in a hall with six hundred. Underlined: Would filling every seat in the smaller room really count as less success than leaving half the larger hall empty? End underlined. Attendance figures need context if they are to support a fair comparison.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined question?

A. It requests information needed to calculate the revenue earned by the two productions.

B. It highlights a consequence of the proposed measure that supports the text's criticism of that measure.

C. It introduces a new topic that the text leaves for a later discussion.

D. It implies that productions in smaller rooms invariably have greater artistic value.

Practice this lesson: 2.15, 2.25.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.9

Answer: B

  1. Identify the proposed rule: use ticket totals alone.
  2. Apply it to the question's comparison: sixty occupied seats would rank below three hundred, even though the smaller room is full.
  3. The question makes the limitation vivid and prepares the call for context. B captures that argumentative work.

Why A fails: No ticket prices are supplied or requested, and the issue is evaluating success rather than calculating revenue.

Why C fails: The question continues the existing concern about ticket totals; it does not change the topic.

Why D fails: The text challenges a sole measure of success without claiming that room size determines artistic value.

Key takeaway: Describe the point a device advances; identifying the device or overextending its implication is not enough.

2.10 Choose precise rhetorical verbs and verify every part

Close choices often share the same topic but use different rhetorical verbs. "Introduces" need not mean "endorses." "Illustrates" need not mean "establishes as universal." "Questions" need not mean "disproves." "Reconciles" means showing how seemingly conflicting claims can fit together, whereas "compares" may simply identify their similarities and differences. Choose the verb that matches the relationship and degree of commitment in the text.

Divide long answers into claims. Check the action, the object of that action, the attribution, and the order. An answer can begin accurately with "describes a common practice" and then fail by adding "explains why it should be abandoned" when the text merely recommends one adjustment. Do not award partial credit to a multiple-choice option.

Finish by testing the closest rival. Identify the exact extra claim it requires or the part of the passage it cannot explain. This is more reliable than deciding which answer sounds sophisticated. On harder items, a single verb or sequencing phrase often creates the decisive difference.

Verb

Evidence the text must supply

Illustrates

A particular case makes a broader point concrete.

Qualifies

A claim is restricted in scope or degree.

Challenges

A reason casts doubt on a specific claim.

Reconciles

An explanation shows how apparent conflict can be resolved.

Distinguishes

A relevant difference between ideas or cases is identified.

W2.10: Similar goals, different meanings

Two designers both recommend leaving some areas of a public square unplanned. For Mara, unplanned space allows users to create activities the designer did not anticipate. For Dev, it leaves room for officials to add facilities as neighborhood needs change. Their recommendations therefore resemble each other physically while assigning future decisions to different people.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

A. It presents a shared recommendation and then argues that its supporters have identical reasons for making it.

B. It introduces two design proposals and uses evidence from completed squares to rank their effectiveness.

C. It identifies a shared recommendation, distinguishes the reasoning behind it, and draws a contrast about who would shape the space later.

D. It describes a dispute about a square and then resolves the dispute by proposing a compromise.

Practice this lesson: 2.16, 2.26.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.10

Answer: C

  1. The opening establishes agreement about leaving space unplanned.
  2. The middle assigns different reasons to each designer: users' initiatives versus officials' later additions.
  3. The ending interprets that difference as one about decision-makers. C preserves the shared recommendation and the distinction.

Why A fails: The passage explicitly distinguishes the reasons and the people who would make future decisions.

Why B fails: No completed-square evidence or effectiveness ranking is offered. The comparison concerns reasoning behind a similar recommendation.

Why D fails: The passage clarifies a difference but proposes no compromise and does not describe an effort to resolve a dispute.

Key takeaway: Check every verb and every stage of a structure answer; one accurate phrase cannot make the entire choice correct.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

  • Name the task: whole-text purpose, overall structure, or one portion's function.
  • Map relationships with plain verbs: presents, illustrates, tests, qualifies, challenges, or revises.
  • For a focal portion, connect its content to the surrounding claim; do not stop at paraphrase.
  • Track whose view each statement expresses and whether the author endorses, reports, or challenges it.
  • Check every part of the selected answer, including its sequence, rhetorical verb, and implied degree of support.

Begin with six brief reasoning drills, then complete twenty multiple-choice questions. Later items generally require closer distinctions. For every answer, identify the decisive evidence and the flaw in the nearest competitor.

These six written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.
