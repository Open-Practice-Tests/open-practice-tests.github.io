A contextual vocabulary reference

These 60 academic words are a starting reference, not a predicted test list or a claim about official word frequency. Each entry teaches one useful sense; a word may have other meanings. On the test, the passage determines which sense fits.

Use the examples to learn relationships, not just substitutions. Notice what kind of subject or object a word takes, how strong its meaning is, and what the surrounding sentence supplies as evidence. The distinctions flag common confusions; they do not make the paired words opposites in every context.

Use the reference actively

  • Retrieve before rereading. Cover the meaning and distinction, then explain the word's sense from its example. Check both the core meaning and the strength of the claim.
  • Build a contrast. Write a fresh sentence in which this word fits but its comparison word would change the meaning. Explain the change in one sentence.
  • Collect another sense when you encounter one. Record the complete source sentence and verify the sense in a reputable dictionary; do not force the reference meaning into every context.
  • Review after a day, several days, and a week. Mix groups and retest missed distinctions. Once recall is reliable, practice choosing meanings in unfamiliar passages rather than reciting isolated definitions.

The entries teach selected senses, not every meaning of each word. The contrasts are starting points: always test a word against the particular sentence you are reading.

Claims and evidence

  • substantiate verb - To establish support for a claim by supplying relevant evidence. The team substantiated its claim of faster delivery by comparing dated shipping records. Contrast: Substantiate supplies evidence; merely reiterating a claim repeats it without adding support.

  • corroborate verb - To support an account or conclusion with additional confirming evidence. Three independently recorded tide measurements corroborated the observer's report of an unusually high surge. Contrast: Corroborate adds support to an account; it does not necessarily prove it.

  • infer verb - To reach a conclusion by reasoning from evidence or premises. From the wet umbrellas beside the door, Lila inferred that rain had recently fallen. Contrast: A reader infers a conclusion; a writer may imply it without stating it.

  • posit verb - To put forward an idea as a basis for reasoning. The model posits a shared ancestor to explain features that several species have in common. Contrast: Positing proposes an explanatory starting point; demonstrating establishes something through evidence or reasoning.

  • contend verb - To argue that a particular claim or position is true. The essay contends that public seating strengthens neighborhoods by giving residents places to gather. Contrast: Contend here means argue a position; contend with means struggle against something.

  • assert verb - To state something firmly as true, with or without supporting evidence. The supplier asserted that the fabric resisted fading but provided no results from durability tests. Contrast: Assert emphasizes stating a position; the word itself does not imply proof.

  • refute verb - To show through evidence or reasoning that a claim is false. A dated photograph of the finished bridge refuted the claim that construction began later. Contrast: Refute shows a claim is false; dispute can mean simply challenging it.

  • account for verb phrase - To provide an explanation for a fact, event, or pattern. Longer delivery routes could account for the additional fuel used after the warehouse moved. Contrast: Account for explains here; elsewhere it can mean constitute a specified proportion.

  • warrant verb - To provide sufficient grounds to justify an action or conclusion. The repeated equipment failures warranted a full inspection before the factory resumed its operations. Contrast: Warrant means justify here; it does not mean guarantee a particular outcome.

  • empirical adjective - Based on observation or experiment rather than on reasoning alone. The team sought empirical evidence by measuring seed growth instead of relying solely on its model. Contrast: Empirical describes the basis of evidence; it does not automatically mean conclusive.

Precision, scope, and certainty

  • qualify verb - To limit a statement by specifying relevant conditions or exceptions. The researcher qualified her recommendation by noting that the treatment had only been tested indoors. Contrast: Qualify limits a claim here; it does not mean meet eligibility requirements.

  • tentative adjective - Expressed or held with uncertainty rather than with firm commitment. With only two samples available, the team offered a tentative explanation and requested further measurements. Contrast: Tentative emphasizes uncertainty or hesitation; provisional emphasizes acceptance subject to later revision.

  • provisional adjective - Accepted or used for the present, but subject to revision. The provisional schedule assigned each speaker a time, with final arrangements awaiting confirmation of travel plans. Contrast: Provisional means currently adopted but revisable; it need not express weak confidence.

  • plausible adjective - Reasonable enough to be believable, though not necessarily established as true. A loose connection was a plausible explanation for the flickering lamp, but inspection was still needed. Contrast: Plausible means believable; probable makes the stronger claim that something is likely.

  • unequivocal adjective - Clear and definite, allowing no reasonable doubt about the intended meaning. The committee's unequivocal rejection left no room to interpret its decision as a request for revisions. Contrast: Unequivocal describes clarity and definiteness; an unequivocal assertion can still be false.

  • contingent adjective - Dependent on a particular event or condition for its occurrence. Approval of the outdoor concert was contingent on organizers arranging a safe evacuation route. Contrast: Contingent on means dependent on; it does not mean merely associated with.

  • constrained adjective - Limited in freedom, range, or scope by particular restricting conditions. With only one vehicle available, the team's sampling area was constrained to nearby streams. Contrast: Constrained means restricted; restricted activity can continue rather than being completely prevented.

  • negligible adjective - So small in amount or effect that it can reasonably be disregarded. Adding one paper label made a negligible difference to the weight of the loaded crate. Contrast: Negligible means too small to matter in context; it does not mean nonexistent.

  • salient adjective - Especially noticeable or important among the features of a situation. The report's most salient finding was the sharp decline in attendance after evening service ended. Contrast: Salient marks prominence or importance; it does not necessarily mean surprising or unusual.

  • exhaustive adjective - Covering all the relevant cases, details, or possibilities within a scope. The catalogue offered an exhaustive inventory of the collection, listing every item and its condition. Contrast: Exhaustive means comprehensive; exhausting describes something that makes a person very tired.

Change and causation

  • engender verb - To bring a feeling, condition, or situation into existence. Repeatedly changing the rules without explanation engendered mistrust among players who wanted a predictable competition. Contrast: Engender creates or gives rise to something; intensify strengthens something already present.

  • precipitate verb - To cause an event to happen, often suddenly or sooner than expected. The unexpected cancellation of funding precipitated the project's closure months before its planned end. Contrast: Precipitate stresses triggering an event; facilitate makes an activity easier to carry out.

  • mitigate verb - To make something harmful, severe, or unpleasant less serious. Shade structures mitigated the afternoon heat on the playground, although the air remained warm. Contrast: Mitigate reduces severity; eliminate removes the problem entirely rather than easing it.

  • exacerbate verb - To make an existing problem or harmful condition more severe. The broken ventilation system exacerbated the room's existing stuffiness as more visitors crowded inside. Contrast: Exacerbate worsens a problem; instigate emphasizes initiating an action or course of events.

  • impede verb - To obstruct or slow the movement, progress, or development of something. Fallen branches impeded the hikers' progress, forcing them to crawl slowly beneath several obstacles. Contrast: Impede hinders progress; prevent can mean stopping the activity from occurring altogether.

  • facilitate verb - To make an action or process easier to carry out. A shared index facilitated the researchers' search by showing which boxes contained the relevant letters. Contrast: Facilitate makes a process easier; it does not guarantee that it succeeds.

  • attenuate verb - To reduce the strength, force, or intensity of something. The thick curtains attenuated street noise, making passing buses sound faint inside the apartment. Contrast: Attenuate emphasizes weakening; mitigate specifically emphasizes reducing harm, severity, or unpleasantness.

  • augment verb - To increase something in size, amount, or extent by adding to it. The small choir augmented its sound by adding three singers for the final performance. Contrast: Augment adds to what exists; replace substitutes something else for the original.

  • supplant verb - To take the place of something previously established or used. An electronic booking system supplanted the paper ledger, which the innkeeper no longer used. Contrast: Supplant replaces what came before; supplement adds something while retaining the original.

  • sustain verb - To maintain something or keep it continuing over time. Regular donations sustained the free workshop for years after its initial grant had been spent. Contrast: Sustain means maintain here; in sustain an injury, it means suffer or experience.

Relationships and structure

  • coherent adjective - Logically connected and consistent so that the whole makes sense. By connecting each result to her central claim, the writer turned scattered observations into a coherent argument. Contrast: Coherent concerns sensible connections; an argument can be coherent without being factually correct.

  • disparate adjective - Markedly different in kind and not easily treated as alike. The festival brought together disparate art forms, from miniature sculpture to improvised dance and electronic music. Contrast: Disparate means different in kind; dispersed means spread across an area.

  • analogous adjective - Similar in a particular respect that permits a useful comparison. The editor's coordinating role was analogous to a conductor's: each guided specialists toward a unified result. Contrast: Analogous supports a comparison in relevant respects; it does not mean identical.

  • complementary adjective - Working together so that each supplies something the other lacks. Their skills were complementary: Ana designed the instruments, while Ravi developed methods for interpreting their measurements. Contrast: Complementary means mutually completing or enhancing; complimentary can mean praising or provided free.

  • incongruous adjective - Not fitting naturally with the surrounding setting or other elements. The flashing neon sign seemed incongruous among the quiet gallery's plain walls and muted paintings. Contrast: Incongruous means out of place; contradictory means logically inconsistent with another statement.

  • reconcile verb - To make apparently conflicting accounts, ideas, or findings fit together. The analyst reconciled the conflicting totals: one survey counted households, while the other counted individuals. Contrast: Reconcile resolves an apparent conflict; refute shows that a claim is false.

  • synthesize verb - To combine separate ideas or information into an integrated whole. The essay synthesizes interviews and census records to develop one account of the neighborhood's transformation. Contrast: Synthesize integrates material into a whole; summarize chiefly condenses its main points.

  • subordinate adjective - Lower in importance or position relative to something else. In the revised proposal, decorative features became subordinate to the main goal of improving wheelchair access. Contrast: Subordinate means secondary in rank or priority; it does not mean unnecessary.

  • integral adjective - Necessary as a part of the functioning or completeness of a whole. Audience participation was integral to the performance; without it, the actors could not complete the final scene. Contrast: Integral means essential to the whole; incidental describes something secondary or accompanying.

  • delineate verb - To describe or mark something clearly and with precise boundaries. The agreement delineates each partner's responsibilities, specifying who orders materials, hires workers, and approves payments. Contrast: Delineate makes features or limits clear; mention can merely refer to them.

Response and attitude

  • ambivalent adjective - Having conflicting feelings or attitudes toward the same person or thing. Maya was ambivalent about moving: the new job excited her, but leaving her community saddened her. Contrast: Ambivalent describes conflicting attitudes; indifferent describes a lack of interest or concern.

  • skeptical adjective - Doubtful about a claim or unwilling to accept it without examination. Skeptical of the claimed savings, the treasurer requested receipts before accepting the contractor's estimate. Contrast: Skeptical means questioning or doubtful; it need not imply a final rejection.

  • receptive adjective - Willing to listen to and consider new ideas or suggestions. The director was receptive to revisions, inviting the cast to suggest changes before rehearsals began. Contrast: Receptive means open to considering an idea; convinced means persuaded that it is sound.

  • disinterested adjective - Free from personal stakes or advantage in judging a matter. The dispute went to a disinterested reviewer who had no financial or personal ties to either company. Contrast: In this sense, disinterested means impartial; uninterested means lacking curiosity or concern.

  • impartial adjective - Treating competing people or positions fairly without favoring one side. The impartial moderator gave both candidates equal time and applied the same speaking rules to each. Contrast: Impartial concerns fair judgment; neutral can also mean taking no position at all.

  • deferential adjective - Showing respectful regard for another person's authority, wishes, or judgment. In a deferential reply, the apprentice acknowledged the master carpenter's experience before offering a different approach. Contrast: Deferential shows respect for another's standing; it need not mean agreeing with every claim.

  • dismissive adjective - Treating something as unworthy of serious attention or consideration. The review was dismissive, calling the proposal a passing fad without addressing any of its evidence. Contrast: Dismissive brushes something aside; critical can involve careful examination of its merits.

  • pragmatic adjective - Focused on workable actions and practical consequences in actual conditions. Her pragmatic solution used available scrap wood to repair the stage before that evening's performance. Contrast: Pragmatic emphasizes what works in practice; it does not necessarily mean unprincipled.

  • circumspect adjective - Careful to consider possible risks or consequences before acting or speaking. The circumspect negotiator checked each clause for unintended obligations before recommending that the union sign. Contrast: Circumspect emphasizes careful judgment; timid emphasizes a lack of courage or confidence.

  • concede verb - To acknowledge a point as true, often despite an opposing position. Although she opposed the expansion, Jo conceded that the current building lacked enough rehearsal space. Contrast: Concede accepts a point; it need not mean abandoning the larger argument.

Prevalence and style

  • pervasive adjective - Spreading widely through many parts or aspects of something. References to water were pervasive in the collection, appearing in nearly every poem and illustration. Contrast: Pervasive emphasizes extensive spread through something; prevalent emphasizes being common or widespread.

  • sporadic adjective - Occurring occasionally and irregularly rather than continuously or at predictable intervals. Attendance was sporadic: some weeks Eli came twice, while other weeks he missed every session. Contrast: Sporadic emphasizes irregular occurrence; periodic can describe recurrence at regular intervals.

  • prevalent adjective - Common or widespread in a particular place or period. Clay roof tiles were prevalent in the town; most houses used them instead of wooden shingles. Contrast: Prevalent means widespread; universal would extend the claim to every relevant case.

  • conventional adjective - Following practices, forms, or ideas that are generally accepted or established. Unlike the experimental plays nearby, this production followed the conventional sequence of exposition, conflict, and resolution. Contrast: Conventional follows established practice; it does not necessarily mean ineffective or inferior.

  • novel adjective - New or unusual in comparison with what is already known. The team proposed a novel fastening method that differed from every design in its existing catalogue. Contrast: Novel means new here; the adjective does not imply usefulness or superiority.

  • idiosyncratic adjective - Distinctive of an individual rather than typical of a larger group. The pianist's idiosyncratic pauses made her interpretation recognizable even when listeners could not see the performer. Contrast: Idiosyncratic emphasizes individual peculiarity; unusual need not be distinctive of any particular person.

  • austere adjective - Plain and restrained in appearance, with little decoration or luxury. The room's austere design consisted of bare walls, a narrow table, and two unpadded chairs. Contrast: Austere emphasizes restraint or severity; simple need not suggest severity or discomfort.

  • ornate adjective - Elaborately decorated or expressed with a great deal of embellishment. Carved vines, gilded birds, and tiny painted flowers covered the ornate frame surrounding the portrait. Contrast: Ornate emphasizes elaborate decoration; elegant emphasizes grace or refinement, with or without ornament.

  • terse adjective - Using very few words, often with an abrupt or curt effect. His terse reply, 'Send the receipt,' supplied the necessary instruction but none of the expected pleasantries. Contrast: Terse can suggest abruptness; concise usually emphasizes efficient expression without unnecessary words.

  • lucid adjective - Clearly expressed and easy to understand despite the subject's possible complexity. Her lucid explanation guided beginners through the complicated process with clear definitions and a simple example. Contrast: Lucid concerns clarity; brief concerns length, so a long explanation can be lucid.
