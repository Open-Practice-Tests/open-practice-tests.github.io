Developing a point

These are common functions and representative expressions, not a rule that every word in a row works everywhere. Always test the exact relationship and the fixed syntax of the sentence.

Function

Common expressions

What the connection must do

Addition

Additionally; moreover; furthermore

Add another relevant point. Mere shared subject matter does not establish the particular relationship.

Similarity

Similarly; likewise

Present a meaningful parallel, such as the same pattern in a second setting. The compared objects need not be identical.

Example

For example; for instance

Provide a particular case that actually illustrates the preceding general idea.

Specification

Specifically; in particular

Narrow attention to a detail or a member of a broader set. This function can overlap with exemplification.

Restatement

In other words; that is

Express substantially the same idea in a different or more explicit form. A new consequence is not merely a restatement.

Confirmation or emphasis

Indeed; in fact

Confirm, sharpen, or sometimes correct a preceding idea. Read the use in context: in fact does not always signal agreement.

Know what the second statement contributes

Pair of propositions

Useful distinction

General claim: some seeds travel on animals. Particular case: hooked seeds catch on fur.

The second statement illustrates a broader claim. It is not the physical result of stating that claim.

One method measures surface heat. A second method measures heat below the surface.

Two methods are mentioned, but their shared topic alone does not decide whether the writer is adding, comparing, or contrasting. Read the framing.

The site is inaccessible by car: no automobile can reach it.

The second description clarifies the first. A transition implying an independent new point would misread the relationship.

A repeated word can help track reference, but it cannot prove a rhetorical relationship. Paraphrase the claims rather than matching vocabulary.

Changing direction or drawing a result

Function

Common expressions

What to verify

Contrast

However; in contrast; by contrast; on the other hand

A relevant difference or opposition. A change of object alone is not enough.

Concession

Nevertheless; nonetheless; even so; still

The next statement holds despite a fact that might lead readers to expect otherwise.

Replacement or correction

Instead; rather; on the contrary

A choice, action, or interpretation replaces or corrects another. These expressions have different uses; do not treat all as generic however.

Result or inference

Therefore; thus; consequently; as a result

The next claim follows as a consequence or justified conclusion. A physical cause and a reason for believing something are different relationships.

Earlier or later time

Previously; subsequently; later

The order is supported by the text. Time order alone does not prove causation.

Simultaneous events

Meanwhile; at the same time

The relevant activities overlap in time. A contrast between them may also matter.

Summary or overall view

In sum; overall; taken together

The sentence consolidates preceding material at the appropriate scope. It should not be labeled a summary merely because it is last.

Check the direction of the reasoning

The valve froze; the water could not pass through it. The first event can explain the second. Reversing the sentences changes what a result marker would claim. A sentence reporting evidence can also lead to an inference without describing a physical cause: the question is what follows from what.

A transition can refer beyond its immediate neighbor

When an opening claim is followed by an example, the next sentence may respond to the claim, the example, or both. Read through any qualification or embedded description to recover the central proposition. A transition placed after a subject may still connect that sentence to the preceding text.

Translate goals into criteria

The goal controls which notes matter and how the selected information must be organized. These are common teaching categories; a question can combine them or use another rhetorical aim.

If the goal asks you to...

The sentence should...

A tempting miss...

Introduce

Supply relevant identifying context and the requested focus, calibrated to any stated audience.

Gives only a date or assumes an unfamiliar audience already recognizes the subject.

Explain a concept or process

State what it is or how it works with the necessary relationship or sequence.

Names the topic or a use without explaining the requested mechanism.

Provide an example

Identify a specific case and make its connection to the stated idea clear.

Repeats the generalization or supplies a case of a different idea.

Emphasize similarity

Show the requested common trait of the named subjects.

Lists two facts or highlights a difference instead.

Emphasize difference

Compare the specified subjects on the relevant dimension.

Discusses only one subject, compares another dimension, or gives a shared feature.

Summarize or generalize

Convey the central pattern at a scope supported by the notes.

Recites a minor detail or turns a limited pattern into a universal statement.

Support a claim

Select facts that bear directly on that particular claim.

Offers interesting related facts that do not support it.

Show change over time

Connect comparable earlier and later states, with the direction of change clear.

Supplies dates without explaining the change or compares unlike measurements.

Describe purpose, method, or finding

Identify the specific research component requested.

Substitutes what researchers hoped to learn for what they actually found.

A word such as both, unlike, or because can express a relationship, but its presence does not prove that the relationship is accurate. Verify the whole claim.

Preserve meaning and scope

Feature

Preserve

Do not silently change it to...

Attribution

A named researcher proposes an explanation.

The explanation is an established fact accepted by everyone.

Certainty

The result suggests a possibility.

The result proves an inevitable outcome.

Population or setting

A pattern occurred in the observed sample or setting.

The pattern holds for all people, places, or conditions.

Causation

Two measures were associated, or one followed another.

One measure necessarily caused the other.

Measured versus predicted

A model estimates an outcome; an instrument records a measurement.

The estimate was an observed outcome, or the observation was merely predicted.

Quantity and unit

A length in meters, a percentage of a stated group, or a rate per hour.

A different unit, denominator, or rate base.

Time and comparison

Two measurements refer to specified dates and the same relevant variable.

A change based on different variables, unmatched periods, or an invented earlier condition.

Relevance

Enough accurate information to meet the stated goal.

Every available fact, an unsupported flourish, or background that displaces the requested point.

Check quantitative sentences carefully

If a measure rises from 20 to 30, it increases by 10 units, or 50% relative to the original 20. If a percentage rises from 20% to 30%, that is an increase of 10 percentage points; the relative increase is still 50%. Identify which description the sentence uses. Keep the comparison's original base and unit.

Verify factual support and complete goal fulfillment as separate checks.

Make an error useful

Record the decision that failed, not just the correct letter. A useful review note tells you what to inspect on the next unfamiliar question.

Error

Precise diagnosis

Next action

Transition sounded natural.

I compared two topics without checking their actual claims.

Paraphrase both propositions before choosing a connector.

True facts seemed sufficient.

The sentence was accurate but explained the method when the goal asked for the finding.

Turn the goal into a short action-and-target checklist.

I overlooked a qualifier.

I treated a result in one trial as a universal result.

Check population, time, attribution, and certainty.

Retest the reasoning

  • For a transition, explain what the wrong connector would imply and why the text does not support that implication.
  • For synthesis, state which exact goal requirement the closest accurate distractor misses, or which note an inaccurate distractor changes.
  • Try a new item that tests the same decision. Remembering the previous answer letter is not evidence that the skill has transferred.

Official skill

Coverage in this guide

Transitions

Twelve lessons on propositions and direction; addition; similarity; contrast; concession; cause and consequence; examples and specification; clarification; chronology; correction; inference and summary; and scope across a complete passage.

Rhetorical Synthesis

Twelve lessons on interpreting goals; audience; introductions and explanations; examples; similarities and differences; summaries; claim support; change over time; research purpose, method, and findings; quantitative comparisons; and interacting constraints.

The guide contains 24 worked multiple-choice examples and 120 independent practice items: 12 short drills and 108 multiple-choice questions. Practice comprises a 12-question diagnostic, 42 items in each of two chapters, and a 24-question cumulative review. Every lesson links to three independent MCQs. Every worked and practice MCQ explains the key and all three alternatives.

The balanced practice allocation is a teaching choice, not a prediction of test frequency. Expression of Ideas is one domain within SAT Reading and Writing; Information and Ideas, Craft and Structure, and Standard English Conventions require separate preparation.
