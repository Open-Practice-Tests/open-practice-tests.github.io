About this guide

Two chapters, 24 worked examples and 120 practice activities: 108 multiple-choice questions and 12 written drills.

How to use this guide

Expression of Ideas asks you to make a text work for its purpose. A transition must express the relationship between the actual ideas, not merely sound fluent. A sentence built from notes must accomplish the stated goal, not merely contain true facts. Strong readers often lose points by stopping after the first plausible connection or by overlooking one word in the goal.

A practical sequence

  • Take the 12-question diagnostic untimed. Record an answer, confidence, and one short reason. Treat a correct guess as unfinished learning.
  • For each lesson, study the method and worked example; attempt its three practice questions independently. Begin a chapter with its six short drills when you need to make the reasoning visible.
  • Use a short note: "expected failure; observed success" for a transition, or "compare both materials on cost" for synthesis. Tie the reason to the actual text.
  • Return to missed questions after a day or two and again about a week later. Reconstruct the relationship or goal before checking the letter. Adjust the intervals as needed.
  • Finish with the 24-question cumulative review. Build accurate reasoning before adding time pressure; use official practice tests to assess transfer to the complete test. [1]

Transitions: prove the relationship

Read the passage with the blank left empty. Identify what the sentence before the blank establishes and what the sentence containing the blank adds or changes. When the connection depends on an earlier sentence, include that sentence too. The relationship comes from the propositions: what is being claimed about whom or what.

Step

Action

What you must establish

1

Read the connected units.

Use complete ideas, not only the nearest words. An internal transition may link its whole sentence to an earlier claim.

2

Paraphrase each proposition.

Keep the direction, certainty, and subject. A prediction is different from an observation; a limitation is different from a rejection.

3

State the relationship plainly.

Does the next idea add, contrast, exemplify, restate, follow in time, replace, summarize, or follow logically?

4

Test the actual choices.

Insert each candidate into the exact text. Ask what it signals and whether the passage supports that signal.

5

Verify the best surviving choice.

Check the wider passage and explain the nearest competitor. Do not settle for a transition that merely sounds natural.

A compact model

Engineers expected the thin coating to wear away quickly. After five years, it remained intact. The first proposition predicts brief durability; the second reports prolonged durability. A contrast marker fits the conflict between expectation and observation. A result marker would wrongly present prolonged durability as a consequence of expecting failure.

What a label cannot do for you

Transition families overlap. An example may also develop a point; a conclusion may also summarize evidence. Do not choose by a rigid ranking such as "specific beats general." Identify the job of this sentence in this passage, then determine which offered transition expresses it most accurately. Similar transitions are not automatically interchangeable in every construction.

Do not let shared vocabulary, positive or negative tone, or the final noun of the previous sentence decide the answer. Two statements can mention different objects while supporting the same point; two statements about one object can oppose each other.

Synthesis: satisfy the whole goal

Begin with the goal. Turn it into a short checklist, then read the notes to identify the evidence that can meet it. Read enough of the notes to verify context and qualifiers; "goal first" does not mean "ignore the notes." The strongest answer needs both factual support and rhetorical fit.

Check

Ask

Common failure

Action

What must the sentence do: introduce, compare, explain, exemplify, summarize, or support?

It gives related information but performs a different action.

Target

Which person, work, process, claim, or comparison dimension is specified?

It addresses the same general topic but the wrong feature.

Audience

What does the reader already know, if the prompt specifies this?

It omits needed identifying context or spends the sentence on familiar background instead of the requested point.

Support

Which notes support each substantive claim?

It changes a number, cause, time, group, attribution, or level of certainty.

Completeness

Does the sentence meet every stated requirement?

It satisfies one part of a compound goal and neglects another.

A compact model

Suppose the notes state that an old sensor recorded temperature once per hour, its replacement records temperature once per minute, and both occupied the same pier. The goal is to emphasize the difference in recording frequency.

Goal fulfilled: The replacement sensor records temperature once per minute, compared with once per hour for the old sensor. Both devices and their contrasting frequencies are explicit. The shared location is unnecessary for this goal.

Accurate but off goal: The old sensor and its replacement both recorded temperature from the same pier. This sentence expresses a similarity in location; it does not establish the requested difference.

Select, connect, and verify

You do not need to use every note. You do need to connect the selected facts in the way the goal requests. Preserve a qualification when omitting it would change the claim. After choosing, point to the notes supporting the sentence and name the requirement that the closest rival misses.
