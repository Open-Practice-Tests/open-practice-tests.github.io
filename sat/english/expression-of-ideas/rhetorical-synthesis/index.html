Rhetorical Synthesis

  • Chapter 2
Practice this lessonReview and reference
←PreviousAll lessons

Select and connect accurate notes to fulfill the writer's complete purpose.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Translate a rhetorical goal into specific requirements for content, audience, emphasis, and scope.
  • Select the notes needed for that goal and distinguish accurate but irrelevant details from unsupported claims.
  • Combine information without changing its meaning, including comparisons, sequences, study findings, and numerical relationships.
  • Evaluate every choice against both the evidence and the complete task, then explain why the closest competitor falls short.

2.1 Turn the goal into selection criteria

A synthesis question asks you to do a particular writing job with a small set of notes. It does not ask for the most complete inventory of the research. Begin with the goal: identify its action, its subject, and any limits. "Explain why a material was chosen for outdoor signs" requires a reason for that choice; the material's inventor and color might be accurate details that do nothing to explain it.

Turn those requirements into a short private checklist before evaluating the choices. Then mark the notes that can satisfy each requirement. A useful checklist might be "name the selected material + connect its property to outdoor use." This step prevents an appealing fact from redefining the task. Read the selected notes together: a design requirement in one note and a tested property in another may form the needed explanation.

Finally, test both accuracy and purpose. A choice can pass the accuracy check while failing the purpose check. Extra information is not automatically disqualifying, and the shortest sentence is not automatically strongest. The decisive question is whether the sentence performs the complete requested job clearly, without unsupported connections. Use your checklist as a filter, not as a demand that the answer repeat the prompt's exact words.

Goal language

Information to seek

Explain why X was selected

A requirement and a property that meets it

Emphasize X's contribution

What X did and why that action matters to the stated topic

Describe a limitation

The condition that restricts a result or use

W2.1: Select the reason, not the whole project

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. A design team created reusable identification tags for plants in a coastal garden.

2. The tags needed to remain readable after repeated exposure to salt spray.

3. The team tested printed paper tags and engraved ceramic tags under the same spray conditions.

4. After testing, the paper tags were illegible, but the engraved ceramic tags remained readable.

5. The team selected ceramic tags, which took longer to produce than paper tags and came in two colors.

The student wants to explain why the team selected ceramic tags for the coastal garden. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. The team selected ceramic tags for a coastal garden after testing two tag materials, and the ceramic tags came in two colors.

B. The team selected ceramic tags because their engravings remained readable after salt-spray testing, meeting the coastal garden's need for durable identification.

C. The ceramic tags selected for the coastal garden came in two colors and required more production time than the paper tags.

D. The team tested paper and ceramic tags under identical conditions to investigate how salt spray affected the readability of plant identifiers.

Practice this lesson: 2.07, 2.08, 2.09.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.1

Answer: B

Chosen sentence: The team selected ceramic tags because their engravings remained readable after salt-spray testing, meeting the coastal garden's need for durable identification.

  1. Reduce the goal to "explain the selection of ceramic tags." A list of project details will not supply the reason.
  2. Connect the readability requirement in note 2 with the test result in note 4; note 5 identifies the chosen material.
  3. Choice B states that the tested property met the requirement, making the reason for the choice explicit.
  4. Check the tempting procedural answer D: it explains what the test investigated, but leaves the selection and its reason unstated.

Evidence from the notes: Coastal need for readable identifiers: notes 1-2. Ceramic engravings remained readable in the test: note 4. Ceramic was the selected material: note 5.

Goal check: B names the chosen material and connects its successful test result to the garden's stated requirement.

Why A fails: Accurate, but the testing and color facts do not tell the reader why ceramic was selected.

Why C fails: Accurate, but production time and colors do not establish the benefit that satisfied the garden's need.

Why D fails: Accurate, but this describes the test's purpose without reporting the decisive result or explaining the selection.

Key takeaway: Use the goal to identify the connection the sentence must make, not just the facts it must mention.

2.2 Introduce a subject to the specified audience

An introduction gives readers the context they need for the writer's purpose. For an unfamiliar person, that often means an identity or role plus the contribution relevant to the topic. For an unfamiliar work, it may mean the creator, the kind of work, and its distinguishing feature. The prompt determines which of these details matter; there is no fixed biographical formula.

First, underline the audience condition. A reader unfamiliar with a composer may need to know that the person composes music. A reader already familiar with the composer may instead need an explanation of a particular project. Next, locate the minimum useful orientation in the notes. Combine it with the detail the goal asks you to highlight. Check pronouns and titles: a sentence can be grammatically complete yet give a new reader too little context to understand its subject.

Do not assume that familiar audiences know every related fact, or that unfamiliar audiences need every date and award. Familiarity is limited to what the prompt states. Likewise, a title alone does not establish that something is a novel, installation, or research project. When choices differ in background detail, decide which background helps this audience accomplish the specified purpose rather than rewarding either maximum explanation or maximum brevity.

Useful introduction

Unfamiliar with the person

Identity or role plus the requested contribution

Knows the person, not the work

Type of work plus its relevant feature

Knows the work, not the technique

Technique's meaning plus its role in that work

C. Textile artist Mira Solen used discarded climbing rope for the panels of Tide Ladder, an installation that she created in 2024.

D. Textile artist Mira Solen explores changes in coastal spaces through Tide Ladder, an installation of discarded rope panels resembling a rising waterline.

2.3 Explain a concept or process

An explanation makes an idea understandable by showing what it means or how its parts work together. A definition identifies a concept's essential feature; a process explanation connects an input, action, or condition to an outcome. A sentence that merely names an application or announces that a process is useful may be accurate without explaining the requested idea.

Build a small chain from the relevant notes: "when this happens, this component changes, producing that result." Include only links the notes support. For a definition, ask what distinguishes the concept from neighboring ideas. For a process, ask which change produces the stated outcome. Pay attention to direction: heat leaving a sample is not heat entering it, and a sensor detecting pressure is not the sensor creating pressure.

Explanations may compress steps, but compression must preserve the mechanism and any required starting or ending point. Not every question demands a complete technical account. If the goal asks how a valve closes, details about manufacturing or eventual disposal may be irrelevant. Avoid adding an intuitive scientific explanation that the notes never establish. Even a plausible connection needs evidence here; your job is to explain the supplied account, including its stated limits, rather than complete it from outside knowledge.

Sentence does this

What to check

Defines a concept

Does it state the distinguishing feature?

Explains a process

Does it connect the necessary actions in the correct direction?

Names a use or benefit

Does that actually answer the requested how or what?

W2.3: Preserve the mechanism

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. A prototype seedling pot uses a two-layer strip to control a small watering valve.

2. The lower layer absorbs moisture and expands; the upper layer neither absorbs moisture nor expands.

3. Unequal expansion makes the strip bend upward when the surrounding soil is wet.

4. The upward bend pushes the valve closed, stopping the flow of water into the pot.

5. The prototype was tested with bean seedlings, and its valve can also be closed by hand.

The student wants to explain how wet soil causes the prototype pot to stop receiving water. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. The prototype pot can stop receiving water when its valve is closed by hand, and the design was tested using bean seedlings.

B. The prototype pot uses a two-layer strip connected to a small watering valve, with one moisture-absorbing layer and one nonabsorbing layer.

C. In wet soil, the strip's lower layer expands while its upper layer does not, bending the strip upward to close the watering valve.

D. Wet soil makes both layers of the strip expand equally, causing the strip to bend upward and close the valve supplying the pot.

Practice this lesson: 2.13, 2.14, 2.15.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.3

Answer: C

Chosen sentence: In wet soil, the strip's lower layer expands while its upper layer does not, bending the strip upward to close the watering valve.

  1. The goal requires a causal chain from wet soil to stopped water flow, not a description of the device's parts alone.
  2. Use notes 2-4 in order: unequal absorption produces unequal expansion, which bends the strip and closes the valve.
  3. C preserves the difference between the layers and the direction of the bend, then links the bend to closure.
  4. Check B carefully: its components are accurate, but it leaves their movement and its consequence unexplained.

Evidence from the notes: Moisture absorption and unequal layer expansion: note 2. Upward bending under wet conditions: note 3. Bending closes the valve and stops flow: note 4.

Goal check: C explains the response to wet soil and connects that response to the valve shutting off water.

Why A fails: Accurate, but explains manual closure and names the test plants instead of explaining the response to wet soil.

Why B fails: Accurate component description, but does not connect moisture to bending or bending to the valve's closure.

Why D fails: Contradicts note 2: the layers do not expand equally, and unequal expansion is the stated reason for the bend.

Key takeaway: When the goal asks how something happens, preserve the links that turn a list of parts into an explanation.

2.4 Use a specific example of a stated idea

An example is a particular case that exhibits the defining feature of a broader idea. To choose one, first translate the idea into a test. If the idea is that an object was repurposed, the example must show an earlier use and a different later use. Describing an old object, an unusual object, or a repaired object does not necessarily pass that test.

Then find a note or combination of notes that demonstrates the required feature. A strong answer often names a concrete case and includes the detail that makes it an instance of the idea. It need not repeat the general statement word for word, but the connection should be clear from the supplied information. Distinguish a specific example from a definition, a claim that examples exist, or a neighboring concept.

Watch the full wording of the goal. "An example from the theater project" limits which case may be selected. "An example of cooperation between species" requires both the relevant participants and the cooperative relationship; merely placing species together is insufficient. Do not reject another genuine example because it is less vivid. If several notes qualify, another stated condition must distinguish the best choice. The safest reasoning identifies the feature and the evidence that the selected case displays it.

Broader idea

An example needs

Repurposing

An old use and a distinct new use

Shared responsibility

At least two participants carrying out parts of the responsibility

A material change

A concrete before-and-after property, not merely a new location

W2.4: Demonstrate what makes the case qualify

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. For this project, repurposing means using an object for a function different from its original one.

2. A neighborhood theater obtained several wooden shipping crates that had carried lighting equipment.

3. The theater converted the crates into seats for its outdoor audience.

4. The theater also repaired a damaged spotlight and returned it to lighting performances.

5. Volunteers painted the stage backdrop and moved unused curtains into a storage room.

The student wants to give a specific example of repurposing in the theater project. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. The theater repurposed wooden crates that had carried lighting equipment by converting them into seats for the audience at its outdoor performances.

B. The theater repaired a damaged spotlight and returned it to use in performances, extending the working life of an existing piece of equipment.

C. The theater's volunteers painted the stage backdrop and moved unused curtains to storage while preparing the space for its outdoor audience during the project.

D. Repurposing involves giving an existing object a function different from its original one, a practice relevant to projects such as the neighborhood theater's.

Practice this lesson: 2.16, 2.17, 2.18.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.4

Answer: A

Chosen sentence: The theater repurposed wooden crates that had carried lighting equipment by converting them into seats for the audience at its outdoor performances.

  1. Define the membership test from note 1: the same object must receive a different function.
  2. Combine notes 2-3: the crates first carried equipment and later served as seats.
  3. A states both functions, so the reader can see why this particular case fits the idea.
  4. B is the nearest conceptual distractor: repairing a spotlight extends its use but does not change its lighting function.

Key takeaway: Show the feature that makes the case an example; naming a related activity does not establish the connection.

2.5 Emphasize a meaningful similarity

A similarity compares two or more subjects on a shared feature. Start by identifying the subjects and the dimension named in the goal: purpose, method, material, structure, or result. The sentence should make that common feature visible. Two facts placed in the same sentence do not automatically establish a similarity, even when both are relevant to the general topic.

Build a parallel statement from the notes: "Both X and Y use method M," or "X and Y share purpose P, although their methods differ." These are reasoning patterns, not required answer wording. Check that each half of the comparison has evidence. If one project reduces water use and another measures water use, you cannot group them as projects that reduced use merely because both concern water.

Keep the similarity at the supported level. The same goal does not imply the same process, and the same type of result does not imply equal numerical results. A difference may appear in a correct answer if it helps frame the requested similarity, but it should not replace that emphasis. When a goal names a comparison dimension, an accurate similarity on another dimension is off-goal. When the goal is broad, do not invent an unstated preference for one true similarity over another.

Notes establish

Safe common claim

Same purpose, different procedures

Both pursue that purpose

Different amounts of improvement

Both improved, if both results show improvement

Related topics, only one shared method stated

Do not infer an identical method

W2.5: Match the requested dimension

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. The Brookline and Mesa projects both collected observations about neighborhood bird activity.

2. The Brookline project invited residents to identify birds by matching photographs to a supplied guide.

3. The Mesa project invited residents to identify bird calls by matching recordings to a supplied audio guide.

4. Both projects accepted observations from residents with no previous bird-identification training.

5. Brookline recorded sightings at sunrise, while Mesa collected recordings throughout the afternoon.

The student wants to emphasize a similarity in who could participate in the two projects. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. Brookline residents identified birds using photographs, whereas Mesa residents matched recorded bird calls to an audio guide when submitting their neighborhood observations.

B. Both the Brookline and Mesa projects welcomed neighborhood residents who lacked prior bird-identification training to contribute observations about local bird activity.

C. The Brookline and Mesa projects both gathered information about neighborhood bird activity, although one collected sunrise sightings and the other collected afternoon recordings.

D. Brookline gathered sightings at sunrise, and Mesa gathered recordings in the afternoon, giving each project a different daily period for observing birds.

Practice this lesson: 2.19, 2.20, 2.21.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.5

Answer: B

Chosen sentence: Both the Brookline and Mesa projects welcomed neighborhood residents who lacked prior bird-identification training to contribute observations about local bird activity.

  1. The requested dimension is participant eligibility, not the projects' topic, identification methods, or observation schedules.
  2. Note 4 explicitly supports the same eligibility condition for both projects.
  3. B states that shared condition and identifies both subjects without claiming they used the same observation method.
  4. C contains a real similarity, but it concerns the research topic rather than who could participate.

Evidence from the notes: The two projects and their bird observations: note 1. Residents without previous identification training were eligible in both: note 4.

Goal check: B compares participant eligibility and makes the shared openness to untrained residents explicit.

Why A fails: Accurate comparison of methods, but contrasts how residents identified birds rather than comparing who could participate.

Why C fails: Accurate topic similarity and schedule difference, but does not state the requested similarity in eligible participants.

Why D fails: Accurate schedule contrast, but supplies neither the participant condition nor an emphasis on similarity.

Key takeaway: A true similarity can still miss the goal when it compares the wrong feature.

2.6 Emphasize a difference on a shared dimension

A useful contrast compares the same kind of information about each subject. If the goal asks how two translations handle a recurring word, compare each translator's treatment of that word. Contrasting one translator's publication date with the other's vocabulary does not explain that difference. Establish the shared comparison dimension before deciding which unequal facts matter.

Create a two-column mental table: subject X and subject Y, with the requested feature as the row label. Fill both cells from the notes. The answer should state or clearly imply the difference between those entries. Useful contrasts can concern categories, methods, timing, degrees, or outcomes. They need not declare one subject superior. Preserve any conditions attached to each entry; results under different test conditions cannot automatically establish an overall ranking.

Check comparative language carefully. "More," "less," "earlier," and "only" make specific claims that require evidence. A feature documented for X is not necessarily absent from Y unless the notes establish that absence. Also separate the comparison from its explanation: notes may show that two choices differ without explaining why. A correct sentence can include shared context, but that context should help readers understand the requested difference, not substitute a general similarity for the contrast.

Proposed contrast

Required support

X used one method; Y used another

Evidence for each method

X had more than Y

Comparable quantities with compatible units and scope

Only X had a feature

Evidence for X's presence and Y's absence

W2.6: Keep the comparison on one row

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. Two teams restored different sections of a painted wooden screen.

2. Team Alder removed surface dust from its section with a soft brush.

3. Team Birch removed surface dust from its section with a low-suction vacuum.

4. Both teams then filled small cracks with the same type of reversible filler.

5. Team Alder worked in March, and Team Birch worked in April; neither report compared the methods' effectiveness.

The student wants to emphasize the difference between the teams' methods of removing surface dust. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. Team Alder worked on the painted screen in March, whereas Team Birch restored its section in April and also removed surface dust.

B. Both restoration teams removed surface dust and then filled small cracks in their sections of the painted wooden screen using the same reversible filler.

C. Team Alder's soft brush removed surface dust more effectively than Team Birch's low-suction vacuum, although both teams used the same filler afterward.

D. To remove surface dust from the painted screen, Team Alder used a soft brush, whereas Team Birch used a low-suction vacuum.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.6

Answer: D

Chosen sentence: To remove surface dust from the painted screen, Team Alder used a soft brush, whereas Team Birch used a low-suction vacuum.

  1. The shared row is "method of removing surface dust." Fill Alder's cell with brush and Birch's cell with low-suction vacuum.
  2. D compares those two entries directly, using the common restoration task to orient the reader.
  3. C includes the right methods but adds an effectiveness ranking that note 5 explicitly says the reports did not make.

2.7 Summarize without widening the evidence

A summary compresses the notes around a central pattern. It does not earn its strength by making the broadest possible claim. First identify the group actually described: four stations, the novels in one collection, or participants in one study. Then identify the pattern within that group. Finally, check whether an exception or limitation changes the pattern enough to belong in the summary. A statement about most of a sample cannot automatically become a statement about all members of a larger population.

Quantifiers do substantial work. Some establishes at least one without, by itself, establishing that not all qualify; most requires a majority; all permits no exceptions within the stated group. If six of eight sites changed, a summary that says the sites generally changed may be supportable, but one that says every site changed is not. Prefer wording whose scope is visibly supported by the notes, especially when the goal asks you to preserve differences or exceptions.

Compression also has limits. A sentence that accurately describes one case may be too narrow to summarize several cases. A sentence that names every case may still miss their shared pattern. Ask: What can I say about this whole set, and what must I retain so that the compression does not mislead?

Notes establish

Supported summary

Unjustified expansion

Three of four sampled groups used grants.

Most sampled groups used grants.

Groups generally depend on grants.

Two techniques appear in all three works studied.

The three works share those techniques.

Every work by the author uses those techniques.

W2.7: Summarizing a pattern and an exception

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. A researcher examined replacement-equipment purchases by four Bellwater community radio stations in 2023.

2. Alder, Beacon, and Cove each combined city grants with listener donations.

3. Drift funded its purchase entirely through a local business sponsorship.

4. All four replaced recording equipment at least ten years old.

5. No other stations or earlier equipment purchases were examined.

The student wants to summarize the funding pattern among the four stations while acknowledging the exception. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. In 2023, Alder, Beacon, and Cove used city grants and listener donations to replace recording equipment that was at least ten years old.

B. Community radio stations typically replace old equipment using a combination of city grants and listener donations, although business sponsorships are also possible.

C. For their 2023 equipment purchases, three of the four stations combined city grants with listener donations, while Drift relied entirely on a business sponsorship.

D. The four Bellwater stations all replaced recording equipment in 2023, and the researcher did not examine their earlier equipment purchases.

Practice this lesson: 2.25, 2.26, 2.27.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.7

Answer: C

Chosen sentence: For their 2023 equipment purchases, three of the four stations combined city grants with listener donations, while Drift relied entirely on a business sponsorship.

  1. Convert the goal into two requirements: summarize funding across the four stations and retain the station that followed a different pattern.
  2. Group Alder, Beacon, and Cove because note 2 gives them the same combination of funding sources; keep Drift separate because note 3 gives it a different source.
  3. Restrict the sentence to these stations and their 2023 purchases; note 5 provides no basis for a general claim about community radio funding.
  4. Choose C because it compresses the three similar cases and states the exception in the same comparison.

Evidence from the notes: The set consists of four stations and the purchases occurred in 2023: note 1. Three stations combined grants and listener donations: note 2. Drift relied entirely on a business sponsorship: note 3.

Goal check: C summarizes the majority pattern and the exception while limiting the claim to the four stations and the specified purchases.

Why A fails: The information is accurate under notes 2 and 4, but it excludes Drift, so the required exception disappears from the summary.

Why B fails: The named funding sources appear in the notes, but typically extends a four-station finding to community radio stations generally, beyond the researcher's evidence.

Why D fails: The shared replacement activity and the study boundary are accurate under notes 1, 4, and 5, but the sentence does not summarize how the purchases were funded.

Key takeaway: A useful summary preserves both the pattern and the boundaries of the evidence that supports it.

2.8 Select evidence that supports the specified claim

When the goal supplies a claim, treat that claim as the proposition your sentence must support. Translate it into a question: What observation would give a reader a reason to accept this particular statement? A date, location, or biography may be relevant to the topic without providing evidence for the claim. Look for the note that bears on the claim's actual relationship, such as coexistence, adaptability, influence, or a change in measured performance.

Build a short evidence chain: claim, relevant observation, connection. If the claim is that a designer incorporated damage into the finished appearance, visible repairs support it more directly than the designer's training or the age of the object. The answer need not mechanically repeat the claim, but the evidence should make its relevance apparent.

Do not strengthen the claim beyond what the evidence permits. One successful trial can support a statement about that trial; it cannot establish that a device always succeeds. When a causal claim attributes an effect to a particular variable, check whether the comparison controls other relevant conditions; a descriptive association does not require that same causal evidence. Finally, distinguish an answer that merely announces the claim from an answer that supplies a concrete reason for it. Repetition is not additional evidence.

W2.8: Evidence rather than background

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. Textile artist Irena Pell completed the wall hanging Mended Field in 2019 using damaged linen cloth.

3. She repaired tears with thick red thread, leaving every repair visible in the finished hanging.

5. The 140-centimeter-wide work was first displayed in a community arts center.

The student wants to support the claim that Pell made the cloth's earlier damage part of the finished work's visual design. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. In Mended Field, Pell left repairs made with thick red thread visible against the cloth's original pale gray areas, incorporating the repaired tears into its appearance.

B. Before completing the wall hanging Mended Field in 2019, Irena Pell had trained as a furniture restorer and then begun making textile art.

C. Pell's Mended Field was made from a damaged linen cloth, measures 140 centimeters wide, and was first displayed in a community arts center.

D. Pell made the cloth's earlier damage part of Mended Field's visual design by concealing every repaired tear beneath a new layer of pale gray fabric.

Answer: A

Chosen sentence: In Mended Field, Pell left repairs made with thick red thread visible against the cloth's original pale gray areas, incorporating the repaired tears into its appearance.

  1. The claim concerns the visible role of damage in the completed object, so search for information about how the tears were treated and what viewers could see.
  2. Note 3 supplies the direct evidence: every red-thread repair remained visible; note 4 supplies the contrast with the original gray cloth.

Why B fails: The career information is accurate under note 2, but training as a restorer does not show how damage functioned in this work's appearance.

Why C fails: These facts are supported by notes 1 and 5, but using damaged cloth alone does not establish that the damage remained part of the visible design.

2.9 Show sequence and change over time

A goal about development or change requires more than collecting dates. Identify the entity being tracked and the dimension that changes: an author's revision, a machine's function, a population's recorded size, or the order of a process. Arrange the relevant observations in time before you choose a sentence. Then decide whether the goal asks for the complete sequence, an initial-to-final contrast, or one turning point.

Keep the time scale visible enough to support the comparison. An annual total and a single month's total do not show a decline on the same basis. Likewise, a beginning and ending value may conceal a temporary rise in between. If the goal asks how something changed throughout an observation, an accurate endpoint comparison can still be incomplete.

Chronology establishes order, not necessarily cause or intention. A bridge can be strengthened after a flood without the notes establishing that the flood prompted the work. Words such as because, led to, and in order to add relationships that dates alone do not supply. Also preserve what stayed the same when it matters to the goal: a revised design may change its material while retaining its shape. A sequence sentence should organize the evidence, not invent the story connecting it.

2.10 Separate a study's purpose, method, and findings

Research notes often contain three different kinds of information. The purpose is the question the researchers intended to investigate. The method is what they did to investigate it. The finding is what their measurements or analysis showed. Mark these roles before reading the choices, because each can sound informative while serving a different goal.

Watch the verbs. Researchers may seek to determine whether a sound affects recall, ask participants to hear a sound, and find a difference in recall. The first states a purpose, the second a method, and the third a finding. A study's purpose does not imply that the expected result occurred. Conversely, a procedural detail does not report an outcome simply because it includes a number, such as the number of participants.

Findings also carry limits. A reported association should remain an association unless the notes justify a causal conclusion. Failure to establish a difference does not prove the treatments identical for every person. If the goal asks for both a purpose and a finding, include both and preserve any qualification needed to characterize the result accurately. Do not let an appealing result replace the original research question, or let a detailed procedure crowd out the requested conclusion.

2.11 Compare quantities on the right basis

A numerical answer must preserve what was counted or measured, the unit, the group or denominator, and the time period. Before calculating, write the comparison in words: total electricity, electricity per square meter, the share of surveyed homes, or an estimated townwide count. A correct number can answer the wrong quantitative question.

For a proportion, divide the relevant part by its own total. A rise from 18 of 60 to 36 of 90 is a rise from 30% to 40%, even though the raw count doubled. Subtracting the percentages gives a 10-percentage-point increase. Dividing that 10-point change by the original 30% gives a relative increase of about 33.3%. Those descriptions are different, and neither licenses calling the share's increase 100%.

Keep comparisons like for like: equal units, compatible periods, and the requested denominator. A larger total can coexist with a smaller rate. Preserve qualifiers such as estimated, approximately, and in the sample; arithmetic cannot turn an estimate into an observation. If the goal does not require a calculation, do not assume that the answer with the most numbers is strongest. Check each quantity's meaning first, then verify any calculation the chosen sentence actually needs.

B. Bicycle arrivals represented 30% of surveyed employees in 2018 and 40% in 2024, an increase of 10 percentage points between the two surveys.

C. Bicycle arrivals represented 30% of surveyed employees in 2018 and 40% in 2024, meaning the percentage arriving by bicycle doubled.

D. Lark Workshop's 2018 travel survey included 60 employees, while its 2024 survey included 90 employees, each of whom selected one travel mode.

Answer: B

Chosen sentence: Bicycle arrivals represented 30% of surveyed employees in 2018 and 40% in 2024, an increase of 10 percentage points between the two surveys.

2.12 Satisfy the complete goal and preserve qualifications

Some goals combine requirements: introduce a concept to unfamiliar readers, attribute an interpretation to its source, and preserve its tentative status. Turn the goal into a short checklist before evaluating choices. Each requirement matters. A sentence that fulfills two of three can be accurate and well written while still being incomplete.

Check attribution closely. A critic's reading is not automatically an artist's stated intention, and a team's prediction is not an observed outcome. Phrases such as according to, proposed, estimated, and may can carry the exact distinction the goal requires. Removing them may change the evidential status of a claim rather than merely shorten the sentence. At the same time, do not add uncertainty where the notes give a definite observation; attach the qualification to the claim that needs it.

Audience information helps determine what context the sentence must supply. Readers unfamiliar with a technical term may need a brief definition. Readers who already know a work may instead need a direct comparison of interpretations. Familiarity does not permit missing evidence or inaccurate attribution. After checking the facts, read the goal again and test the whole sentence against every requirement. This final pass often distinguishes the best answer from a plausible choice that accomplishes only part of the assignment.

C. Orin proposed that heat released by interior walls contributed to cooling in the rooms she monitored, but her monitoring did not directly test that explanation.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

• Name the requested job before selecting facts: introduction, explanation, example, comparison, support, summary, sequence, or another stated aim.

• Identify any required audience, entities, comparison dimension, date range, quantity, qualification, and attribution.

• Map each claim in the leading choice to a note; preserve units, denominators, uncertainty, and the limits of the evidence.

• Check what the sentence emphasizes and connects, since several choices can be accurate while doing different jobs.

• Reject each remaining choice for a specific reason: missing a goal requirement, selecting off-goal evidence, or distorting the notes.

Begin with six short reasoning drills, then complete thirty-six multiple-choice questions. Each lesson has three questions. Identify the decisive relationship or goal requirement, and explain the closest competing answer. The model responses to the drills are examples; other well-supported responses may also work.

These six written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.
