Transitions

  • Chapter 1
Practice this lessonReview and reference
All lessonsNext→

Connect the actual claims in a passage with a word or phrase that accurately signals their relationship.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Reduce the ideas around a blank to clear propositions and identify the direction of their connection.
  • Distinguish addition, similarity, contrast, concession, causation, illustration, clarification, sequence, replacement, inference, and summary without treating these teaching labels as official subskills.
  • Choose a transition from the whole passage while preserving its scope, timing, and strength of evidence.
  • Verify the selected choice by inserting it literally and explaining why each competing relationship fails in this context.

1.1 Find the claims before naming the connection

A transition connects ideas, not isolated words. Begin by reducing the material before the blank to a short proposition: someone expected a difficult task, a device lost power, or two observations agreed. Then reduce the material after the blank in the same way. Preserve words such as only, although, and not; removing them can reverse the relationship you need to see.

Next, describe the connection in ordinary language. Does the second idea support the first, challenge it, explain it, illustrate it, or follow from it? Track the direction. A predicted event followed by contrary evidence calls for a different signal from a problem followed by the action it caused. Make a provisional prediction, then compare the choices and revise that prediction if closer reading reveals a different connection.

Finally, read beyond the nearest sentence when necessary. An intervening detail may explain how evidence was collected while the new sentence responds to an earlier claim. Your short paraphrase should identify that earlier claim explicitly. A transition is not justified merely because two sentences discuss the same topic. Nor does a change of topic automatically establish contrast. Insert your choice, reread the entire passage, and ask whether its promised relationship actually occurs.

W1.1: An earlier expectation meets new evidence

The designers of a community calendar expected its grid of color-coded symbols to confuse first-time users. To test that expectation, they asked twelve volunteers to locate three events without any instructions. ______, all twelve found the events immediately and correctly explained what the symbols meant.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. Consequently

B. For example

C. However

D. Previously

Practice this lesson: 1.07, 1.08, 1.09.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.1

Answer: C

Completed text: The designers of a community calendar expected its grid of color-coded symbols to confuse first-time users. To test that expectation, they asked twelve volunteers to locate three events without any instructions. However, all twelve found the events immediately and correctly explained what the symbols meant.

  1. Identify the claim being tested: the designers predicted that new users would be confused. The intervening sentence describes the test; it does not establish what the test would find.
  2. Paraphrase the result: every volunteer used the calendar successfully without instructions. That result runs against the designers' prediction.
  3. Predict a contrast or expectation-reversal signal. Insert 'However' and connect the final sentence to the prediction in the first sentence.
  4. Check the nearest competitor. Testing an expectation does not cause the expectation to be false, so the sequence of test and result does not justify 'Consequently.'

Why A fails: 'Consequently' would present the volunteers' success as a consequence of the preceding expectation or the mere decision to test it. The passage instead reports evidence that contradicts the expectation; it supplies no such causal explanation.

Why B fails: 'For example' would make successful use an illustration of predicted confusion or of asking volunteers to participate. The final sentence does neither: it states a contrary result.

Why D fails: 'Previously' would place the successful use before the events just described. The sentence reports the outcome of the test, not an earlier occasion.

Key takeaway: Keep the proposition being answered in view even when a sentence about procedure stands between the prediction and the evidence.

1.2 Add another point without promising a stronger link

An additive transition tells readers that another relevant point is coming. Words and phrases such as moreover, furthermore, and in addition can connect independent reasons, features, problems, or pieces of evidence that advance the same discussion. The new point need not be a duplicate. A program's affordability and its convenient location are different benefits, but the second can still reinforce a favorable assessment.

Use a two-part check. First, identify what each sentence contributes to the larger point. Second, ask whether the second contribution actually follows from, restates, or exemplifies the first. If each offers a separate reason, an additive signal may fit. Do not invent a mechanism just because two facts are favorable, or assume that one precise fact is an example of another precise fact.

Addition is broad, so it deserves careful treatment when evaluating choices. A contrast can sometimes also be described as adding information. You should not reject a plausible additive choice merely because another option has a narrower label. The question must establish a meaningful relationship that distinguishes the options in context. In your own reasoning, explain the specific extra point and test whether a competing signal would falsely turn it into an effect, an opposite, or a reformulation.

W1.2: Two independent features of a catalog

An online neighborhood seed catalog lets gardeners locate varieties by name through an alphabetical index. The index appears on a dedicated page linked from the catalog's main screen. ______, the catalog includes audio recordings that explain when and how to plant each variety.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. Consequently

B. Moreover

C. By contrast

D. In other words

Practice this lesson: 1.10, 1.11, 1.12.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.2

Answer: B

Completed text: An online neighborhood seed catalog lets gardeners locate varieties by name through an alphabetical index. The index appears on a dedicated page linked from the catalog's main screen. Moreover, the catalog includes audio recordings that explain when and how to plant each variety.

  1. Separate the catalog's two features: an alphabetical way to find names and an audio way to obtain planting instructions. The second feature supplies information beyond the first.
  2. Notice what the middle sentence does: it locates the index within the catalog. It neither causes nor explains the existence of planting recordings.
  3. Insert 'Moreover' to add another useful feature of the catalog. Both features advance the discussion of what the catalog provides.
  4. Test the closest rival, 'In other words.' Naming varieties alphabetically does not convey when or how to plant them; the final sentence contributes genuinely new information.

Why A fails: 'Consequently' would claim that including an alphabetical index produced the audio recordings. The passage supplies no dependency between those independently described features.

Why C fails: 'By contrast' would present the audio recordings as opposing the alphabetical index. The catalog includes both, and no shared dimension with opposite values is established.

Why D fails: 'In other words' would treat planting recordings as a rephrasing of the index. The index locates names; the recordings provide instructions, so they are distinct forms of help.

Key takeaway: Addition can join different useful features without claiming that one caused, opposed, or merely rephrased the other.

1.3 Identify the exact feature that two cases share

A similarity transition, such as similarly or likewise, tells readers to carry a relationship from one case into another. The cases do not need to match in every respect. Two artists can work in different media yet both use repeated shapes to organize a composition. State the shared feature in precise terms before choosing a parallel signal.

A useful procedure is to build a quick comparison: case A does X; case B also does X. Keep X at the same level of specificity. 'Both concern science' is usually too broad, whereas 'both use a change in color as evidence of a reaction' identifies the relevant parallel. Check that the second case is not simply one example contained within the first category.

Differences elsewhere in a passage do not automatically defeat a similarity transition. A passage might first distinguish two projects' purposes and then compare their methods. The blank's placement determines which dimension matters. Conversely, two sentences that repeat the same claim about one subject may call for clarification, not similarity. Avoid using shared vocabulary as proof: a library that restricts access and a library that expands access mention the same institution but describe opposite policies.

W1.3: A shared solution in different crafts

In the fictional coastal town of Neral, traveling storytellers make their wooden stage panels fold inward so that a complete stage fits on a cart. ______, instrument makers in the inland town of Sovan build harps with removable necks, allowing musicians to carry the instruments in compact cases.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. Instead

B. Similarly

C. For instance

D. Consequently

Practice this lesson: 1.13, 1.14, 1.15.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.3

Answer: B

Completed text: In the fictional coastal town of Neral, traveling storytellers make their wooden stage panels fold inward so that a complete stage fits on a cart. Similarly, instrument makers in the inland town of Sovan build harps with removable necks, allowing musicians to carry the instruments in compact cases.

  1. Summarize the first case: storytellers alter the structure of a stage to make it easier to transport. Summarize the second: makers alter the structure of harps for the same purpose.
  2. Name the common feature: each craft uses a design that becomes more compact for travel. The objects, locations, and particular mechanisms differ.
  3. Insert 'Similarly' to carry that design-purpose relationship from the first case to the second. The passage does not claim that the crafts are identical.
  4. Distinguish a parallel from an example. A harp in Sovan is not an instance of a stage panel used by Neral's storytellers; it is a separate case with a comparable solution.

Why A fails: 'Instead' would imply that making portable harps replaces the storytellers' folding-stage practice. The passage describes two practices that coexist in different towns.

Why C fails: 'For instance' would make the harp an example of the specific stage-panel practice just described. The second craft is outside that practice, although it shares a purpose.

Why D fails: 'Consequently' would claim that the first town's stage design caused the second town's harp design. No contact, influence, or other causal link is supplied.

Key takeaway: Compare the role or relationship shared by two cases, not merely their objects, locations, or vocabulary.

1.4 Mark a meaningful contrast on a shared dimension

A contrast transition signals a meaningful difference: one policy permits what another forbids, one measurement rises while another falls, or new evidence challenges a proposed interpretation. Common signals include however, by contrast, and on the other hand. The exact wording must fit the passage, but memorizing a list cannot replace identifying the disagreement.

To test a contrast, name the comparison dimension. 'The first design is blue; the second was built in June' gives two different facts without establishing opposing values on one dimension. 'The first design absorbs sound; the second reflects it' supplies a clear opposition in acoustic behavior. For an argument, the shared dimension may be whether a claim is supported, not a physical characteristic.

Read qualifiers carefully. A method might perform well on average but poorly in one subgroup; those claims can create a useful contrast without logically contradicting each other. Likewise, total attendance can increase while attendance per event decreases. Do not flatten such comparisons into 'the first sentence is false.' Ask exactly what the new information limits, reverses, or distinguishes. If the sentences merely provide two compatible benefits, a contrast signal needs further support.

W1.4: Opposite effects on the same measurement

An engineering class tested two experimental wall panels in the same model room. With the first panel installed, the room's measured echo time was shorter than it was without any panel. ______, with the second panel installed, the echo time was longer than it was without any panel.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. Similarly

B. In other words

C. As a result

D. By contrast

Practice this lesson: 1.16, 1.17, 1.18.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.4

Answer: D

Completed text: An engineering class tested two experimental wall panels in the same model room. With the first panel installed, the room's measured echo time was shorter than it was without any panel. By contrast, with the second panel installed, the echo time was longer than it was without any panel.

  1. Identify the baseline: the room without a panel. Both results use that baseline, so their directions are directly comparable.
  2. Record the first effect as shorter echo time and the second as longer echo time. The two panels move the same measurement in opposite directions.
  3. Choose 'By contrast' to signal that opposition. No scientific knowledge about panel materials is needed; the passage supplies the relevant results.
  4. Check that the second test is neither a matching result nor caused by the first result. Separate trials can produce contrasting outcomes without one producing the other.

Why A fails: 'Similarly' would make the second panel's effect match the first panel's effect. The first shortens echo time relative to the baseline, whereas the second lengthens it; those are opposite results on the same measurement.

Why B fails: 'In other words' would equate the two results. A measurement longer than the baseline does not restate a measurement shorter than the baseline.

Why C fails: 'As a result' would make the second panel's effect a consequence of the first panel's effect. The passage describes separate tests and supplies no such dependence.

Key takeaway: A defensible contrast identifies what is being compared and how the two cases differ on that dimension.

1.5 Acknowledge a fact, then reverse its expected implication

A concession accepts a fact while limiting the conclusion a reader might draw from it. Signals such as nevertheless, nonetheless, and even so often introduce something that holds despite a preceding disadvantage, obstacle, or reason for doubt. The first statement need not be false. A project can be expensive and still receive approval.

Use an expectation test: given the first fact, what outcome would a reader reasonably anticipate? Then compare that expectation with the actual outcome. If the passage says that a trail is steep but the group completes it easily, the second fact runs against the expected difficulty. Avoid assuming an expectation from personal opinions; the wording must establish the relevant obstacle or evaluative context.

Concession and contrast overlap. You may describe a concession as a type of contrast, but that does not make synonymous options distinguishable. If both however and nevertheless accurately complete a passage, a sound item would need different choices or different wording. For your own decisions, focus on the accepted fact and the implication being resisted. Also check scope: a small success does not necessarily reverse a claim about the entire project, and an obstacle may concern cost while the conclusion concerns a separate question.

W1.5: A cost objection does not determine the decision

A theater's purchasing committee had named purchase price its chief concern in choosing a lighting system. The new model's initial price was forty percent above the amount the committee had authorized. ______, the committee approved the new model, deciding that its unusually low maintenance needs justified the higher initial expense.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. For example

B. Nevertheless

C. As a result

D. Likewise

Practice this lesson: 1.19, 1.20, 1.21.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.5

Answer: B

Completed text: A theater's purchasing committee had named purchase price its chief concern in choosing a lighting system. The new model's initial price was forty percent above the amount the committee had authorized. Nevertheless, the committee approved the new model, deciding that its unusually low maintenance needs justified the higher initial expense.

  1. Locate the accepted obstacle: the new system costs more than the authorized amount, and purchase price is the committee's stated chief concern.
  2. Predict the implication of that obstacle: a reader would expect the committee to reject the expensive model. The passage itself establishes why price matters.
  3. Read the actual decision: the committee approves the model despite its price. 'Nevertheless' signals that reversal while allowing both the cost figure and the approval to be true.
  4. Keep the explanation in its proper position. Low maintenance needs explain the approval within the final sentence; the material before the blank presents a reason against approval, so 'As a result' misconnects those propositions.

Why A fails: 'For example' would make approval illustrate the excessive purchase price. Approval is a decision that resists the price objection, not an instance of a price being over budget.

Why C fails: 'As a result' would present approval as a consequence of exceeding a budget that the committee had made its chief concern. The passage gives the opposite relationship: approval occurs despite that obstacle, for a reason supplied afterward.

Why D fails: 'Likewise' would signal a parallel case or matching decision. The authorized amount and the over-budget model do not establish another approval for this decision to parallel.

Key takeaway: A concession can preserve a true obstacle while introducing a decision that runs against the obstacle's expected implication.

1.6 Trace causes and consequences without inventing them

A result transition says more than 'this happened next.' Phrases such as as a result, consequently, and therefore present a new event, action, or conclusion as following from what came before. Identify the connection that licenses that claim: a blocked route requires a detour, a rule determines an outcome, or a stated need prompts a response.

Draw the relationship in words: because of X, Y. Then test whether the passage actually supports that sentence. Explicit mechanisms, constraints, and stated decisions are useful evidence. Chronological order alone is insufficient. If a town changes its bus schedule and later records more library visits, the sequence does not establish that the schedule caused the increase.

Keep the direction straight. Evidence of a damaged cable might explain a machine's failure; the failure did not necessarily damage the cable. Also preserve uncertainty. A finding may support a cautious conclusion without proving a physical cause. When a study cannot separate two influences, a resulting decision to withhold a causal claim can be logical even though the claim itself would not be. In every case, connect the transition to the exact event or judgment that follows the blank, not to an attractive topic elsewhere in the passage.

W1.6: A closure creates a necessary response

Floodwater covered the only road leading to a field station, and officials closed that road to all vehicles. The station's supply truck could no longer reach its destination by land. ______, the delivery team transferred the urgently needed supplies to a boat that could reach the station from the river.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. Consequently

B. For instance

C. In other words

D. Similarly

Practice this lesson: 1.22, 1.23, 1.24.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.6

Answer: A

Completed text: Floodwater covered the only road leading to a field station, and officials closed that road to all vehicles. The station's supply truck could no longer reach its destination by land. Consequently, the delivery team transferred the urgently needed supplies to a boat that could reach the station from the river.

  1. State the constraint: the only road is closed, so the truck cannot complete the delivery by land. The need for supplies remains.
  2. State the response: the team switches the supplies to a boat that can use an available route. The change solves the transportation problem created by the closure.
  3. Choose 'Consequently' because the obstacle motivates the change in delivery method. This is a supported response, not merely an event that happens later.
  4. Check whether the final action merely restates the problem. It does not: a blocked truck and a transfer to a boat are distinct events, with the problem prompting the response.

Why B fails: 'For instance' would make transferring supplies to a boat an example of the truck's inability to arrive by land. The transfer is a response to that inability, not an instance of a truck failing to travel.

Why C fails: 'In other words' would equate a truck's inability to arrive by land with a team's transfer of supplies to a boat. The final sentence adds a new action taken to solve the earlier problem, not a restatement of that problem.

Why D fails: 'Similarly' would compare parallel actions or outcomes. A truck being blocked and a team choosing an accessible boat route have different roles in one cause-and-response sequence.

Key takeaway: A cause or constraint must support the particular response after the blank, and the transition must point in the correct direction.

1.7 Give an example or specify a general claim

A sentence can move from a broad claim to a case that makes the claim concrete. If the first sentence says that a technique has several uses and the next describes one use, an example transition fits. A sentence can also narrow a general reference by identifying exactly what it includes. If a project has one assigned task and the next sentence names that task, a specifying transition fits. In both moves, the second sentence supplies detail; it does not automatically oppose the first sentence or follow from it as an effect.

First, underline the broad category or general reference. Next, name the detail after the blank. Ask whether that detail is an instance within the category or the precise content of the earlier reference. Then read the whole completed passage. The detail must actually belong to the category: a fact about a building is not necessarily an example of the building's energy-saving features.

The boundary between illustration and specification is sometimes flexible. A named case can both illustrate a generalization and make it more specific. Do not invent a rigid distinction between "for example" and "specifically" if both would express the actual relationship. Compare the available choices and eliminate signals that misrepresent the content, rather than assuming a single label must describe every sentence.

A. On the contrary

B. In summary

C. For example

D. Alternatively

Practice this lesson: 1.25, 1.26, 1.27.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.7

Answer: C

Completed text: A surface can be shaped to direct water toward a chosen collection point. For example, a model rain collector built for a design class has shallow channels that converge on a single opening, guiding water into a container below the model. The channels demonstrate how the surface itself can control the direction of flow.

  1. Name the first proposition: the shape of a surface can direct water toward a collection point. This is a general statement about a design possibility.
  2. Name the second proposition: this particular model uses converging channels to send water to one opening. Its channels are a concrete instance of the principle.
  3. Insert "For example." The model now illustrates the broad claim, and the final sentence explicitly connects the channels to that claim.
  4. Check the closest rival, "In summary." The general claim has not already described the model, its channels, or its container. Introducing that new case does not summarize previously supplied details.

Why A fails: "On the contrary" announces that the next statement refutes an earlier statement. The channels support the claim that shape can direct water; they do not refute it.

Why B fails: "In summary" would make the model sound like a compressed account of details already given. The first sentence supplies a principle, and the next supplies new evidence in the form of a case.

Why D fails: "Alternatively" would offer a different method or choice in place of one already proposed. The general principle is not a competing collector design; this collector implements that principle.

Key takeaway: A concrete case belongs under a general statement. It need not replace, contradict, or result from that statement.

1.8 Restate or clarify the same idea

Clarification explains an existing idea in different terms. The second sentence may translate technical language, unpack a definition, or remove a possible misunderstanding. It should preserve the first sentence's central meaning and scope. A statement about every member of a group is not equivalent to a statement about one member; a possibility is not equivalent to a certainty. Familiar transition phrases cannot repair a change in the actual claim.

Use an equivalence check. Write a short paraphrase of the sentence before the blank, keeping any words such as "only," "may," or "under these conditions." Do the same for the sentence after the blank. If both paraphrases communicate the same criterion or assertion, try a clarifying phrase such as "in other words." If the second introduces a separate event, a new case, or a different explanation, investigate another relationship.

Clarification is not necessarily shorter than the original statement. It may be longer because it makes an implication of a definition explicit. Nor is every repeated topic a restatement: two sentences about the same experiment can report different findings. Correction can overlap with clarification when a writer replaces an inaccurate interpretation. Read whether the earlier interpretation was actually asserted, rejected, or merely mentioned before deciding what the transition must do.

W1.8: Translate a definition without changing it

For this experiment, the researchers define a sample as recovered when its mass returns to its starting value; its color is irrelevant to that definition. ______, a sample meets the researchers' recovery criterion as soon as its mass matches its original mass, even if its color remains different from its original color.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. In the meantime

B. In other words

C. In contrast

D. Alternatively

Practice this lesson: 1.28, 1.29, 1.30.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.8

Answer: B

Completed text: For this experiment, the researchers define a sample as recovered when its mass returns to its starting value; its color is irrelevant to that definition. In other words, a sample meets the researchers' recovery criterion as soon as its mass matches its original mass, even if its color remains different from its original color.

  1. Extract the rule: recovery requires the original mass, and color does not affect the classification. Keep both parts of the definition.
  2. Compare the next sentence: matching the original mass is sufficient even when color remains changed. This communicates the same rule in more accessible terms.
  3. Insert "In other words." The transition tells readers that the second sentence explains the definition rather than reporting another stage of the experiment.
  4. Check "Alternatively." The text does not offer a second recovery criterion. Both sentences use exactly the same mass criterion and exclude color from the decision.

Why A fails: "In the meantime" introduces a concurrent situation within an ongoing interval. The passage states a definition and explains it; it describes no separate activity or waiting period during which the criterion applies.

Why C fails: "In contrast" suggests that the second criterion differs from the first. Both require a return to starting mass and treat a remaining color change as irrelevant.

Why D fails: "Alternatively" suggests that researchers could use a different criterion instead. The second sentence restates their existing criterion and offers no alternative test.

Key takeaway: Match the conditions and qualifiers, not just the repeated nouns. A valid clarification keeps the same rule.

1.9 Track sequence and simultaneous events

Chronological transitions locate events relative to one another. "Afterward" moves to a later event; "previously" moves to an earlier one; "meanwhile" can mark events occurring during the same period. The order in which a passage mentions events may differ from the order in which those events happened. A writer can describe a completed object and then explain the earlier steps used to make it.

Build a tiny timeline before choosing. Identify the event that supplies the time reference, then place the event after the blank before it, after it, or during it. Use dates, times, completion words, and stated dependencies. "Once the coating had dried" puts the next action after drying. "While the coating dried" puts it during that interval. If another sentence interrupts the account, the relevant time reference may be farther back.

Do not turn sequence into cause. Two independently scheduled tasks can occur together, and one event can follow another without resulting from it. Conversely, a later step may genuinely depend on an earlier step. The passage must establish the relationship you choose. Also separate chronology from the sequence of an argument: "first" may introduce the first reason in a list rather than the earliest historical event. The surrounding nouns and verbs show what is being ordered.

B. Previously

C. On the contrary

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.9

Answer: D

Completed text: A lighting demonstration had two stages separated by a four-minute pause. At 3:10, a technician switched off the ceiling lamps, ending the first stage; no lights operated during the pause. Next, at 3:14, the technician switched on two spotlights to begin the second stage, which illuminated only a small part of the floor.

  1. Mark the reference event: the first stage ended at 3:10 when the ceiling lamps went off. The pause followed that event.
  2. Mark the event after the blank: the spotlights came on at 3:14, beginning the second stage. This occurs later, after the stated pause.
  3. Insert "Next." It advances the account to the next stage in the demonstration and agrees with both times.
  4. Test "Meanwhile" against the actual intervals. The spotlights did not operate during the first stage or during the pause, so simultaneous activity is excluded by the text.

Why A fails: "Meanwhile" places the spotlight event during an already described activity or interval. The text states that the first stage had ended and the dark pause was over before the spotlights came on.

Why B fails: "Previously" puts the 3:14 event before the 3:10 event. The stated times and stage numbers establish the opposite order.

Why C fails: "On the contrary" announces a refutation. Switching on spotlights after the pause does not contradict the earlier lamps being switched off or the pause remaining dark; those statements concern different times.

Key takeaway: Use the event times and intervals. The order of stages is stronger evidence than a transition that merely sounds smooth.

Practice this lesson: 1.31, 1.32, 1.33.

1.10 Replace, correct, or offer an alternative

Some transitions connect competing actions or interpretations. "Instead" often introduces an action that replaces another. "Rather" often supplies a correction to a rejected description. "Alternatively" can offer another available method without saying that the first method is wrong or abandoned. These are useful patterns, not rules that make the words mutually exclusive in every passage.

Identify the status of the first option. Is it still available, explicitly rejected, or shown to be inaccurate? Then identify what the second statement does with it. A team that cancels a coating plan and chooses replacement parts has substituted an action. A historian who rejects a date and supplies the corrected date has corrected a claim. A designer who describes two usable fastening methods has offered alternatives. Do not treat the second method as an example or paraphrase of the first merely because both achieve the same goal.

Look carefully at negative language. A sentence saying that an object "does not measure distance" has already rejected that function. A following statement about what it actually measures can complete the correction. But a sentence may simply explain a definition already stated correctly; then clarification may fit. Resolve the actual content and reference, and avoid assuming that every "not" automatically demands "rather."

Answer: A

Completed text: A repair team initially recommended sealing a cracked pipe with a protective coating. Tests then showed that the crack extended through the pipe wall, so the team withdrew its recommendation to use the coating. Instead, the team chose to replace the damaged section of pipe before restoring water service to the building.

  2. Identify the chosen action: replace the damaged section. This is the plan adopted in place of the abandoned coating plan.
  3. Insert "Instead." The transition accurately connects the rejected action with its replacement.

Key takeaway: Track whether the first idea survives. An abandoned plan followed by a new plan calls for a replacement relationship.

1.11 Distinguish inference, summary, and generalization

An inference states what follows from evidence or conditions. A summary collects earlier points into a shorter account. A generalization identifies a pattern across instances. These functions can overlap: a concluding sentence can both summarize findings and infer a broader pattern. Do not rely on a rule that "therefore" and "overall" can never fit the same passage. Judge what each offered choice would signal in this particular text.

Ask what changes after the blank. Does the sentence derive an unstated consequence from earlier facts? Does it compress several facts already supplied? Does it move from several cases to a claim about their shared feature? For an inference, write the missing reasoning step. For a summary, identify which earlier points it gathers. For a generalization, identify the exact group and conditions that the evidence covers.

Preserve the strength of the evidence. A complete record can support conclusions that an incomplete sample cannot. Three observations in one setting do not establish a universal law. A transition indicating conclusion does not make an overclaim valid, and "suggests" does not erase an unsupported change of population. If two relationship labels both seem reasonable, return to the actual options and check whether any alternative introduces opposition, chronology, or a concrete example that the final statement does not supply.

W1.11: Infer from an exhaustive schedule

The workshop schedule lists every tool required for every session. Hand drills appear only beside Thursday sessions, and participants need to bring only the tools required for sessions they attend. Mira has registered exclusively for Tuesday sessions. ______, Mira does not need to bring a hand drill to satisfy the workshop's tool requirements.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. Nonetheless

B. Therefore

C. In the meantime

D. Similarly

Practice this lesson: 1.37, 1.38, 1.39.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.11

Answer: B

Completed text: The workshop schedule lists every tool required for every session. Hand drills appear only beside Thursday sessions, and participants need to bring only the tools required for sessions they attend. Mira has registered exclusively for Tuesday sessions. Therefore, Mira does not need to bring a hand drill to satisfy the workshop's tool requirements.

  1. Collect all three relevant conditions: the tool list is complete, hand drills are required only on Thursday, and Mira attends only Tuesday sessions.
  2. Apply the participant rule. Mira must bring tools only for her own sessions, so a Thursday-only tool falls outside her requirements.
  3. Insert "Therefore." The final sentence is a conclusion derived from combining the rules with Mira's registration; it is not a new scheduling event.
  4. Test the apparent limitation: the conclusion concerns what Mira must bring, not what she owns or might choose to bring. The wording preserves that narrow scope.

Why A fails: "Nonetheless" would signal that Mira's lack of a hand-drill requirement runs against the earlier information. Her Tuesday registration and the Thursday-only drill rule directly support that conclusion.

Why C fails: "In the meantime" introduces a situation concurrent with another activity or interval. The passage lists rules and Mira's registration, then derives a requirement; it describes no parallel activity.

Why D fails: "Similarly" calls for a parallel case or comparable situation. No other participant's registration and tool requirements have been described; the text is reasoning from rules to a conclusion about Mira.

Key takeaway: A valid inference uses all necessary conditions and concludes only what those conditions establish.

1.12 Resolve scope and placement across the passage

A transition can connect to an earlier sentence, a group of sentences, or one important clause within a sentence. The closest noun is not necessarily its reference point. A passage may insert background between two compared findings, or place a favorable detail immediately before a decision that depends on a broader requirement. Read beyond the two words touching the blank.

Use a four-part procedure. State the passage's local question or goal. Label the job of each sentence: rule, evidence, background, example, qualification, or conclusion. Find which earlier proposition the sentence after the blank logically answers or develops. Then insert every option and describe its effect in plain language. For a contrast, identify the shared dimension; for a conclusion, name the evidence; for an example, locate the general claim.

A transition need not express the same relation to every earlier fact. A final decision can oppose the expectation created by one favorable result while remaining consistent with a rule stated earlier. Conversely, two editions of a book may differ on organization even when an intervening sentence discusses the cover. This lesson concerns interpreting the transition at its supplied location. You are not being asked to move sentences or apply old sentence-placement rules. Finish by checking the whole completed passage for one coherent line of reasoning.

  • Keep the subject, comparison dimension, time reference, and scope of each claim distinct.
  • Treat a useful prediction as provisional until all four options have been inserted.
  • Reject a choice for the relationship it falsely signals, not because its wording is less familiar.

W1.12: A favorable result does not settle the decision

To qualify as a replacement, a pump had to deliver at least 40 liters of water per minute and use less electricity than the existing pump. In testing, a proposed model used 20 percent less electricity than the existing one. ______, the engineering team rejected the model because it delivered only 32 liters per minute.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. In other words

B. Previously

C. Similarly

D. Even so

Practice this lesson: 1.40, 1.41, 1.42.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.12

Answer: D

Completed text: To qualify as a replacement, a pump had to deliver at least 40 liters of water per minute and use less electricity than the existing pump. In testing, a proposed model used 20 percent less electricity than the existing one. Even so, the engineering team rejected the model because it delivered only 32 liters per minute.

  1. Identify the whole-passage rule: the model must satisfy both a flow requirement and an electricity requirement. Meeting just one is insufficient.
  2. Read the sentence immediately before the blank. The electricity result is favorable and could create an expectation that the model will be accepted.
  3. Read the decision and its explanation. The team rejects the model despite that favorable result because the flow rate fails the other requirement.
  4. Insert "Even so." The transition concedes the electricity success while introducing the rejection. The full passage explains why the rejection remains consistent with the original two-part rule.

Why A fails: "In other words" would present rejection as a restatement of the electricity result or of the qualification rule. Rejection is a new decision based on the model's failure to satisfy the separate flow requirement.

Why B fails: "Previously" would place the rejection before the reported testing evidence. The passage presents the team's rejection as based on the model's measured flow rate and supplies no earlier rejection event.

Why C fails: "Similarly" would introduce another parallel success or comparable acceptance decision. The text shifts from a successful electricity result to rejection on another criterion.

Key takeaway: Connect the local contrast to the global rule. A result can be favorable without being sufficient.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

• Identify the full propositions being connected, including an earlier claim if the nearest sentence only supplies background or a method.

• Describe the relationship in ordinary language before selecting a transition, then check its direction and scope.

• Separate independent additions from parallel cases, examples, reformulations, replacements, and conclusions by examining what the new statement actually contributes.

• Preserve qualifications and distinctions: different facts are not automatically contrasts, sequence is not automatically causation, and an example is not automatically a generalization.

• Insert all plausible choices literally and reject each distractor for a relationship it falsely signals in this passage, not merely for having a less specific label.
