Building Vocabulary

Use context to find the right meaning, then practice retrieving it.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Infer the meaning a particular sentence requires before comparing answer choices.
  • Distinguish plausible words by their meaning, degree, and relationship to the evidence.
  • Build a manageable vocabulary routine using contexts, contrasting meanings, and retrieval.

5.1 Find the meaning the passage creates

Knowing a word's common definition is useful, but it does not settle its meaning in a passage. A familiar word can have an unfamiliar sense. Your first job is to identify what the word is doing here: describing a result, limiting a claim, explaining a cause, or naming a response.

Read the whole passage, including what follows the target word. Locate any definition, example, contrast, or consequence that restricts its meaning. Replace the word with an ordinary phrase. Then reread the sentence with that phrase in place. A successful replacement preserves the author's claim; it does not merely sound grammatical.

Context does not always reveal an exact dictionary definition. It can still narrow the meaning enough to decide between choices. Distinguish what the passage establishes from what you merely associate with its topic.

W5.1: A familiar word with an unfamiliar job

The chemist qualified her initial claim that the coating prevented corrosion: it protected the metal in dry air, she explained, but provided little protection in humid conditions.

As used in the text, what does 'qualified' most nearly mean?

A. Praised

B. Limited

C. Acquired

D. Explained

Practice this lesson: 5.05.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.1

Answer: B

  1. The original claim sounds general: the coating prevented corrosion. The explanation restricts that claim to dry air.
  2. Predict: 'added a condition to' or 'limited.' The colon introduces the evidence for this reading.
  3. Substitute 'limited her initial claim.' This preserves the change from a broad statement to a conditional one.

Why A fails: She identifies a limitation; she does not praise the claim.

Why C fails: The eligibility sense of 'qualify' does not fit something done to a claim.

Why D fails: She does explain the limitation, but 'limited' captures the specific effect on the original claim.

Key takeaway: Choose the meaning supported by the relationship between the ideas, not the first definition you remember.

5.2 Predict before comparing choices

Answer choices can pull your interpretation toward meanings the passage never supports. Before reading them, write or mentally supply a short, plain prediction. 'Not certain yet' is more useful than searching immediately for a sophisticated synonym.

Use three checks: direction (support, opposition, or qualification), degree (slight, substantial, or absolute), and relationship (cause, contrast, example, or result). A word can fit one check and fail another. Something uncertain is not necessarily false; something difficult is not necessarily impossible.

Your prediction is a working hypothesis. If none of the choices fits, reread and revise it. Do not force a choice to match your first impression. After selecting a word, read through the completed sentence and its neighboring sentences.

W5.2: Uncertain does not mean evasive

Although the recovered letters suggest that two architects collaborated, the archive is incomplete. The historian therefore describes the collaboration as a _____ conclusion, one that additional records might confirm or overturn.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word?

A. Definitive

B. Evasive

C. Tentative

D. Irrelevant

Practice this lesson: 5.01, 5.07.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.2

Answer: C

  1. Predict: 'possible, but not settled.' The incomplete archive limits certainty.
  2. 'Might confirm or overturn' is decisive. The historian treats the conclusion as open to revision.
  3. 'Tentative' preserves both the existing support and the remaining uncertainty.

Why A fails: 'Definitive' makes the conclusion final, contradicting the possibility of reversal.

Why B fails: 'Evasive' suggests avoiding an issue. The historian states the uncertainty directly.

Why D fails: The conclusion directly concerns the letters and the proposed collaboration.

Key takeaway: Predict a meaning and its strength. Do not turn limited evidence into either certainty or dismissal.

5.3 Learn multiple senses and useful word families

Learn flexible words through contrasting sentences. In 'a compelling account of the migration,' account means an explanation or description. In 'these sites account for half the observations,' account for means make up. In 'temperature differences account for the pattern,' it means explain.

Related forms also require attention. 'A qualified engineer' has appropriate credentials; 'qualified support' includes reservations. Notice the word's grammatical role and what accompanies it. The table is a small set of useful distinctions, not a list of meanings to substitute automatically.

Word or family

Contrast to learn

qualify / qualified

Qualify a claim: limit it. A qualified applicant: one who meets the relevant requirements.

concede / concession

Concede a point: acknowledge it, often reluctantly. A concession can also be something yielded.

sustain / sustained

Evidence sustains a claim: supports it. Sustain an effort: keep it going.

reserve / reserved

Reserve judgment: delay deciding. A reserved manner: restrained or uncommunicative.

arrest / arresting

Arrest deterioration: stop it. An arresting image: one that captures attention.

temper / temperate

Temper enthusiasm: moderate it. A temperate response: measured rather than extreme.

speculate / speculative

A speculative explanation is conjectural. A speculative investment pursues potential gains while accepting substantial uncertainty and risk.

W5.3: Use the object of the verb

The conservators could not reverse the paper's yellowing, but a protective enclosure arrested further deterioration.

What does 'arrested' mean here?

Practice this lesson: 5.02, 5.06.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.3

Answer: Stopped or halted.

  1. Find the object: the enclosure arrested 'further deterioration,' not a person.
  2. The contrast separates reversing past damage from stopping additional damage.
  3. Substitute 'halted further deterioration.' Do not weaken this to 'noticed' or strengthen it to 'repaired.'

Key takeaway: Record a word with its phrase and sense. 'Arrest deterioration' is more informative than 'arrest = stop.'

5.4 Separate near-synonyms by precision

Difficult vocabulary questions may use ordinary words whose meanings overlap. Compare the exact distinction the passage makes. Concise concerns few words; precise concerns exactness. Skeptical means doubtful; hostile means antagonistic. A response can be one without being the other.

Ask what each candidate would commit the author to saying. 'Unprecedented' requires no prior occurrence; 'unusual' requires only uncommonness. 'Refuted' means shown false; 'questioned' merely indicates doubt. Reject a stronger word when the passage supplies only weaker evidence.

Do not use positive or negative tone as your entire method. Tone may eliminate an option, but several remaining words can share that tone. The deciding clue may concern completeness, independence, permanence, or the source of a conclusion.

W5.4: Broad coverage versus useful accuracy

The catalog records every surviving object from the excavation, including unremarkable fragments omitted from earlier publications. Its coverage is _____.

Which choice completes the text most precisely?

A. Accurate

B. Comprehensive

C. Accessible

D. Innovative

Practice this lesson: 5.09, 5.10, 5.15.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.4

Answer: B

  1. Predict: 'complete in scope.' Underline 'every surviving object' and the inclusion of previously omitted fragments.
  2. The passage establishes breadth of coverage, not freedom from recording errors.
  3. 'Comprehensive' names the supported quality. A catalog can be comprehensive without being easy to use or methodologically novel.

Why A fails: 'Accurate' concerns correctness. Including every object does not establish that every description is correct.

Why C fails: Nothing establishes ease of access or understanding.

Why D fails: Adding omitted objects does not establish an innovative cataloging method.

Key takeaway: Ask which property the evidence proves. A generally favorable word is not necessarily the precise word.

5.5 Use word parts as clues, then verify

Prefixes, roots, and suffixes can suggest a meaning or grammatical role. Re- may suggest again; pre-, before; inter-, between; and -less, without. But apparent pieces are not always meaningful pieces, and meanings change over time. Treat a word-part guess as a candidate, not a verdict.

Context has the final vote. Invaluable means extremely valuable, not without value. Disinterested can mean impartial rather than bored. An unfamiliar word with a recognizable prefix still needs to fit the action, contrast, and degree in the sentence.

When studying, confirm uncertain meanings in a reliable dictionary and check an example of the relevant sense. During a question, use context and elimination. If only the word parts support your answer while the passage contradicts it, discard the answer.

W5.5: A useful hypothesis from roots

The exhibition is retrospective: it brings together paintings from the artist's earliest experiments through her final series, allowing visitors to trace the development of her career.

Infer the meaning of 'retrospective' and identify the stronger evidence for your answer.

Practice this lesson: 5.03.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.5

Answer: Looking back over earlier work or events; the career-spanning description confirms the meaning.

  1. 'Retro-' suggests backward or earlier. This gives a plausible direction, not a complete definition.
  2. The explanation after the colon supplies the decisive clue: work from the beginning through the end of a career.
  3. Do not infer 'old-fashioned.' The exhibition looks backward in time; the passage does not judge the style of the paintings.

Key takeaway: Let word parts generate a possibility. Require the sentence to confirm it.

5.6 Build memory through retrieval and transfer

Collect words that caused a mistake, hesitation, or imprecise reading. For each, record the source sentence, the relevant plain-language meaning, and one contrast that prevents confusion. A short, actively reviewed collection is more manageable than an ever-growing list you only reread.

Make two kinds of cards: sentence to meaning, and meaning to word. Answer before turning the card over. Then use the word in a fresh sentence. Check that your sentence shows its meaning: 'The result was tenuous' supplies less useful practice than a sentence explaining why the evidence was weak.

A practical starting schedule is a brief review the next day, about three days later, one week later, and two weeks later. Adapt the intervals: review missed items sooner and space consistently successful items farther apart. This is a workable routine, not a mandatory timetable. Mix old and new words, and occasionally test them in unfamiliar passages.

W5.6: A vocabulary card that trains meaning

The two surviving receipts cannot sustain the claim that the workshop supplied every theater in the city.

Create a context card, then demonstrate a different sense of the same word.

Practice this lesson: 5.04.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.6

Answer: Here, 'sustain' means support. In 'Donations sustained the workshop for another year,' it means kept going.

  1. Card front: copy the receipts sentence and ask, 'What does sustain mean, and which clue supports that reading?'
  2. Card back: 'Support with sufficient evidence; two receipts cannot justify a claim about every theater.'
  3. Transfer check: compare evidence supporting a claim with donations maintaining an operation. Retrieve both senses without looking.

Key takeaway: Knowing a word means recovering the appropriate sense in a new context, not recognizing a familiar card.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

  • Read through the context, predict a plain meaning, and check direction, degree, and logical relationship.
  • Treat multiple senses, nearby words, and grammatical roles as part of vocabulary knowledge.
  • Distinguish the exact property the evidence supports; verify word-part guesses against the sentence.
  • Review a small, useful collection through retrieval, spaced revisits, and fresh contexts.

Begin with four short drills, then complete twelve multiple-choice questions. Difficulty increases within the set. For every multiple-choice answer, identify a decisive clue and explain why the closest alternative fails.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 5.01

The director welcomed the unusually large audience, but the unexpected cost of renting extra equipment tempered her enthusiasm.

Replace 'tempered' with a plain phrase and identify the clue that rules out 'increased.'

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Predict a contextual meaning

Answer: Reduced or moderated; 'but' introduces the expense as a limit on her enthusiasm.

The audience creates enthusiasm; the unexpected expense makes that enthusiasm less strong. The passage does not say her enthusiasm disappeared entirely.

If you missed it: Name both the direction and degree: less enthusiastic, not necessarily no longer enthusiastic.

Drill 5.03

Although the notebook had little resale value, its record of the composer's revisions was invaluable to scholars reconstructing her working process.

A student thinks 'invaluable' means 'not valuable.' Correct the interpretation using the sentence rather than a memorized definition.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 5.03

Check word-part guesses

Answer: It means extremely valuable or useful. 'Although' contrasts little financial value with substantial scholarly value.

The revisions help scholars reconstruct a process. Treating both halves as statements of worthlessness would erase the stated contrast. The apparent negative prefix is misleading here.

If you missed it: Label the two kinds of value before interpreting the unfamiliar word.

Drill 5.04

The critic conceded that the novel's opening was slow, while maintaining that its final chapters justified the extended preparation.

Create a card defining 'conceded,' distinguishing it from 'retracted,' and giving a new sentence that demonstrates the meaning.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Build a retrieval card

Answer: Meaning: acknowledged a point, especially one favoring an opposing view. Contrast: 'retracted' means withdrew a previous statement. Sample: 'The coach conceded that the opponent defended well but attributed the loss primarily to missed shots.'

The critic accepts a weakness without withdrawing the favorable overall assessment. No earlier statement about the opening is identified as being withdrawn.

If you missed it: Include a contrast on the back of the card, then test the word in a new situation.
