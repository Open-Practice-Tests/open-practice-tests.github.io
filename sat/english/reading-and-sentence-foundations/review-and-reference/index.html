The five checks

Check

Ask yourself

Avoid

1. Structure

What is the main subject? What finite verb or verb phrase belongs to it? Which clauses are embedded?

Choosing the nearest noun or mistaking a participle for the main predicate.

2. Meaning

Can I restate the sentence while preserving contrast, conditions, and references?

Deleting a qualifier or assigning this to the nearest noun automatically.

3. Argument

Who makes the claim? What supports it? What does the evidence leave unresolved?

Confusing a cited view with the author's position or an association with a demonstrated cause.

4. Precision

Do direction, strength, scope, comparison, and time match?

Turning some into most, not all into none, or a requirement into a guarantee.

5. Vocabulary

What simple meaning fits the whole context? Why is the closest alternative worse?

Choosing a familiar definition or root-based guess without checking its fit.

Annotate only what helps

Underline a difficult main verb, mark a limitation, or write a short gist. On easier questions, do this mentally. Preserve meaning without marking every word.

A precise elimination routine

  • Name the task: grammar, paraphrase, inference, evidence, function, or vocabulary.
  • Predict the required meaning or structure before judging the choices when practical.
  • Test each plausible choice against the exact words of the passage. A choice can be true in general and still fail the question.
  • Reject an answer for a specific reason. Extreme words can be correct when the passage supports them; cautious wording can still express the wrong idea.
  • If two choices remain, find the difference between them and return to the part of the text that decides that difference.

Retest with transfer

After correcting a mistake, change one feature of the original sentence: replace some with all, reverse a comparison, move an only, or choose a different sense of a word. Explain how the correct interpretation changes. This checks understanding more effectively than memorizing an answer letter.

The historian qualified her claim: it applied to urban districts, not the entire region. What does qualified mean here?

Meaning: limited or added a condition to. Clue: urban districts, not the entire region. Contrast: qualified for a job = met its requirements. New sentence: The researcher qualified the conclusion by noting the small sample.

The practice includes a 10-question diagnostic, 16 items per chapter, and a 15-question cumulative review. Every multiple-choice practice item includes reasoning for the correct choice and all three alternatives.
