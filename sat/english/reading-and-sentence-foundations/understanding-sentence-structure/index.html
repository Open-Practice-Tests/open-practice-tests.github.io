1.1 Find the subject and the predicate

The subject identifies what the clause makes a statement about. The predicate says something about that subject; it includes the verb and the words completing or developing its meaning. When the subject is a noun phrase, its simple subject is the central noun or pronoun; the complete subject also includes its modifiers. A whole clause can also function as a subject, as you will see later in this chapter.

Ask two questions: What is the main verb saying happens or is true? What is doing that action or being described? With a linking verb, the predicate describes or identifies the subject: in 'The explanation seems plausible,' plausible describes the explanation. With an action verb, an object may receive the action: in 'The explanation challenges an assumption,' an assumption is the object.

A prepositional phrase often sits between the subject and verb: 'The pattern of changes is significant.' The subject is pattern, not the nearby plural noun changes. Bracket intervening material temporarily; return to it after locating the core.

W1.1: A subject hidden behind a long description

The discovery of several unusually narrow channels beneath the glacier has complicated the team's explanation of how meltwater reaches the coast.

What is the sentence's main statement?

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.1

Answer: The discovery has complicated the team's explanation.

  1. Locate the verb phrase has complicated. Ask what has complicated something: the discovery.
  2. Bracket of several unusually narrow channels beneath the glacier. This identifies the discovery; the channels are not the main subject.
  3. The object is the team's explanation of how meltwater reaches the coast. Keep enough of this object to understand what was complicated.
  4. The sentence reports a problem for an explanation. It does not say the explanation has been disproved.

Key takeaway: The nearest noun to a verb is not necessarily that verb's subject.

1.2 Recognize the whole verb phrase

Not every word that resembles a verb is the main verb. A finite verb anchors a clause grammatically. In ordinary statements, the finite form may be the main verb itself or an auxiliary or modal beginning a larger verb phrase. A nonfinite form, such as to examine, examining, or a participle such as examined, does not independently supply that anchor.

In has examined, has is finite and examined is a participle; together they form the verb phrase. In may have examined, the modal may begins the finite verb phrase. Do not search only for an obvious past-tense ending. Forms such as can and present-tense read also serve as finite verbs.

The same spelling can do different jobs. Compare 'Researchers tested the material' with 'The material tested yesterday was fragile.' The first tested anchors the clause; the second describes the material. Function in the sentence matters more than the ending.

W1.2: Three verb-like expressions, one main predicate

Researchers examining the fragments may have overlooked evidence preserved beneath a layer of paint.

Which words form the main verb phrase?

Practice this lesson: 1.02, 1.06.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.2

Answer: may have overlooked

  1. Researchers is the subject. Examining the fragments describes those researchers; it does not complete the main clause.
  2. May have overlooked forms the main verb phrase. Read the modal too: the sentence presents a possibility, not a confirmed oversight.
  3. Preserved beneath a layer of paint describes the evidence. Here, preserved is a nonfinite participle.
  4. A useful compressed reading is 'Researchers may have overlooked evidence.'

Key takeaway: Keep auxiliaries and modals with the verb; they can change the claim's meaning.

1.3 Distinguish phrases from clauses

A clause typically contains a subject and a predicate. A phrase functions as a unit within a clause. In this guide, concentrate on finite clauses: 'the storm ended' contains a subject and finite verb, whereas 'after the storm' is a prepositional phrase.

Different phrases do different jobs. Noun phrases name things; prepositional phrases locate, describe, or connect them; participial phrases often modify nouns; infinitive phrases can express purpose. Recognizing the job helps you attach information to the right word.

A useful question is What does this group of words modify or complete? In 'a report about erosion near the bridge,' about erosion identifies the report's topic. In 'the team worked near the bridge,' near the bridge locates the work. Do not treat every nearby phrase as part of the subject.

Some grammar frameworks classify certain nonfinite constructions as clauses. You do not need to resolve that terminology to answer SAT questions. The practical distinction is whether the construction supplies a finite predicate for the statement you are reading.

W1.3: A phrase becomes a dependent clause

After the storm ended, volunteers working near the river collected branches from the footbridge.

Separate the opening clause, the main clause, and the description of the volunteers.

Practice this lesson: 1.03, 1.09.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.3

Answer: Opening clause: after the storm ended. Main clause: volunteers working near the river collected branches from the footbridge. Volunteer description: working near the river.

  1. The storm ended contains a subject and finite verb. After makes that clause express a time relationship.
  2. Volunteers collected branches supplies the main statement.
  3. Working near the river modifies volunteers; it is not the main predicate.
  4. Compare after the storm: removing ended leaves a prepositional phrase, not the finite time clause.

Key takeaway: Look inside the word group; length alone does not distinguish a phrase from a clause.

1.4 Identify what dependent clauses do

An independent clause can stand as a sentence. A dependent clause cannot stand alone in its current form, even when it has a subject and finite verb. Dependent clauses can express circumstances, describe nouns, or occupy positions otherwise filled by noun phrases.

A circumstance clause may begin with although, because, if, or when. A relative clause describes a noun: 'the samples that the team collected.' A noun clause functions as a subject, object, or complement: 'What the team found surprised us' or 'The report explains why the samples differed.'

Introductory words are clues, not a substitute for analysis. A relative word can be omitted: 'the samples the team collected.' A content-clause that can also disappear: 'We know the samples differed.' Ask what role the clause plays. When a sentence contains several finite verbs, identify a subject for each one.

W1.4: Two embedded clauses with different jobs

The claim that the bridge is unsafe has alarmed residents who use it daily.

Which clause states the claim's content, and which describes the residents?

Practice this lesson: 1.08, 1.11, 1.14.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.4

Answer: 'That the bridge is unsafe' gives the claim's content; 'who use it daily' describes the residents.

  1. The main subject-verb pair is claim - has alarmed. The main sentence reports residents' reaction.
  2. That the bridge is unsafe explains what the claim says. It does not independently establish that the bridge is unsafe.
  3. Who use it daily identifies the residents. Who is the subject of use and refers to residents.
  4. Do not promote an embedded claim into a fact endorsed by the sentence's narrator.

Key takeaway: A sentence can report a claim without asserting that the claim is true.

1.5 Separate coordination from subordination

Coordination links units at the same grammatical level. These may be words, phrases, or clauses. In 'The team measured and compared the samples,' two verbs share one subject. In 'The team measured the samples, and the assistant recorded the results,' each coordinated clause has its own subject and predicate.

Subordination puts one clause inside a larger sentence as supporting information, a description, or a complement. 'Although the samples looked identical, their chemical compositions differed' presents the visual similarity as a concession and the chemical difference as the main assertion.

Follow both structure and meaning. And can connect two actions without proving that one caused the other. Although signals that the main point holds despite another fact. Because explicitly introduces a reason. These relationships will matter when you evaluate summaries and evidence.

Do not assume that everything after and starts a new independent clause. Identify exactly which units the conjunction connects.

W1.5: Two actions inside one larger contrast

Although the first design reduced weight and improved portability, the engineers rejected it because its joints loosened during repeated use.

What did the engineers decide, and how do the other clauses support that decision?

Practice this lesson: 1.10, 1.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.5

Answer: They rejected the design because its joints loosened, despite its weight and portability advantages.

  1. The opening dependent clause has one subject, the first design, and two coordinated verbs: reduced and improved.
  2. The independent clause supplies the decision: the engineers rejected it.
  3. The because clause gives the stated reason: the joints loosened during repeated use.
  4. Preserve the contrast. A summary mentioning only the advantages would miss the sentence's main assertion.

Key takeaway: Identify what is connected, then name the relationship between the ideas.

1.6 Test completeness and rebuild the meaning

A word group may contain several finite verbs and still be a fragment if all those verbs belong to dependent clauses. 'The instrument that the team developed after its funding increased' has developed and increased, but no main predicate for the instrument. Add 'proved reliable' to complete the sentence.

Use a repeatable reading routine: locate finite verb phrases; match them to subjects; mark dependent material; state the main assertion; restore any details that affect its meaning. This is a reading aid, not an instruction to delete every modifier. Words expressing negation, uncertainty, comparison, or a necessary condition must survive the reconstruction.

A main subject can itself be a whole clause. Read that clause as one unit before looking for its predicate. This prevents you from mistaking an embedded verb for the sentence's main verb.

W1.6: A whole clause serving as the main subject

That the two manuscripts contain the same unusual error does not establish that one was copied directly from the other.

What is the main subject, and what is the sentence saying about it?

Practice this lesson: 1.04, 1.12.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.6

Answer: Subject: that the two manuscripts contain the same unusual error. Main predicate: does not establish that one was copied directly from the other.

  1. First identify the three verb phrases: contain, does not establish, and was copied.
  2. The first that clause packages an observation as the main subject: the manuscripts share an unusual error.
  3. Does not establish states what that observation fails to prove.
  4. The final clause gives the unproven conclusion: direct copying. The sentence neither proves nor rules out copying.

Key takeaway: Simplify structure without strengthening the author's claim.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

  • Find each finite verb phrase and match it to its subject.
  • Identify the main clause before interpreting embedded descriptions and claims.
  • Track what conjunctions connect and the relationship they express.
  • Compress carefully: preserve negation, uncertainty, and details that limit the claim.

Drill 1.01

The catalog of regional folk songs includes several recordings from the 1930s.

Identify the simple subject, the main verb, and the complete object.

Subject and object

Answer: Subject: catalog. Verb: includes. Object: several recordings from the 1930s.

'Of regional folk songs' describes the catalog; 'songs' is not the main subject. 'From the 1930s' belongs with the recordings and specifies which recordings are included.

Answer: Main verb phrase: are testing. Purpose: to reduce glare. Coating description: designed for curved screens.

'Are' anchors the finite verb phrase 'are testing.' Neither the infinitive 'to reduce' nor the participle 'designed' provides the main predicate. The engineers are testing; they are not described here as the coating's designers.

Clause relationships

Answer: Independent: the town restricted outdoor watering. Concession: although the reservoir was nearly full. Reason: because a dry summer was expected.

All three clauses contain subjects and finite verb phrases. Only the middle clause stands independently in its current form. The town imposed restrictions despite current water levels because of the expected summer.

Drill 1.04

The species that thrive in shallow pools.

Explain why this is a fragment, then complete it by adding a main predicate.

Sentence completeness

Answer: It lacks a main predicate for 'the species.' One completion: 'The species that thrive in shallow pools can tolerate rapid temperature changes.'

'Thrive' is finite, but it belongs to the relative clause 'that thrive in shallow pools.' The added 'can tolerate' gives the whole sentence its main predicate. Other grammatical completions are possible.
