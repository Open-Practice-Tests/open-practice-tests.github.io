Unpacking Difficult Sentences

2.1 Find the sentence's main statement

A difficult sentence often contains a simple statement surrounded by explanations. Ask: Who or what is this mainly about, and what is said about it? Locate the main subject and the whole verb phrase making the main statement. Its finite element may be a main verb, an auxiliary such as has, or a modal such as may. Find what completes the statement. This structural starting point is not the final meaning.

Watch for competing verbs. In 'The report that the researchers revised challenges the assumption,' revised describes what happened to the report; challenges carries the main statement. An -ing word may describe an action without serving as the main verb: 'The researchers examining the samples disagree.' The main statement is 'The researchers disagree.'

A sentence may open with background information. Read past the opening until you find the clause making the central statement.

  • Find the subject and main verb together.
  • Complete the claim: 'The evidence suggests' needs the information that follows.
  • Restore surrounding information before deciding what the sentence means.

W2.1: Follow the verb that carries the claim

After several pages of commentary, the letter that the curator discovered among the painter's receipts reveals that the supposedly unfinished portrait had already been delivered.

What does the sentence mainly assert?

Practice this lesson: 2.01, 2.05.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.1

Answer: The letter shows that the portrait had already been delivered, despite being regarded as unfinished.

  1. Structure: opening phrase + 'the letter' + description + 'reveals' + the revealed information.
  2. Main subject and verb: 'the letter reveals.' 'Discovered' belongs to the description; 'had been delivered' belongs to the revealed claim.
  3. Complete skeleton: 'The letter reveals that the portrait had already been delivered.' Stopping at 'reveals' omits what the letter reveals: that the portrait had already been delivered.
  4. Restore the qualification: 'supposedly unfinished' reports a belief, not an established fact. Delivery does not independently prove completion.

Key takeaway: A useful skeleton includes the subject, main verb, and whatever information completes the central claim.

2.2 Unpack layers without deleting meaning

Descriptions can nest inside other descriptions. A relative clause describes a noun: 'the rooms that face the courtyard.' An appositive renames it: 'the annex, a converted workshop.' A prepositional phrase adds a relationship: 'the windows of the annex.' Attach each layer to what it describes.

Temporarily bracket; do not permanently discard. 'The rooms that face the courtyard are quiet' concerns only those rooms. Concluding that all rooms are quiet changes the claim. Even information enclosed by commas can be central to a question about cause, contrast, or evidence.

Ask separately: 'Can the sentence remain grammatical without this phrase?' and 'Would removing it change the answer?' Grammatically removable information can still be essential.

W2.2: Keep the restriction on the claim

The seedlings that grew beneath the cloth screens, a temporary substitute for the nursery's damaged roof panels, survived the cold night, whereas several seedlings in the uncovered beds did not.

Which details must a faithful paraphrase preserve?

Practice this lesson: 2.06, 2.13.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.2

Answer: The screened seedlings survived the cold night, while some seedlings in uncovered beds did not; the screens were a temporary replacement for damaged roof panels.

  1. Initial structure: 'The seedlings survived,' contrasted with 'several seedlings did not.'
  2. Restore the restriction: the first group grew beneath screens. Not every seedling survived.
  3. Attach the appositive: 'a temporary substitute' describes the screens, not the seedlings or the night.
  4. Preserve quantity and evidence: some uncovered seedlings died. The sentence neither says all died nor proves the screens caused the difference.

Key takeaway: A short paraphrase may omit background, but it must preserve which group the statement describes and what comparison it makes.

2.3 Resolve references by meaning, not distance

Words such as it, they, their, this, that, and such connect information. A reference can represent a noun, several nouns, an action, or an entire claim. The nearest noun is a candidate, not an automatic answer.

Check grammar (does number fit?), meaning (can this thing perform the action?), and continuity (does the passage remain coherent?). Substitute the candidate explicitly. If 'this' represents an event, replace it with an event description, not merely a nearby object.

Sometimes wording is ambiguous: 'Maya told Leila that her draft was ready' does not establish whose draft was ready. Use surrounding evidence; without it, do not manufacture certainty.

W2.3: Track an object and an entire event

The editor sent the poet's corrected pages to the printer only after comparing them with the original manuscript. This prevented an earlier version from being printed.

What do 'them' and 'This' refer to?

Practice this lesson: 2.02, 2.07, 2.08.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.3

Answer: 'Them' refers to the corrected pages. 'This' refers to checking those pages against the original manuscript before sending them to the printer.

  1. 'Them' means the corrected pages, which the editor compared with the manuscript. The nearby 'printer' is singular.
  2. 'This' means checking the pages before sending them. A manuscript alone does not describe the precaution.
  3. Faithful meaning: verification before forwarding the pages prevented use of an outdated version.
  4. Trap: 'sending pages' alone omits the check explaining why the mistake was avoided. Include enough of the event to preserve its role.

Key takeaway: Replace a reference with its proposed meaning and verify that the complete sentence still makes sense.

2.4 Preserve contrast, limits, and uncertainty

Small words control large claims. Although introduces a concession; but marks a turn; because supplies a reason; unless sets a condition. May, apparently, some, only, and not yet determine strength, quantity, or timing. Keep these controls when simplifying.

When clauses pull in different directions, name the relationship: 'A is true, but B limits what follows from A.' Keep both ideas. An encouraging opening does not erase a cautious conclusion.

'The delay may reflect a material shortage' offers a hypothesis, not a demonstrated cause. Similarly, failure to establish that a treatment helped does not establish that it failed.

W2.4: Keep a promising result separate from proof

Although the revised route shortened several deliveries, the trial did not establish that it would reduce the company's overall costs, which also depend on fuel use and staffing.

Give a concise paraphrase that preserves the result and its limit.

Practice this lesson: 2.03, 2.09, 2.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.4

Answer: The route shortened some deliveries, but the trial did not show that it would lower total costs, which also depend on fuel and staffing.

  1. Structure: 'Although [benefit], the trial did not establish [broader conclusion].' The final clause helps explain the uncertainty.
  2. Preserve quantity: 'several deliveries' means 'some,' not 'every delivery.'
  3. Preserve strength: unproved savings do not mean costs definitely increased or stayed unchanged.
  4. Trap: 'The route failed' erases its benefit. 'The route saved money' converts uncertainty into fact.

Key takeaway: Record both what the passage establishes and what it leaves unresolved.

2.5 Restore ordinary order in literary sentences

Literary prose may delay the subject, put a description first, or use older patterns. Restore ordinary subject-verb order: 'Beyond the orchard stood the house' means 'The house stood beyond the orchard.' Location comes first; the house remains the subject.

In passive voice, the subject receives the action: 'The letter was hidden by Nora' means 'Nora hid the letter.' Without 'by Nora,' the agent is unstated; do not invent one. 'Little did he suspect' means 'He scarcely suspected,' not 'He suspected something small.'

Then reread the original. Unusual order can emphasize place, surprise, or contrast. Decoding the literal meaning does not establish a character's unstated motives.

W2.5: Decode inversion and passive voice

At the end of the deserted platform stood Ada, her ticket folded twice in her glove. Not until the last carriage had passed was the letter drawn from her pocket.

What happens, and what remains unknown?

Practice this lesson: 2.04, 2.11, 2.12.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.5

Answer: Ada stands at the end of an otherwise empty platform, with a twice-folded ticket in her glove. The letter is taken from her pocket only after the final carriage passes; the passage does not explain her reason for waiting.

  1. Restore: 'Ada stood at the end of the deserted platform.' The ticket phrase describes her situation.
  2. Restore: 'The letter was not drawn from her pocket until the last carriage had passed.' 'Not ... until' fixes timing.
  3. The passive construction describes removal. Ada is the natural contextual candidate, but the sentence does not explicitly name an agent.
  4. Trap: a ticket and a passing train do not prove Ada missed her train. Her delay does not establish regret or secrecy.

Key takeaway: Translate unusual order into ordinary prose; keep observations separate from imagined explanations.

2.6 Paraphrase, then audit your paraphrase

A paraphrase should clarify meaning without changing it. State the literal claim in familiar words, then check who or what, action or relationship, logical connection, and limits. Shorter but less accurate is not better.

For poetry, reconstruct sentences across line breaks, which may interrupt a grammatical thought. Identify the literal scene before interpreting imagery. Ask what an image contributes locally; do not immediately search for a grand theme. An object's personal significance does not establish every emotion associated with it.

Audit both ways: Does the text support each part of your paraphrase? Does your paraphrase retain everything the question needs? Remove unsupported additions such as 'always,' 'deliberately,' or 'therefore.'

W2.6: Read through the line break

The latch still lifts beneath my thumb, though strangers tend the pear tree now; I pause, then leave the gate unturned, its welcome only in my mind.

Paraphrase the speaker's situation without adding a backstory.

Practice this lesson: 2.15, 2.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.6

Answer: The speaker can still lift the familiar latch, but strangers now tend the pear tree. The speaker pauses and does not open the gate, recognizing that the sense of welcome exists only in the speaker's mind.

  1. Literal sequence: the latch lifts; strangers now tend the tree; the speaker pauses and leaves the gate unopened.
  2. Read across lines: 'though' contrasts physical familiarity with changed circumstances. The final phrase qualifies the welcome.
  3. The familiar gate feels welcoming, but that feeling does not establish a present invitation.
  4. Trap: nothing says the speaker was expelled, the strangers are hostile, or a family member died. Those backstories exceed the wording.

Key takeaway: An accurate modest interpretation is stronger than an elaborate interpretation built on invented facts.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

  • Find the main subject and verb, then include the information needed to complete the claim.
  • Bracket complicated layers temporarily; restore restrictions, contrasts, and uncertainty before choosing an answer.
  • Resolve references by grammar, meaning, and continuity. A reference can represent an entire event.
  • Translate difficult wording into plain language, then check that every part of your paraphrase is supported.

Begin with four short drills, then complete twelve multiple-choice questions. Difficulty increases within the set. For every multiple-choice answer, identify a decisive clue and explain why the closest alternative fails.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 2.01

Despite the objections of several residents who attended the hearing, the proposal to convert the warehouse into studios received the council's approval.

Write the main subject, main verb, and complete central statement.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Recover the main statement

Answer: Subject: the proposal. Main verb: received. Central statement: The proposal to convert the warehouse into studios received the council's approval.

'Attended' describes residents. The objections contrast with approval; they did not prevent it. Retain the detail identifying which proposal passed.

If you missed it: Underline the main subject and verb; restore identifying details.
