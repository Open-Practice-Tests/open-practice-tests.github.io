2.1 Protect the grammatical core

Before choosing punctuation, find the sentence's main subject and finite verb. Then identify any object or complement the verb requires. These relationships are grammatical connections, not places to insert punctuation merely because the subject is long. In The collection of maps from several coastal villages reveals a changing shoreline, the subject is collection, and the verb is reveals. Everything between them helps identify the collection; the length of that material does not create a comma boundary.

The same protection applies inside a verb phrase, between a verb and its object, and between a preposition and its object. Compare has gradually expanded, examined the samples, and because of the weather. A colon cannot simply introduce whatever comes next: in ordinary sentence use, the material before a colon must form an independent clause. The collection includes: leaves includes waiting for its object.

A genuine supplement can interrupt a core connection: The collection, assembled over decades, reveals a changing shoreline. Both commas mark the inserted phrase; neither licenses an unrelated comma elsewhere. Your procedure is to locate the core, bracket any real insertion, and read the sentence with that insertion removed. Do not remove an identifying modifier and assume that its punctuation must therefore be optional.

The accuracy of the old measurements surprised the team.

W2.1: A long subject still needs its verb

An engineering team tested several designs for a small greenhouse. The arrangement of vents along the roof and near the side ______ the amount of air that could circulate without the use of a motor. The researchers kept the dimensions of the greenhouse constant throughout the tests.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. walls, influenced

B. walls; influenced

C. walls influenced

D. walls: influenced

Practice this lesson: 2.05, 2.06.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.1

Answer: C

Completed text: An engineering team tested several designs for a small greenhouse. The arrangement of vents along the roof and near the side walls influenced the amount of air that could circulate without the use of a motor. The researchers kept the dimensions of the greenhouse constant throughout the tests.

  1. The second sentence has the subject arrangement. The prepositional phrases of vents, along the roof, and near the side walls describe that arrangement.
  2. The finite verb is influenced, and its object is the amount of air that could circulate.... No supplementary phrase begins or ends at the blank.
  3. Choice C preserves The arrangement ... influenced the amount.... Read the entire sentence after insertion: the long subject connects directly to its verb.

Why A fails: The comma separates the complete subject from its verb. It does not close a supplement; none has been opened.

Why B fails: The semicolon leaves The arrangement of vents ... walls without a verb and influenced... without its own subject. It cannot join these two incomplete pieces.

Why D fails: The colon follows a subject phrase, not an independent clause. The material before it has no finite verb.

Key takeaway: A pause after a long subject is not a grammatical boundary.

2.2 Identifying and supplementary modifiers

An identifying modifier tells readers which person, object, or group the sentence means. A supplementary modifier adds information about a referent already identified. Compare The paintings that need repair are downstairs with The paintings, which need repair, are downstairs. The first identifies a subset of paintings by their condition. The second presents the need for repair as additional information about the paintings under discussion.

Use the surrounding text to decide what the writer is identifying. If the text distinguishes repaired paintings from damaged ones, punctuation must preserve that distinction. Do not infer the intended meaning solely from whether a sentence remains grammatical after deletion: both kinds of modifier may be removable grammatically while changing the intended reference. Also avoid the false rule that which always makes a clause supplementary. Meaning and structure decide its function; the presence of a particular relative word is not a complete punctuation test.

An internal supplementary clause normally needs both an opening and a closing mark. A relative clause can contain its own finite verb, so identify the main verb separately. In The journal, which contains detailed sketches, belongs to the archive, contains is inside the supplement; belongs completes the main clause. A missing closing comma can hide that main connection.

W2.2: A verb inside a relative clause is not the main verb

The archive owns one notebook from the expedition, and its first page identifies its author. The notebook, which includes sketches of several plants not described in the surviving ______ a record of the route the expedition followed. Conservators are comparing the sketches with modern photographs.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. letters, provides

B. letters provides

C. letters; provides

D. letters: provides

Practice this lesson: 2.07, 2.08.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.2

Answer: A

Completed text: The archive owns one notebook from the expedition, and its first page identifies its author. The notebook, which includes sketches of several plants not described in the surviving letters, provides a record of the route the expedition followed. Conservators are comparing the sketches with modern photographs.

  1. The main subject is The notebook. The opening comma introduces the supplementary relative clause beginning which includes.
  2. That relative clause ends after letters. The main verb, provides, must connect to The notebook, outside the relative clause.
  3. Choice A supplies the closing comma. Removing the supplement gives The notebook provides a record of the route the expedition followed. The identified notebook remains the subject.

Why B fails: Without the closing comma, the supplementary clause is not properly closed before the main verb provides. The opening comma still requires its corresponding boundary.

Why C fails: The semicolon does not close the comma-marked supplement. It also puts provides a record..., which lacks its own subject, after a semicolon.

Why D fails: The colon does not close the supplementary relative clause and follows material whose main subject has not yet received its main verb.

Key takeaway: Find the end of the relative clause, then reconnect the main subject to the main verb.

2.3 Appositives, titles, and names

An appositive is a noun or noun phrase that renames another noun. In Leah Ortiz, a textile conservator, examined the cloth, the phrase a textile conservator renames Leah Ortiz. Because it is supplementary and occurs inside the sentence, commas enclose it. If a supplementary appositive comes at the end, the sentence's final punctuation ends it; no extra closing comma is needed.

A name is not automatically an appositive requiring commas. The composer Mina Shah can work as one identifying noun phrase: the name tells readers which composer. Conversely, The festival's only composer, Mina Shah, ... presents a uniquely identified role followed by a supplementary name. Do not apply a blanket rule that all names take commas or that no name ever does. Read the whole noun phrase and the context that establishes its reference.

Titles behave similarly. Her first collection, Winter Windows, won the prize presents a title as supplementary information about an already identified collection. In a question, first decide what the phrase renames. Then locate where the renaming phrase ends, and check that punctuation does not separate a title from a required part of its own phrase or separate the complete subject from its verb.

  • Name within an identifying phrase: The historian Mara Bell compared the records.
  • Supplementary role after an identified name: Mara Bell, a historian of trade, compared the records.
  • Context matters: a title, job label, or name does not determine punctuation by itself.

W2.3: Close a title appositive before the main verb

Poet Lena Imani published several collections over a long career. Her first collection, ______ its images of crowded markets with unusually quiet scenes of domestic life. Later collections developed the same contrast in longer narrative poems.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. Morning Windows; combined

B. Morning Windows combined

C. Morning Windows: combined

D. Morning Windows, combined

Practice this lesson: 2.09, 2.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.3

Answer: D

Completed text: Poet Lena Imani published several collections over a long career. Her first collection, Morning Windows, combined its images of crowded markets with unusually quiet scenes of domestic life. Later collections developed the same contrast in longer narrative poems.

  1. The subject is Her first collection. The opening comma signals a supplementary appositive giving that collection's title.
  2. The title ends before combined. That verb belongs to the main clause: Her first collection combined its images....
  3. Choice D closes the title appositive with a comma. The resulting sentence preserves both the supplementary title and the subject-verb connection.

Why A fails: A semicolon cannot replace the closing comma here. The material after it begins with combined and has no separate subject.

Why B fails: The title appositive has an opening comma but no closing comma before the main verb. The punctuation leaves its boundary unmarked.

Why C fails: A colon is not the matching close for the comma-marked title and cannot divide the subject and its main verb.

Key takeaway: Identify the noun being renamed, then mark the complete renaming phrase.

2.4 Matched punctuation and the sentence underneath

Commas, em dashes, and parentheses can enclose an internal supplement. Each creates an opening and a closing boundary. If the punctuation already printed in the text opens parentheses, the completion must respect that structure. If a pair of em dashes encloses an interruption, replacing only one dash with a comma generally leaves an inconsistent boundary rather than creating a new grammatical sentence.

The useful check is not merely to count marks. Identify exactly what each pair encloses, then temporarily remove that material and read the remaining sentence. In The mechanism—small enough to fit inside a button—released the dye, the underlying sentence is The mechanism released the dye. A comma after the second dash would incorrectly separate that subject from its verb.

Not every dash needs a partner. A dash can introduce an explanation at the end of an independent clause; a sentence can also end with a supplement. Parentheses, however, always require a closing parenthesis. Nor must every mark in a long sentence match its nearest neighbor: parentheses may appear within a comma-marked phrase. Match boundaries by grammatical role. When alternatives offer different complete punctuation systems, more than one can be grammatical; valid questions give additional constraints that determine one answer.

W2.4: Match the given closing dash

The museum needed a material that would protect a fragile surface without leaving residue. The new ______ thin layer made from a water-soluble polymer—protected the painted surface during transport and could later be removed. Conservators tested the method on sample panels before using it on the original object.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. coating, a

B. coating—a

C. coating; a

D. coating: a

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.4

Answer: B

Completed text: The museum needed a material that would protect a fragile surface without leaving residue. The new coating—a thin layer made from a water-soluble polymer—protected the painted surface during transport and could later be removed. Conservators tested the method on sample panels before using it on the original object.

  1. The main subject will be The new coating, and the main verb is protected. The noun phrase between them renames the coating.
  2. The appositive a thin layer made from a water-soluble polymer ends at the supplied em dash. Its opening boundary must be an em dash as well.
  3. Choice B gives a paired interruption. Removing it leaves The new coating protected the painted surface during transport and could later be removed.

Why A fails: The comma opens the appositive, but the supplied em dash does not match that opening boundary. The fixed sentence requires the pair of dashes.

Why C fails: The semicolon separates the subject The new coating from the rest of its clause. The words before it do not form an independent clause.

Why D fails: The colon follows only the subject The new coating, not an independent clause, and does not form the required pair with the closing dash.

Key takeaway: Use the fixed punctuation as a constraint, then verify the underlying sentence.

2.5 Introductions and supplements at the edges

An introductory adverbial dependent clause sets up a condition, concession, time, or reason before the main clause. In Although the original labels had faded, the accession numbers remained readable, although makes the opening clause dependent. A comma marks its boundary with the main clause. A semicolon or period would leave the introductory dependent clause stranded.

Introductory nonfinite phrases also need careful handling. After comparing the two independent sets of measurements, the researchers revised the estimate begins with a phrase rather than an independent clause. Locate the end of the full introductory element before placing its comma. Do not insert the comma after only part of the phrase, such as between comparing and its object. Modifier attachment is a separate check: the subject after an introductory participial phrase must match what the phrase describes: the actor for an active phrase or the entity affected for a passive phrase.

At the other end of a sentence, a supplementary phrase may add a result or description: The sample fractured, leaving a visible gap. However, a modifier can also be integrated into the sentence's meaning. Position alone does not settle punctuation. Short introductory phrases and some trailing modifiers allow punctuation variation; do not manufacture an error from an optional comma. Focus on clear clause boundaries, required phrase connections, and the intended grammatical relationship.

W2.5: Finish the entire introductory clause

Although the first set of measurements from the instruments mounted along the southern ______ the research team obtained enough reliable readings to estimate the direction of the current. A second set of instruments was installed the following month.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. pier proved unreliable;

B. pier proved unreliable.

C. pier proved unreliable,

D. pier proved unreliable:

Practice this lesson: 2.13, 2.14.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.5

Answer: C

Completed text: Although the first set of measurements from the instruments mounted along the southern pier proved unreliable, the research team obtained enough reliable readings to estimate the direction of the current. A second set of instruments was installed the following month.

  1. The introductory clause begins with Although. Its subject is the first set of measurements, and its finite verb is proved, followed by the complement unreliable. The prepositional material belongs inside that clause.
  2. The main clause begins with the research team and has the verb obtained. The dependent introductory clause must connect to that complete main clause.
  3. Choice C places the comma after the whole introductory clause. The sentence contrasts the unreliable first measurements with the team's success in obtaining enough other readings.

Why A fails: The words before the semicolon form an although clause, not an independent clause. A semicolon cannot make that dependent clause stand alone.

Why B fails: The period strands the introductory although clause as a sentence fragment. The following lowercase the also cannot begin a newly punctuated sentence here.

Why D fails: The colon follows a dependent clause. The clause before a colon must be independent in this ordinary sentence construction.

Key takeaway: An introductory clause ends after its complete predicate, not after its longest noun phrase.

2.6 Series and the different jobs of punctuation

A series places parallel items in a shared grammatical role. The items may be nouns, phrases, or clauses: cataloged the paintings, photographed the frames, and recorded the dimensions contains three coordinated verb phrases. Identify the items before judging the marks. A comma belongs between items, not between a verb and the object inside one item.

When list items already contain commas, semicolons can make the larger divisions clear: The panel included Ana Liu, a chemist; Leo Bell, a sculptor; and Nia Rao, an engineer. The commas attach roles to names, while the semicolons separate the larger items. Do not treat a semicolon inside such a list as though it were joining independent clauses. Conversely, do not assume every list needs semicolons. Some complex lists can be written grammatically in more than one way; a question must provide constraints beyond a preference for clearer style.

A colon can introduce a list after an independent clause: The panel included three specialists: is complete, whereas The panel included: is not. The serial comma is not a useful sole deciding factor here because conventions vary. Coordinate adjectives sometimes form a small series, but the stronger SAT skill is identifying which words form each grammatical unit and which marks separate whole units.

Within one list item

Commas may enclose a supplementary role or description.

Between complex list items

Semicolons can show where one whole item ends and the next begins.

Before an explanatory list

A colon can follow a complete independent clause.

W2.6: A complete clause can introduce a complex list

The restoration project brought together three ______ Ana Morel, a specialist in metalwork; Noor Patel, a textile conservator; and Jules Bell, a researcher who studies early pigments. Each expert examined a different part of the object before the team agreed on a treatment plan.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. specialists:

B. specialists;

C. specialists.

D. specialists, including:

Practice this lesson: 2.15, 2.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.6

Answer: A

Completed text: The restoration project brought together three specialists: Ana Morel, a specialist in metalwork; Noor Patel, a textile conservator; and Jules Bell, a researcher who studies early pigments. Each expert examined a different part of the object before the team agreed on a treatment plan.

  1. The words The restoration project brought together three specialists form an independent clause. The phrase three specialists completes the verb phrase brought together.
  2. The following list identifies the three specialists. Each name has supplementary descriptive material, so semicolons separate the larger list items while commas work within those items.
  3. Choice A introduces that explanatory list with a colon. Check each level separately: complete clause, colon, then three parallel name-and-description items.

Why B fails: The material after the semicolon is a list of names and noun descriptions, not an independent clause. This semicolon is outside the internal list divisions and cannot introduce the list.

Why C fails: The period leaves the list as a sentence fragment: the names and their descriptions do not supply a main predicate.

Why D fails: The colon splits including from the names it introduces. In this choice, including is a preposition whose object is the list; punctuation cannot divide that preposition-object connection.

Key takeaway: A semicolon separating list items has a different job from a semicolon separating clauses.

2.7 Transitions inside clauses and between clauses

Transitions such as however, therefore, and for example express relationships, but they do not function as coordinating conjunctions. At a boundary between independent clauses, however cannot turn a comma splice into a complete sentence. Compare The first trial failed; however, the second trial succeeded. The semicolon separates independent clauses; the following comma sets off the transition.

Inside a clause, a transition can interrupt a grammatical connection: The second trial, however, succeeded. Here the subject is The second trial, and the verb is succeeded. The two commas enclose the interruption. Replacing the first comma with a semicolon would strand the subject before that semicolon and the predicate after it. A transition's familiar appearance does not determine its punctuation.

Use a three-part check. First, remove the transition temporarily. Second, decide whether the underlying structure contains one clause or two independent clauses. Third, restore punctuation that marks both the clause structure and the transition's position. Some words have other grammatical uses: however carefully can mean no matter how carefully, for example. Read the phrase's actual role rather than treating every occurrence of a familiar word as the same interruption.

W2.7: However does not create a clause boundary

The early surveys suggested that the reservoir was too shallow to retain much water during the dry season. Measurements from the deeper basin, ______ that the reservoir could store considerably more water than the surveys had indicated. The engineers revised their estimate after reviewing the new measurements.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. however; showed

B. however: showed

C. however showed

D. however, showed

Practice this lesson: 2.17, 2.18.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.7

Answer: D

Completed text: The early surveys suggested that the reservoir was too shallow to retain much water during the dry season. Measurements from the deeper basin, however, showed that the reservoir could store considerably more water than the surveys had indicated. The engineers revised their estimate after reviewing the new measurements.

  1. The main subject in the second sentence is Measurements from the deeper basin. Its verb is showed. This is one main clause, not two clauses separated by a transition.
  2. The printed comma before the blank opens the interruption however. A second comma must close it before the main verb.
  3. Choice D yields Measurements from the deeper basin, however, showed.... Without the transition, the core is Measurements ... showed that....

Why A fails: The semicolon does not close the comma-marked interruption and separates the subject from its verb. Showed... has no independent subject after that boundary.

Why B fails: The colon separates the subject and its verb; the words before it do not form a complete independent clause. It also fails to close the interruption consistently.

Why C fails: This choice omits the closing comma for however. The supplied opening comma marks a parenthetical transition, so its internal boundary must be completed.

Key takeaway: Determine the number of clauses before choosing punctuation around a transition.

2.8 Several structures in one sentence

Hard punctuation questions often place several familiar structures inside one another. A subject may contain a relative clause; that clause may contain a supplement; the complete subject may then be followed by its main verb. The solution is to work from the outside inward. First find the main clause. Next identify each inserted or modifying unit. Finally assign each punctuation mark to the unit it opens, closes, or separates.

Write a very short skeleton when necessary: The records reveal a pattern. Then restore one layer at a time: The records from the station reveal a pattern; The records from the station (including the winter logs) reveal a pattern. The parenthetical words do not require a new comma between logs) and reveal. Closing an insertion returns you to the original sentence; it does not automatically create another boundary.

Be especially careful with a mark that does two different jobs in nearby places. A semicolon may separate list items in one part of the sentence and independent clauses in another. Two commas may belong to the same supplement even when a parenthetical phrase lies between them. Do not reject a choice because the sentence is long or unfamiliar. Insert it literally, read the full sentence, and test the specific grammatical connections. Every required boundary must work at the same time.

W2.8: Close the interruption without breaking the core

The two instruments—a sensor that recorded changes in air pressure and a camera that photographed a reference scale at regular ______ independently throughout the trial. Their separate records allowed researchers to compare the timing of pressure changes with the movement visible in the photographs.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. intervals—, operated

B. intervals—operated

C. intervals; operated

D. intervals, operated

Practice this lesson: 2.19, 2.20.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.8

Answer: B

Completed text: The two instruments—a sensor that recorded changes in air pressure and a camera that photographed a reference scale at regular intervals—operated independently throughout the trial. Their separate records allowed researchers to compare the timing of pressure changes with the movement visible in the photographs.

  1. The main subject is The two instruments, and the main verb is operated. The two noun phrases between them explain which instruments the passage means.
  2. The printed em dash opens that supplementary explanation. Its internal relative clauses, beginning that recorded and that photographed, belong to the two instrument descriptions.
  3. Choice B closes the explanation with an em dash and directly resumes the main predicate. Removing the explanation yields The two instruments operated independently throughout the trial.
  4. Choice A looks similar, but its extra comma remains after the supplement is removed: The two instruments, operated.... That is the decisive additional error.

Why A fails: The closing em dash is correct, but the extra comma then separates the subject The two instruments from its main verb operated. No further supplement explains that comma.

Why C fails: The semicolon cannot close the dash-marked explanation. The remaining predicate operated independently... also lacks a separate subject after the semicolon.

Why D fails: The comma fails to match the opening em dash. The long explanation needs its closing dash before the main predicate resumes.

Key takeaway: A choice must satisfy every boundary, including what happens immediately after an insertion closes.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

• Protect subject-verb, verb-object, auxiliary-verb, and preposition-object connections unless a complete, properly marked supplement intervenes.

• Use context to determine whether a modifier identifies its referent or adds supplementary information.

• Match the punctuation around an internal supplement; then read the remaining sentence as a whole.

• Classify an introductory element and a transition by their grammatical roles before choosing punctuation.

• For complex sentences and lists, label the different levels of structure and verify the literal completed text.

Begin with four short reasoning drills, then complete sixteen multiple-choice questions. For each answer, identify the decisive rule and the flaw in the nearest competitor. Some drills admit more than one valid repair; explain why your revision works.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 2.01

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Find an unjustified break in the core | Review lesson 2.1

Answer: Main subject: location. Main finite verb: suggests. Corrected sentence: The location of the small marks near the edges of the earliest clay tablets suggests that the marks were added before the tablets dried.

The prepositional phrases identify the location under discussion. They do not form a comma-marked supplement. The comma after tablets therefore separates the full subject from suggests. The dependent that clause is the content of what the location suggests; it does not alter that main connection.

Explain the effect of modifier punctuation | Review lesson 2.2

Answer: Version 1 identifies the discarded subset as the samples that had cracked; it preserves the contrast with intact samples. Version 2 presents cracking as supplementary information about the samples under discussion and says those samples were discarded.

The difference concerns reference and scope, not simply whether a clause can be removed without destroying the sentence. In Version 1, the modifier performs the identifying work. Version 2 treats the group as already identified. Neither sentence is inherently ungrammatical; the surrounding context determines which meaning is intended.

If you missed it: Ask which group the noun phrase refers to, and paraphrase that group before selecting punctuation.

Drill 2.04

Version 1: The original estimate, however, remained unchanged. Version 2: The calculations were repeated; however, the original estimate remained unchanged.

Explain why the first version uses two commas around however while the second uses a semicolon before it.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 2.04

Locate a transition relative to the clauses | Review lesson 2.7

Answer: Version 1 has one main clause, The original estimate remained unchanged; however interrupts it between subject and verb. Version 2 has two independent clauses, The calculations were repeated and the original estimate remained unchanged; the semicolon separates them, and the comma sets off the transition at the start of the second clause.

The transition expresses a contrast in both versions, but its location in the grammar differs. Neither the meaning of however nor a remembered pattern such as semicolon + however is enough to decide the punctuation. The clauses on each side control the boundary.
