1.1 Find the main clause, not merely a verb

An independent clause has a subject and a finite verb and can function as a complete sentence. In the machine runs, runs is finite. In the machine was running and the machine has stopped, was and has are finite auxiliaries; running and stopped are nonfinite participles. Each complete verb phrase is finite because it contains a finite verb. A phrase such as running at low speed does not, by itself, supply a complete sentence.

A sentence can contain a finite verb without having a complete main clause. In The machine that runs at low speed, the verb runs belongs to the relative clause beginning with that. The main subject, machine, still needs its own predicate: The machine that runs at low speed produces little noise. Treat a long subject as a unit until you reach its main verb.

At a proposed boundary, temporarily bracket descriptions and relative clauses. Read the remaining subject-predicate structure. A fragment lacks a complete main clause; a fused sentence runs independent clauses together; a comma splice joins independent clauses with only a comma. Length and pauses do not identify these errors. Short strings can contain two clauses, while a long string can remain one unfinished subject.

Text

Diagnosis

The records that survived the flood

A subject with a relative clause; no main predicate yet.

The records that survived the flood were restored.

Complete main clause: records were restored.

The records survived, the labels dissolved.

Two main clauses joined with only a comma.

W1.1: A verb inside a description

A museum is studying scraps of cloth found beneath a collapsed roof. The textile fragments that survived the unusually damp burial ______ a rare record of the dyes available to local weavers. Researchers will compare the colors with those on pottery from the same site.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. period, provide

B. period provide

C. period. Provide

D. period; provide

Practice this lesson: 1.05, 1.06.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.1

Answer: B

Completed text: A museum is studying scraps of cloth found beneath a collapsed roof. The textile fragments that survived the unusually damp burial period provide a rare record of the dyes available to local weavers. Researchers will compare the colors with those on pottery from the same site.

  1. Locate the full subject: "The textile fragments that survived the unusually damp burial period." The word "survived" belongs to the relative clause describing the fragments.
  2. Find the main predicate: "provide a rare record." The core is "The textile fragments provide a rare record." No sentence boundary belongs between that subject and its predicate.
  3. Insert B. The first sentence supplies background; the second now has its own main subject and finite verb. The following sentence is already complete.

Why A fails: The comma separates the main subject, fragments, from its main verb, provide. It is not closing a separately opened parenthetical insertion.

Why C fails: The period leaves "The textile fragments that survived ... period" without a main predicate. "Provide a rare record ..." also lacks a stated subject in this descriptive context.

Why D fails: The material before the semicolon is an unfinished main-clause subject. A relative-clause verb does not make that larger unit independent.

Key takeaway: When a verb follows that, which, or who, check whether it belongs to a description rather than to the main clause.

1.2 Coordinate clauses and recognize a shared subject

The coordinating conjunctions for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so can connect independent clauses. The usual SAT pattern is independent clause, conjunction independent clause: The sensor failed, but the backup recorded the event. Each side has its own subject and finite predicate. The conjunction expresses the relationship; the comma marks the join.

The same conjunctions can connect smaller units. In The backup recorded the event and transmitted the data, one subject governs two predicates. The second predicate is not a separate independent clause with its own stated subject. A semicolon before transmitted would therefore be wrong. Normally, do not add a comma merely because a shared predicate is long. Published prose sometimes uses a comma for clarity or emphasis; do not turn the useful default into a claim that every comma before a compound predicate is impossible.

Your procedure is structural: identify what the conjunction joins, then test the punctuation at that level. A conjunction may also occur inside a description or list without connecting the two main clauses. Reading only the nearest and or but can hide a comma splice elsewhere in the sentence.

Pattern

What is joined

The team revised and retested the device.

Two verbs with one subject.

The team revised the device, and a second group retested it.

Two independent clauses.

The team revised the device; a second group retested it.

Two independent clauses joined by a semicolon.

W1.2: Two actions, two stated subjects

The engineer wanted to distinguish a faulty sensor from a change in air pressure. She first measured the pressure inside the ______ she adjusted the sensor without opening the chamber. Comparing the two readings allowed her to evaluate the sensor under stable conditions.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. chamber, and

B. chamber and,

C. chamber,

D. chamber; whereas

Practice this lesson: 1.07, 1.08.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.2

Answer: A

Completed text: The engineer wanted to distinguish a faulty sensor from a change in air pressure. She first measured the pressure inside the chamber, and she adjusted the sensor without opening the chamber. Comparing the two readings allowed her to evaluate the sensor under stable conditions.

  1. The first clause is "She first measured the pressure inside the chamber." The second is "she adjusted the sensor without opening the chamber." Both contain a stated subject and a finite verb.
  2. The intended relationship is addition or sequence. Choice A supplies the coordinating conjunction "and" after a comma, producing the standard join between the two clauses.
  3. Check both sides of the join after insertion. The comma closes the first clause, and "and" introduces the second; it is not separated from "she adjusted" by an intrusive comma.

Why B fails: The comma after and improperly detaches the conjunction from the clause it introduces. It does not supply the normal boundary before the conjunction.

Why C fails: A comma alone joins the independent clauses "She first measured ..." and "she adjusted ...," creating a comma splice.

Why D fails: Whereas makes the following clause dependent. A clause-joining semicolon cannot attach that dependent clause to the preceding independent clause.

Key takeaway: Locate the subject after the conjunction. A second stated subject often signals a new clause; a second verb alone may share the first subject.

1.3 Use periods and semicolons only at complete-clause boundaries

A period ends a sentence. A semicolon can join closely related independent clauses without a coordinating conjunction: The first sample was contaminated; the second remained usable. In this ordinary clause-joining use, both sides must be complete. A semicolon is not simply a stronger comma or a signal that a sentence has become long.

To test the right side, read through its descriptions until you find the main predicate. The second sample, stored in a sealed container, remained usable is complete. The second sample, stored in a sealed container is not. To test the left side, make sure its finite verb is not trapped inside a relative or dependent clause.

A correctly capitalized period and a semicolon often both work between the same independent clauses. Do not invent a grammar distinction based only on how dramatic the pause feels. If two options appear equivalent, compare every character: capitalization, an added conjunction, a missing subject, or punctuation elsewhere may distinguish them. A semicolon also separates complex list items in another use, covered in Chapter 2. Do not apply the two-clause test to a semicolon that actually divides items in such a list.

W1.3: Look past the inserted description

A literary historian found several unsigned reviews in an old newspaper. The account that most clearly described the performance circulated ______ author, despite repeated requests from the editor, remained anonymous. The historian therefore compared the review with other documents before proposing an attribution.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. widely, its

B. widely its

C. widely; its

D. widely. Although its

Practice this lesson: 1.09, 1.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.3

Answer: C

Completed text: A literary historian found several unsigned reviews in an old newspaper. The account that most clearly described the performance circulated widely; its author, despite repeated requests from the editor, remained anonymous. The historian therefore compared the review with other documents before proposing an attribution.

  1. Identify the main subject and verb on each side. The relative clause "that most clearly described the performance" modifies account; it does not supply the main verb of that clause.
  2. The left main clause is "The account ... circulated widely." The right main clause is "its author ... remained anonymous." The phrases between those core elements do not change their completeness.
  3. A semicolon can connect those independent clauses. Choice C supplies that boundary without an unnecessary conjunction or a comma splice.

Why A fails: The comma alone would connect the two independent main clauses, producing a comma splice.

Why B fails: Without punctuation or a conjunction, the independent clauses run together.

Why D fails: Although subordinates the entire new sentence about the author. With no main clause following it before the period, that sentence is a fragment.

Key takeaway: Test the main clause on each side, not just the few words immediately touching the punctuation.

1.4 Keep subordinate clauses attached

Subordinating words such as although, because, if, unless, while, when, and since can make a clause dependent. Although the sample was small contains a subject and a finite verb, but it cannot stand alone as a complete sentence in this use. It needs a main clause: Although the sample was small, the pattern was clear.

When an introductory adverbial dependent clause comes before its main clause, a comma normally marks the boundary between them. This rule does not apply to a noun clause functioning as the subject: What the team discovered changed the plan takes no comma between its subject and verb. A period or clause-joining semicolon cannot repair that boundary, because the introductory material is not independent. A colon cannot follow that unfinished introduction either. When a dependent clause follows the main clause, punctuation depends on its role and meaning; do not mechanically insert a comma before every because or when.

Find the reach of the subordinating word. In The team knew that although the sample was small, the pattern was clear, one dependent structure contains another. Conversely, a sentence can begin with a complete main clause even if a subordinate clause occurs later inside it. Label whole units before judging a boundary. Do not use the shortcut "I see although, so everything before the blank must be a fragment."

W1.4: A long introduction is still dependent

A conservation team compared two methods for cleaning a painted ceiling. Although the method that removed the most surface grime required fewer ______ team selected the slower method because it left the original pigment intact. Preserving the pigment mattered more than finishing the work quickly.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. applications; the

B. applications. The

C. applications: the

D. applications, the

Practice this lesson: 1.11, 1.12.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.4

Answer: D

Completed text: A conservation team compared two methods for cleaning a painted ceiling. Although the method that removed the most surface grime required fewer applications, the team selected the slower method because it left the original pigment intact. Preserving the pigment mattered more than finishing the work quickly.

  1. Begin at "Although" and follow its clause through the verb "required" to "fewer applications." This entire introductory unit is subordinate.
  2. The main clause begins "the team selected the slower method." It supplies the independent statement that the opening concession prepares us to understand.
  3. Choice D uses a comma to attach the dependent introduction to that main clause. A finite verb inside the although-clause does not make the introduction independent.

Why A fails: The semicolon treats the although-clause as an independent clause, but the subordinating word leaves it dependent.

Why B fails: The period strands "Although the method ... required fewer applications" as a fragment.

Why C fails: The colon follows a dependent clause rather than a complete introductory main clause.

Key takeaway: A subordinate clause can contain its own subject, verb, and embedded description and still require a main clause.

1.5 Separate transition meaning from boundary grammar

Words and phrases such as however, therefore, nevertheless, consequently, and for example can explain a relationship between statements. In this transitional use, they are not coordinating conjunctions. The road was closed, however, the bridge remained open still contains a comma splice. A transition does not give a comma permission to connect two independent clauses.

Two useful patterns are independent clause; however, independent clause and independent clause. However, independent clause. The semicolon or period establishes the sentence boundary. The comma after however sets off the introductory transition inside the next clause. These marks do different jobs.

A transition can move within a clause: The bridge, however, remained open. Here paired commas set off an interruption; they do not join independent clauses. Also read the actual use of the word. In however carefully the team measured, however means something like "no matter how" and begins a subordinate construction. It is not the same pattern as contrastive however,. For boundary questions, first solve the clause structure, then check that the transition fits the relationship.

W1.5: A transition cannot replace a conjunction

Researchers expected a new coating to reduce heat loss from a small test chamber. Measurements showed that the coated walls retained heat more effectively than the untreated ______ the door continued to release enough heat to limit the chamber's overall performance.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. walls, however,

B. walls; however,

C. walls however,

D. walls; however;

Practice this lesson: 1.13, 1.14.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.5

Answer: B

Completed text: Researchers expected a new coating to reduce heat loss from a small test chamber. Measurements showed that the coated walls retained heat more effectively than the untreated walls; however, the door continued to release enough heat to limit the chamber's overall performance.

  1. The first independent clause is "Measurements showed that the coated walls retained heat more effectively than the untreated walls." The second is "the door continued to release enough heat ...".
  2. Contrastive "however" introduces the second statement, but it is an adverb, not the coordinating conjunction "but." It cannot turn a comma alone into a valid clause join.
  3. Choice B places a semicolon between the independent clauses and a comma after the transition. Read the whole sentence to confirm that both punctuation jobs are satisfied.

Why A fails: The comma before however would be the only boundary between independent clauses. The transition does not cure that comma splice.

Why C fails: The independent clauses lack a boundary before the transition. A comma after however only sets off the transition; it does not separate the clauses.

Why D fails: The second semicolon follows only the transition however, not a complete independent clause. A comma is needed to set off this introductory adverb.

Key takeaway: Name two jobs: the mark between clauses and the mark around the transition. Do not ask one comma to do both.

1.6 Introduce explanations and lists with a colon

A colon can introduce a list, explanation, example, or specification after a complete independent clause. In The archive lacks one crucial document: the original map, the words before the colon form a complete clause. The words after it explain which document is missing; they need not form another independent clause.

Apply two tests. First, read the left side by itself and verify that it is complete. Second, ask whether the right side supplies the promised explanation or detail. The colon is inappropriate in The archive lacks: the original map, because it separates a transitive verb from its object. It is also inappropriate in The tools include: brushes and spatulas or The result depends on: temperature. A phrase such as as follows can complete an introduction; the mere expectation of a list cannot.

A complete clause can follow a colon when it explains the preceding one. A semicolon can also connect complete clauses, so do not assume that related ideas force a colon whenever a semicolon is offered. Inspect the entire choices. At the SAT boundary, incomplete material on the right often distinguishes a valid colon from an invalid semicolon or period.

W1.6: The explanation need not be a sentence

Students were comparing early and late versions of a stage design. The revised plan solved one persistent ______ narrow entrance through which every performer had to pass. By adding a second entrance, the designer made it possible for several groups to enter from different directions.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. problem; the

B. problem. The

C. problem: the

D. problem; and the

Practice this lesson: 1.15, 1.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.6

Answer: C

Completed text: Students were comparing early and late versions of a stage design. The revised plan solved one persistent problem: the narrow entrance through which every performer had to pass. By adding a second entrance, the designer made it possible for several groups to enter from different directions.

  1. The words "The revised plan solved one persistent problem" form a complete independent clause. The noun problem is already the object of solved.
  2. The following noun phrase, "the narrow entrance through which every performer had to pass," identifies the problem. Its relative clause modifies entrance; it does not make the whole phrase an independent sentence.
  3. Choice C uses a colon after the complete introduction to present that identifying explanation. A phrase can correctly follow this colon.

Why A fails: The material after the semicolon is a noun phrase with a relative clause, not a complete independent clause.

Why B fails: The period makes the following noun phrase a separate fragment; the relative-clause verb had does not supply a main predicate for entrance.

Why D fails: The semicolon is followed by and plus a noun phrase, not an independent clause. Adding and does not give that phrase a main predicate.

Key takeaway: A colon needs a complete introduction, but its explanation can be a noun phrase.

Practice this lesson: 1.15 and 1.16.

1.7 Distinguish an explanatory dash from a paired insertion

A single em dash can introduce an emphatic explanation or addition after a complete clause: The experiment revealed an unexpected obstacle—a leak beneath the valve. The explanation may be a phrase rather than an independent clause. A colon could often work at the same point; the SAT cannot legitimately force a choice between two options that are otherwise both correct merely by asking which pause feels stronger.

Paired em dashes perform a different job. They enclose material inserted into an ongoing clause: The valve—the only part made abroad—failed. Removing the insertion leaves The valve failed. The first dash here does not follow a complete independent clause, and it does not need to: it opens a pair rather than introducing an end-of-sentence explanation.

To decide whether a second dash is needed, inspect what follows the inserted material. If the main sentence resumes, close the insertion. If the single dash introduces a final explanation, do not invent a second dash before the period. Distinguish real em dashes from hyphens, which join words and cannot serve as sentence-boundary marks. Chapter 2 develops paired punctuation in detail; here the priority is recognizing which job the dash performs.

W1.7: An explanation after a complete statement

The village's oral histories described a path across land now covered by water. A survey of the lakebed revealed a line of flat ______ remains of a carefully built walkway. The finding gave researchers a physical feature to compare with the route described in the stories.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. stones—the

B. stones; the

C. stones. The

D. stones; and the

Practice this lesson: 1.17, 1.18.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.7

Answer: A

Completed text: The village's oral histories described a path across land now covered by water. A survey of the lakebed revealed a line of flat stones—the remains of a carefully built walkway. The finding gave researchers a physical feature to compare with the route described in the stories.

  1. The independent statement is "A survey of the lakebed revealed a line of flat stones." The direct object "a line of flat stones," headed by line, completes the verb revealed.
  2. The following noun phrase, "the remains of a carefully built walkway," explains what the stones were. It has no main finite verb and therefore cannot be placed after an ordinary clause-joining semicolon as a separate clause.
  3. Choice A introduces the final explanation with an em dash. No main sentence resumes after that explanation, so the dash is single rather than the opening of a required pair.

Why B fails: The words after the semicolon form a noun phrase, not an independent clause.

Why C fails: The period leaves "The remains of a carefully built walkway" as a sentence fragment.

Why D fails: The semicolon is followed by and plus a noun phrase. A coordinating conjunction does not supply the missing main predicate.

Key takeaway: Ask whether the dash introduces a final explanation or opens an interruption that the main clause later resumes.

1.8 Diagnose nested structures without losing the main sentence

The hardest boundary questions often hide a simple clause pattern inside several layers of description. A relative clause may contain a subordinate clause; a main subject may be followed by a long supplement; a verb inside the supplement may sit closer to the blank than the actual main verb does. Counting verbs without assigning them to clauses will not solve these sentences.

Use three passes. First, find the main subject and its predicate. Second, bracket the descriptions and dependent clauses attached to that core. Third, read the units on the two sides of the blank as complete expressions. Decide whether you have two independent clauses, an introduction plus a main clause, or one main clause whose subject and predicate must remain connected.

Then insert each surviving choice literally. A choice can repair one join while creating a second problem, such as a comma after a conjunction or a semicolon before a phrase. Preserve the actual meaning and wording; do not silently add a subject or treat a relative-clause verb as the missing main predicate. The goal is a short structural reason you can defend: "two independent clauses," "dependent introduction," or "one subject still awaiting its main verb."

W1.8: Two main clauses beneath several layers

The catalog that the curator prepared after the paintings had been cleaned identifies the materials used in each ______ also records the changes that became visible when the old varnish was removed. Visitors can therefore compare the paintings' present appearance with evidence of earlier revisions.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. work, it

B. work it

C. work; although it

D. work; it

Practice this lesson: 1.19, 1.20.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.8

Answer: D

Completed text: The catalog that the curator prepared after the paintings had been cleaned identifies the materials used in each work; it also records the changes that became visible when the old varnish was removed. Visitors can therefore compare the paintings' present appearance with evidence of earlier revisions.

  1. Reduce the left side to "The catalog identifies the materials." The relative clause beginning with "that the curator prepared" and the dependent clause beginning with "after" are inside that complete main clause.
  2. Reduce the right side to "it also records the changes." The clause beginning with "that became visible" describes changes, and the when-clause adds the circumstances.
  3. There are two independent main clauses. Choice D joins them with a semicolon; the nested clauses do not make either main clause a fragment.
  4. Check that the selected punctuation addresses the actual main-clause boundary rather than one of the smaller clauses inside the sentence.

Why A fails: A comma alone joins the complete clauses "The catalog ... identifies ..." and "it also records ...," producing a comma splice.

Why B fails: The main clauses run together without a conjunction or valid boundary punctuation.

Why C fails: Although makes the right-hand clause dependent. The semicolon therefore lacks an independent clause on its right, even though that clause has its own subject and verb.

Key takeaway: Nested clauses add detail; they do not change the basic boundary once the two main cores are identified.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

• Find the main subject and its finite verb before judging a boundary.

• A period or ordinary clause-joining semicolon needs a complete independent clause on each side.

• A coordinating conjunction and a subordinating conjunction create different sentence structures.

• A transitional adverb such as however does not by itself join independent clauses.

• A colon or explanatory dash must follow a complete clause; the explanation after it can be a phrase.

Begin with four short reasoning drills, then complete sixteen multiple-choice questions. For each answer, identify the decisive rule and the flaw in the nearest competitor. Some drills admit more than one valid repair; explain why your revision works.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.
