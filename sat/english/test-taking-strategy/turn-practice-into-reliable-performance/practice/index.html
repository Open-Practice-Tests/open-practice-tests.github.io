Question 5

  • Guide question 6.05
  • Foundation

Ravi misses a subject-verb agreement question. With no time limit and before seeing the explanation, he correctly identifies "collection" as the subject but still chooses the plural verb because several objects are in the collection. He says he does not understand why the grammatical subject should control the verb.

Which next action most directly addresses the difficulty shown here?

Why this answer

Repair the missing knowledge | Review lesson 6.1

Correct choice: Study how grammatical number controls agreement, then explain a new example containing a singular collective subject.

Ravi has already located the subject, so faster subject identification does not address his stated uncertainty. The missing knowledge is how the subject's grammatical number controls agreement in this example. He should learn that relationship and explain a fresh contrasting sentence. The untimed failure provides a concrete reason to address knowledge before interpreting this as only a pacing problem.

A: He identified the subject correctly; the unresolved step comes after that identification.

C: Sound can be a useful check for familiar structures, but it does not supply the rule Ravi says he does not understand.

D: Shortening time leaves the stated misunderstanding in place and is not supported by this record.

If you missed it: After studying the rule, identify the subject and justify the verb in a new sentence before returning to mixed practice.

Strategy in use: Use the student's actual reasoning to locate the failure; do not automatically assign all grammar errors the same repair.

Question 6

  • Guide question 6.06
  • Challenge

During a practice module, Eva spends four minutes repeatedly rereading one inference item, then rushes through the final questions. On one rushed agreement item, she selects the verb that matches the nearest noun. In an independent untimed redo, she identifies the real subject and explains the correct verb. She wants a repair plan that accounts for both observations.

Which plan is best supported?

Why this answer

Distinguish linked causes | Review lesson 6.1

Correct choice: Practice a specific exit from unproductive rereading and retain a subject check on later agreement items.

The record shows two actionable points: unproductive rereading consumed time, and a later answer followed the nearest noun rather than the subject. Eva's independent redo suggests that she can apply the agreement rule when she attends to structure. The plan should therefore address time allocation while preserving the specific grammar check. It should not assume that fixing one point will automatically eliminate every other error.

A: Her redo shows relevant agreement knowledge, while the earlier stall remains a documented source of time loss.

B: Rushing may matter, but the proposed plan discards the precise structural check that her wrong answer missed.

C: More inference study may help, but staying indefinitely is not a tested repair for the observed stall and offers no guarantee about later answers.

If you missed it: Write a stall trigger for deferral and a short subject-verb check; observe both in the next fresh timed module.

Strategy in use: A useful error log can record a primary process problem and a secondary item-level mistake.

Question 7

  • Guide question 6.07
  • Challenge

After a timed set, Jun reads the explanation for a missed vocabulary question. Ten minutes later, he answers the same question correctly and says, "So I knew this word all along; the clock caused my mistake." His original scratch note contains only the chosen letter, and he cannot reconstruct his initial reasoning.

Which conclusion is most justified?

Why this answer

Do not overdiagnose a redo | Review lesson 6.1

Correct choice: The redo shows learning after feedback, but it does not establish what Jun knew before feedback or isolate the clock as the cause.

Jun's second response followed an explanation that could have supplied both the answer and the missing reasoning. It therefore cannot reconstruct his earlier knowledge or distinguish time pressure from a vocabulary, context, or reasoning failure. He can still use the item to learn the meaning and method. A fresh contextual use will provide better evidence about what he can now do independently.

B: The explanation can change what Jun knows immediately; a short interval does not make the redo independent.

C: A missed vocabulary item can arise from several causes, and the original reasoning is unavailable here.

D: The item remains useful for learning even though it is weak evidence for a precise diagnosis of the original miss.

If you missed it: Record the cause as uncertain, explain the word in this context, and check a fresh use without rereading the explanation first.

Strategy in use: An honest "not yet known" diagnosis is more useful than a confident story unsupported by the record.

Question 8

  • Guide question 6.08
  • Transfer

After studying comma splices, Imani can explain why a comma alone cannot join the two independent clauses in a worked example. She has fifteen minutes today and another short session two days from now. Her immediate goal is to check whether she can recognize and repair the same structure in unfamiliar sentences.

Which plan best matches that goal?

Why this answer

Move beyond a reviewed example | Review lesson 6.2

Correct choice: Try fresh boundary items today, explain the clause structure, and attempt different relevant items later without a reminder.

Imani wants to check an operation on unfamiliar sentences, so she needs new examples containing relevant structures. Explaining the clauses reveals whether her answer follows the rule. A later attempt without a reminder adds a check that the operation remains available. A full-test total is too broad to diagnose this specific repair, and memorizing one example cannot establish transfer.

A: Repeated reading may reinforce the explanation but does not require independent recognition in a new sentence.

B: A full test includes many other skills, and its total alone would not show how she handled boundary structures.

D: Speed on a known answer is not evidence that she can identify the structure in unfamiliar material.

If you missed it: Use one fresh example with two independent clauses and another with a dependent clause so that recognition matters.

Strategy in use: Choose practice that tests the repaired operation, not merely the memory of its most recent example.

Question 9

  • Guide question 6.09
  • Transfer

Noah wants to compare two question orders. For his usual route, he selects a mixed set with several difficult reading questions. For the new route, he selects only editing questions he expects to answer quickly. He plans to compare the percentages correct and average times.

Which change would most improve this comparison?

Why this answer

Avoid selective evidence | Review lesson 6.2

Correct choice: Use fresh sets with reasonably similar length and skill mix, keep timing stable, and record costs as well as accuracy.

The selected sets differ in precisely the demands a route must manage. An editing-only set cannot reveal how the new route handles reading or the transitions between question groups. Similar fresh mixed sets and stable timing make the comparison more informative. Even then, differences in difficulty remain a limit, so Noah should inspect the questions and gather additional trials rather than treating one number as decisive.

B: Percentages do not remove differences in skill mix or the deliberate selection of easier expected work.

C: Excluding incorrect work hides time costs that matter during a real module.

D: Extra time changes another important condition and makes it harder to attribute the outcome to the route.

If you missed it: Define the comparison before choosing sets, and include time spent on wrong answers and navigation.

Strategy in use: A useful trial includes the parts of the test a method may handle poorly, not only its likely strengths.

Question 10

  • Guide question 6.10
  • Challenge

Tessa compares reading the question stem first with reading the passage first on two fresh, similarly mixed 12-question reading sets under the same time limit. Passage first yields 11 correct and one minute remaining. Task first yields 10 correct and three minutes remaining. She also notices that the second set contains several passages she personally finds easier. She wants to choose a default for future reading practice.

Which response is most reasonable given this evidence?

Why this answer

Interpret competing outcomes | Review lesson 6.2

Correct choice: Inspect the errors and time costs, then gather further fresh comparisons before treating either method as clearly better.

The results point in different directions: task first left more time but produced one fewer correct answer. Two different sets cannot isolate the method, and Tessa's impression of passage difficulty adds another reason for caution. She should examine what caused the errors and where time was gained, then compare again. No conversion from extra minutes to guaranteed correct answers is justified.

A: Unused time is not automatically worth a particular accuracy cost, and the set difference may explain some of the apparent speed gain.

B: A single lower result on a different set cannot establish that task first is permanently inferior.

C: Averaging these results does not create a prediction for either method or remove differences between the sets.

If you missed it: Alternate the reading methods on later fresh mixed reading sets, keeping timing and review procedures stable and recording the missed evidence behind each error.

Strategy in use: Task first is a useful default to try, but an individual comparison still requires honest evidence about the result.

Question 11

  • Guide question 6.11
  • Foundation

Sofia has installed Bluebook but has not used its tools. She wants a brief first session devoted to navigation, followed on another day by a realistic scored rehearsal. She has no approved timing accommodations.

Which sequence best fits those two purposes?

Why this answer

Give each resource a job | Review lesson 6.3

Correct choice: Use the untimed test preview to explore the interface, then take a fresh full-length Bluebook practice under standard timing.

The preview is designed for a short encounter with the digital interface and tools; it does not provide scores or answer feedback. A fresh full-length Bluebook practice supplies the scored rehearsal. Sofia should follow standard timing and avoid the early-advance freedom available in practice if she wants to rehearse the live timing experience.

A: The preview does not yield a score prediction, and repeated untimed work does not reproduce module pacing.

C: Printed questions can train content, but they do not verify use of the Bluebook interface.

D: An untimed preview cannot serve as a scored rehearsal, regardless of what Sofia did in the first session.

If you missed it: Write one goal before opening each resource: learn a tool, repair a skill, or rehearse the test.

Strategy in use: The purpose of a practice session determines which result is meaningful.

Question 12

  • Guide question 6.12
  • Transfer

Marcus plans to take an official practice test next weekend as a fresh rehearsal. Today he finds a video that solves every question from that exact test. He also has access to separate practice items for the skill he wants to review. He wants both useful learning today and an unfamiliar rehearsal later.

Which choice best preserves both goals?

Why this answer

Preserve an honest rehearsal | Review lesson 6.3

Correct choice: Use other relevant items today and save the test and its explanations until after the timed rehearsal.

Using separate items lets Marcus train the relevant skill while preserving the intended test as unfamiliar material. Seeing a solution, answer letter, or complete question is exposure even if he is not sure he will remember it. He can still learn from repeated official material later; he should simply avoid labeling that performance a fresh rehearsal.

A: The passage of a week does not guarantee that prior solutions no longer influence his answers or speed.

B: An answer letter can be remembered, and the video may also expose wording or structure that makes the later item familiar.

D: Changing timing does not make the questions themselves unfamiliar.

If you missed it: Keep a record of tests and questions already attempted or seen in explanations and videos.

Strategy in use: Protect a few unseen tests for measurement while using other material actively for learning.

Question 13

  • Guide question 6.13
  • Challenge

Nadia answers ten hard transition questions correctly in an untimed targeted set. She then misses two of four transition questions in a fresh timed mixed set. One miss reverses cause and result; the other treats a contrast as an example. She wants to know what to practice next, rather than estimate a score from these small samples.

Which plan is best supported?

Why this answer

Interpret a small targeted sample | Review lesson 6.3

Correct choice: Review the two relationship errors, practice fresh examples of those distinctions, and check them again in timed mixed work.

The small samples do not establish a stable accuracy rate or a single cause for the difference. They do reveal two concrete relationship errors worth investigating. Nadia can repair those distinctions and then test them in fresh mixed timing, where she must recognize the task and apply the method among other questions. The targeted success is useful evidence, but it does not erase the later misses.

B: The mixed errors identify meaningful problems, and perfect performance on one targeted set does not make context or recognition irrelevant.

C: Timing may contribute, but the record does not isolate speed as the only cause; the errors have specific logical content.

D: A small sample limits broad conclusions, but each item can still reveal an actionable reasoning error.

If you missed it: For each miss, write the relationship in plain language before choosing a transition; test the distinction in a new passage.

Strategy in use: Use small samples to locate concrete problems, not to declare either mastery or failure of an entire domain.

Question 14

  • Guide question 6.14
  • Transfer

It is six weeks before SAT Weekend. Priya plans to use a school-managed laptop that already has Bluebook installed. She has never signed in on it or tried a practice test, and she does not know whether she can change its settings. She wants to avoid discovering a device problem at the test center.

Which next action best fits her situation?

Why this answer

Separate setup from final checks | Review lesson 6.4

Correct choice: Begin the current device checks now, practice on the laptop, and ask school staff for help with managed settings.

Priya has time to verify the device and obtain help from the people who manage it. Installation alone does not demonstrate that sign-in, settings, and testing will work. School-managed devices may require school assistance, so she should not assume a test center can fix restricted settings. Exam setup near the test date is a later step, not a substitute for early device preparation.

A: The ticket schedule does not guarantee device readiness, and waiting removes time to resolve school-management issues.

B: Test-center staff may lack the permissions needed to change school-managed settings.

C: A phone is not an approved SAT testing device; internet access alone does not establish eligibility.

If you missed it: Use the current Bluebook device instructions with the school staff who manage the laptop, then complete later setup when it opens.

Strategy in use: Follow the instructions for your actual device and administration; do not infer readiness from installation alone.

Question 15

  • Guide question 6.15
  • Transfer

During an actual test, Ben's Bluebook screen stops responding to his clicks. He cannot move to another question or select an answer. He has practiced a short breathing reset for ordinary frustration, but this is a device problem.

Which response is most appropriate?

Why this answer

Choose the right response to the problem | Review lesson 6.4

Correct choice: Raise his hand for the proctor's help and follow official troubleshooting directions rather than assuming he can pause or restore time himself.

Ben cannot interact with the test, so he needs procedural or technical assistance. A brief personal reset may help him stay composed, but it does not fix an unresponsive screen. He should involve the proctor and follow the applicable troubleshooting process. He should not assume a particular time adjustment, restart the test on his own initiative, or access a prohibited device.

A: The described failure is loss of device interaction, and there is no evidence that stress caused it.

C: The scenario does not authorize an independent restart or establish that time will be restored.

D: Phone restrictions still apply; a technical difficulty does not authorize using a phone during testing.

If you missed it: Practice distinguishing a reading stall from a technical failure; memorize the appropriate help action for the latter.

Strategy in use: Use the proctor and Bluebook instructions for technical problems; a personal routine cannot override testing procedures.

Question 16

  • Guide question 6.16
  • Transfer

During the scheduled break between Reading and Writing and Math, Alex wants to check a list of grammar rules on his phone because he worries about one earlier answer. He has no relevant approved exception. Reading and Writing has ended, and he will next take Math.

Which response best fits the rules and the work still available to him?

Why this answer

Use the scheduled break appropriately | Review lesson 6.4

Correct choice: Leave the phone and notes unavailable, use permitted break activities, and return on time prepared to begin the next section.

Alex cannot return to a completed Reading and Writing module, and the scheduled break does not authorize access to his phone or reference notes. The useful remaining action is to follow break instructions and return ready for the next section. He can review his general preparation after testing; he should not seek another student's answer or try to reopen completed work.

A: Prohibited-device and reference-material restrictions continue during the break.

B: The end of a section does not authorize discussing test content with another student.

D: Completed modules cannot be reopened after moving on to the next section.

If you missed it: Plan permitted break activities in advance and direct your return-to-work routine toward the next section.

Strategy in use: Follow your actual approved arrangements and the proctor's instructions; this scenario assumes the standard scheduled break.
