Review and reference

Use sentence structure, logical relationships, and writing goals to make decisive choices.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Identify what the answer differences are testing before rereading aimlessly.
  • Use complete sentence structure to resolve punctuation and form questions.
  • Choose transitions by the relationship between ideas at the blank.
  • Select synthesized sentences that meet the full goal without changing the notes.

4.1 Boundaries: find the structure before the mark

When the answer choices differ mainly in punctuation, begin by identifying exactly what changes. Then read the whole sentence, including the words and punctuation already printed outside the blank. Find the subject and its complete main verb phrase, including any helping verb. Temporarily set aside descriptions between them. In "The boxes near the doorway contain samples," the core is "boxes contain." A long subject does not create a sentence boundary.

Next, decide what stands on each side of the proposed mark. An independent clause can stand as a sentence; a dependent clause or phrase cannot. "The survey ended" is independent. "Although the survey ended" is dependent. A semicolon can join two closely related independent clauses. A comma by itself cannot do that. A colon requires a complete clause before it and introduces an explanation, example, or list; what follows need not be a complete clause. These are structural checks, not instructions to select a particular mark whenever you see a particular word.

Be equally alert to places where no mark belongs. Do not separate a verb from its ordinary direct object or a subject from its verb. In "The survey records rainfall and tracks temperature," the two verbs share one subject. Their length does not make them two independent clauses. If a nonessential description interrupts a sentence, check both its opening and closing punctuation. One comma in "The map, revised last spring, shows three trails" closes the description; it does not divide the subject from its verb.

The fastest reliable decision is often a short structural proof: "dependent clause, then main clause" or "verb directly followed by its object." Read the chosen completion once to verify that proof against the entire sentence. Avoid choosing punctuation by the pause you hear. If two marks seem possible, inspect the exact words each option includes and every fixed mark nearby. A period and a semicolon can sometimes both work in a sentence; the task is to distinguish the actual options, not to rank punctuation marks by strength.

W4.1: A long description does not create a boundary

The center's new catalog, which brings together descriptions of more than four thousand ceramic fragments, ______ their dimensions and lists the location where each fragment was recovered. Researchers can use this information to compare objects that are now housed in different museums.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. records;

B. records,

C. records

D. records:

Practice this lesson: 4.05, 4.06, 4.07.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W4.1

Answer: C

Completed text: The center's new catalog, which brings together descriptions of more than four thousand ceramic fragments, records their dimensions and lists the location where each fragment was recovered. Researchers can use this information to compare objects that are now housed in different museums.

  1. The choices differ only in the punctuation after "records," so locate that verb and its object.
  2. The subject is "catalog." The two commas already enclose the description beginning "which brings together." Removing that description leaves "The center's new catalog records their dimensions and lists the location."
  3. "Their dimensions" is the direct object of "records." No punctuation is needed between this verb and its object. Select C and read the completed sentence once.

Why A fails: The semicolon separates "records" from its object; "their dimensions and lists the location" is not an independent clause.

Why B fails: The comma interrupts the direct connection between the verb "records" and the object "their dimensions." The preceding comma has already closed the description.

Why D fails: The colon also separates the verb from its object. Before the colon, "The center's new catalog ... records" does not form the intended complete statement.

Key takeaway: Long material near a blank can distract you from a short subject-verb-object core.

Strategy in use: Inspect the answer differences first, then simplify the sentence mentally. Do not reread the historical details unless they affect the grammar.

4.2 Form, structure, and sense: expand the context when needed

Answer differences tell you where to look, but they do not supply the answer. If the choices are "indicates," "indicate," "indicating," and "have indicated," ask whether the blank needs a finite verb and what its subject is. If the choices change a pronoun, identify the precise referent and its grammatical role. If an introductory modifier appears before four different subjects, ask who or what actually performs the introductory action. This first classification prevents a vague search for whichever sentence sounds most formal.

For agreement, trace the verb back to its subject rather than to the nearest noun. "The arrangement of the mirrors affects the image" has the singular subject "arrangement"; "mirrors" belongs to an intervening phrase. Descriptions beginning with "that" or "which" can contain their own subjects and verbs. Identify the main clause separately. For verb form, remember that an -ing form alone cannot serve as the main finite verb: "The sensor detecting movement" is a noun phrase, while "The sensor detects movement" is a complete clause.

Tense, reference, and meaning may require a wider reading window. "By the time the exhibition opened, the artist had finished the mural" orders two past events. A sentence elsewhere may establish that an action is current, ongoing, or already completed. Do not force every verb in a passage into the same tense; different time relationships can require different forms. For a modifier, "After comparing the images, the analyst revised the estimate" makes the analyst the person who compared them. "After comparing the images, the estimate was revised" fails to name a logical subject for the introductory action.

Once you identify the governing relationship, test every part of the candidate against it. A choice may agree in number but leave the sentence without a main verb; another may have a finite verb but attach a description to the wrong noun. Your final check should be specific: locate the subject, order the events, identify the referent, or name the actor. If the nearby text does not resolve the issue, read farther. Saving a few seconds by ignoring necessary context is not efficient editing.

W4.2: The closest noun is not the subject

In sediment taken from the floor of an ancient lake, researchers found microscopic grains associated with several drought-tolerant plants. The presence of these grains in samples from multiple depths ______ that such plants persisted in the region over a considerable period.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. indicates

B. indicate

C. indicating

D. have indicated

Practice this lesson: 4.08, 4.09, 4.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W4.2

Answer: A

Completed text: In sediment taken from the floor of an ancient lake, researchers found microscopic grains associated with several drought-tolerant plants. The presence of these grains in samples from multiple depths indicates that such plants persisted in the region over a considerable period.

  1. The options change the form of a verb. Identify the main subject before deciding among them.
  2. The subject is the singular noun "presence." The phrases "of these grains" and "in samples from multiple depths" describe that presence; their plural nouns do not control the main verb.
  3. The sentence needs a finite main verb. "Indicates" supplies that verb and agrees with "presence." The "that" clause states what the presence indicates.

Why B fails: "Indicate" does not agree with the singular subject "presence." The nearby plural nouns "samples" and "depths" are inside a prepositional phrase.

Why C fails: "Indicating" without a helping verb leaves the sentence without a finite main verb. A sentence can contain a verb form without containing the main verb it needs.

Why D fails: "Have indicated" is a finite verb phrase, but its plural form does not agree with "presence." Changing the tense does not repair that agreement error.

Key takeaway: Locate the grammatical subject before reacting to nearby singular or plural nouns.

Strategy in use: You do not need to infer how long the plants lived. The sentence's core, "The presence indicates," decides the question.

Practice this lesson: 4.08, 4.09, 4.10.

4.3 Transitions: state the relationship before matching a word

A transition is a claim about how one idea relates to another. Before inserting a familiar word, identify the proposition before the blank and the proposition in the sentence containing it. Describe the connection in ordinary language: "this is an example," "this happened despite the obstacle," or "this gives a more precise version of the result." Your prediction is provisional. If no choice fits it, reread the relevant ideas and revise the prediction instead of forcing a synonym match.

Read far enough to identify the actual reference point. A paragraph may introduce an old explanation, replace it with a new finding, and then describe that finding more precisely. The final sentence may elaborate on the new finding even though it also conflicts with the old explanation. The transition must make sense at its own location. Words in the topic do not decide the relationship: two sentences about different materials can agree, and two sentences about the same material can contrast.

Distinguish close relationships by asking what the author is doing. "The device is costly. Nevertheless, demand remains high" defeats a reasonable expectation. "The team rejected the device. Instead, it used a manual gauge" supplies a replacement. "The device reduced measurement time. Consequently, the team completed more trials" presents a result. A general claim followed by one case supports "for example"; a claim followed by a more exact statement may support "specifically." These families can overlap, so the passage and the actual competing choices matter more than a memorized label.

For the final two, translate each candidate into a sentence about the logic. "As a result" asserts that the preceding situation led to the following one. "In other words" asserts a restatement. Look for support for that assertion, not merely a smooth sound when you read aloud. Keep the passage's degree of certainty: a possible explanation is not an established cause. Then reread the complete connection with your choice inserted. If you have reread twice without identifying any new relationship evidence, select your best-supported answer and consider a purposeful revisit instead of continuing the same unproductive loop.

W4.3: A specific case develops a general claim

Public art can give a familiar place a new visual identity without changing its physical layout. ______ a series of painted panels along the walls of an existing pedestrian tunnel can turn an ordinary passageway into a recognizable neighborhood landmark.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. In contrast,

B. Otherwise,

C. Previously,

D. For example,

Practice this lesson: 4.11, 4.12, 4.13.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W4.3

Answer: D

Completed text: Public art can give a familiar place a new visual identity without changing its physical layout. For example, a series of painted panels along the walls of an existing pedestrian tunnel can turn an ordinary passageway into a recognizable neighborhood landmark.

  1. The first sentence makes a general claim: public art can alter how a place looks while preserving its layout.
  2. The second sentence gives one way that can happen: painted panels change the visual identity of an existing tunnel.
  3. The tunnel is a case illustrating the general claim, so "For example" expresses the relationship. The passage states no opposing case, alternative condition, or change of time.

Why A fails: The painted tunnel supports the claim about public art rather than contrasting with it. The fact that the second sentence is more concrete does not create an opposition.

Why B fails: "Otherwise" would introduce an alternative circumstance or consequence. No such alternative condition is stated; the painted tunnel is a specific illustration of the general claim.

Why C fails: The passage gives no sequence of earlier and later events. Both sentences describe a general possibility.

Key takeaway: To recognize an example, identify the broad claim and show how the concrete case fits it.

Strategy in use: Compress the connection to "general possibility → one illustration." The attractive word "can" in both sentences helps preserve possibility; it does not by itself determine the transition.

Practice this lesson: 4.11, 4.12, 4.13.

4.4 Rhetorical synthesis: satisfy the goal and preserve the notes

Read the student's goal before deciding which notes deserve attention. Turn the goal into a short checklist. "Introduce a device to unfamiliar readers and explain its function" requires both identification and function. "Emphasize a similarity between two works" requires a supported shared feature of the named works. This is a reading-and-selection task: the longest answer, the largest number of facts, and the most impressive phrasing do not earn automatic preference.

Next, scan the notes with that checklist in mind, then read the relevant notes closely. Identify the people, works, variables, comparison groups, dates, and qualifiers that the goal actually uses. You may not need every fact, but you must verify every substantive claim in a candidate. A note about an artist's general practice does not necessarily establish a feature of a particular artwork. "Some trials" does not mean "all trials," and a finding in one setting does not establish a universal result.

Use two separate tests for a close pair: Is the statement supported? Does it accomplish the requested goal? A true statement can fail because it describes only one of the two objects to be compared or explains a result when the goal asks about a method. Conversely, a sentence can sound perfectly tailored to the goal while inventing a cause or exaggerating a result. For numerical comparisons, verify the units and which group each number describes. Preserve the difference between a count and a percentage, and between an observed difference and a causal explanation.

Then perform a compact completion check. Name the words that fulfill each part of the goal and identify the notes that support them. If you cannot do either, the choice needs further scrutiny. You need not reconstruct an entire essay or combine every note. Select the sentence that performs the requested writing job accurately and directly. When all four choices are grammatical, spending time comparing their polish can conceal the real distinction: one includes the necessary relationship, explanation, or qualification, while a close rival leaves part of the goal unfinished.

W4.4: A two-part goal requires two supported details

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. A research team used wildlife cameras to compare animal visits at three woodland sites.

2. The trial lasted seven nights and recorded thirty-seven animal visits in total.

3. At every site, researchers mounted the camera one meter above the ground.

4. They programmed all three cameras to take one photograph every thirty seconds.

5. The team identified camera height and photographing interval as the two features standardized across sites.

The student wants to explain how researchers kept two features of a wildlife-camera trial consistent across sites. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. To compare animal visits at three sites, the researchers mounted each camera one meter above the ground and recorded thirty-seven visits during the trial.

B. At all three sites, researchers positioned cameras one meter above the ground and programmed them to take a photograph every thirty seconds.

C. Researchers used cameras to compare animal visits at three sites, where thirty-seven visits were recorded during a trial lasting seven nights.

D. Each camera took a photograph every thirty seconds, providing images that researchers used to compare animal visits at three woodland sites.

Practice this lesson: 4.14, 4.15, 4.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W4.4

Answer: B

Correct choice: At all three sites, researchers positioned cameras one meter above the ground and programmed them to take a photograph every thirty seconds.

  1. The goal asks about two features kept consistent across sites. Write the checklist "same feature 1 + same feature 2," with an explicit connection to all sites.
  2. The notes identify camera height and photographing interval as the two standardized features: one meter above ground and one photograph every thirty seconds.
  3. B names both features and says that they applied at all three sites. Its claims are supported by the notes about mounting height and camera programming.
  4. The duration, research purpose, and total visits are supported facts, but substituting one of them for a second standardized feature leaves this particular goal incomplete.

Why A fails: This accurately gives the standardized mounting height and total visits. It supplies only one of the two features that were kept consistent; the total is an outcome of the trial.

Why C fails: This gives the purpose, number of sites, total visits, and duration. It does not explain the standardized camera height or photographing interval.

Why D fails: This includes the standardized interval and the research purpose. It omits the other standardized feature, the camera height, and does not explain a second feature kept consistent across sites.

Key takeaway: True details are useful only when they do the writing job the goal requests.

Strategy in use: Read the goal first, locate the two standardization notes, and test each candidate against both. The notes are numbered in this guide to make that verification visible.

Practice this lesson: 4.14, 4.15, 4.16.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

• Inspect the choices to locate the decision, then read enough context to settle it.

• For punctuation, identify clause cores and fixed marks before choosing a boundary.

• For form, trace the subject, main verb, referent, actor, or timeline that governs the choice.

• For transitions, state the relationship in your own words and test the closest rival against it.

• For synthesis, verify both goal completion and support from the notes.

Begin with four short drills, one per lesson, then complete twelve multiple-choice questions, three per lesson. Labels distinguish SAT-format questions from strategy decisions. Give the evidence or stated condition that decides each item. Model drill responses are examples; another well-supported response may also work.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 4.01

The conservators cleaned the mural, several previously hidden details became visible.

Identify the two clause cores. Give two valid repairs that preserve both claims, and explain why the original comma is insufficient.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 4.01

Prove a sentence boundary | Review lesson 4.1

Answer: Clause cores: "conservators cleaned" and "details became visible." Two repairs are "The conservators cleaned the mural; several previously hidden details became visible" and "The conservators cleaned the mural. Several previously hidden details became visible."

Each side is an independent clause. A comma alone joins them incorrectly. A semicolon or a sentence-ending period supplies a valid boundary; a comma plus an appropriate coordinating conjunction could also be a valid repair.

If you missed it: On the next punctuation item, identify both clause cores before comparing marks.

Strategy in use: This drill permits more than one repair. On a four-choice question, evaluate the exact completions provided rather than assuming only one punctuation mark can ever work.

Drill 4.02

The schedule of workshops that introduce students to several different printmaking techniques ______ revised every autumn.

Complete the sentence with "is" or "are." Name the main subject and explain the role of "that introduce students."

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 4.02

Locate the subject and main verb | Review lesson 4.2

Answer: is. The main subject is "schedule." "That introduce students to several different printmaking techniques" describes the plural noun "workshops" and contains its own verb, "introduce."

The main clause is "The schedule ... is revised every autumn." The plural "workshops" controls the verb inside its descriptive clause, but the singular "schedule" controls the main verb phrase "is revised."

If you missed it: Mentally bracket a descriptive clause, then reconnect the main subject to the main verb.

Strategy in use: If you identify two verbs, ask which clause each belongs to. Do not let the nearer verb or noun replace the main sentence structure.

Drill 4.03

An early description called the tile surface uniformly smooth. New scans show that the surface has an uneven texture. In the center, shallow grooves alternate with small raised ridges.

Explain how the final sentence relates to the sentence immediately before it. Then explain why choosing a transition solely from the contrast with the first sentence would be risky.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 4.03

State the local relationship | Review lesson 4.3

Answer: The final sentence describes the uneven texture more precisely by identifying grooves and ridges in the center. Although those details also conflict with the early description, their local role is to develop the new finding.

The paragraph first contrasts an old description with new evidence, then elaborates on the evidence. A contrast earlier in a passage does not determine every later transition.

If you missed it: Before choosing a transition, summarize the proposition directly before the blank and the proposition containing the blank.

Strategy in use: Read the full short passage to locate the relationship, then focus on the ideas the transition actually joins.

Drill 4.04

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. Artist Mina Kade often explores the relationship between buildings and natural light.

2. Her wall installation North Window, completed in 2018, uses discarded window glass.

3. Her sculpture Folded Light, completed in 2021, also uses discarded window glass.

4. North Window hangs against an interior wall, while Folded Light stands on the floor.

5. Both works appeared in a retrospective devoted to Kade's use of salvaged materials.

The goal is to emphasize a similarity between North Window and Folded Light. Write one supported sentence, then identify the two notes that establish the shared feature.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 4.04

Convert a goal into a verification checklist | Review lesson 4.4

Answer: Both North Window and Folded Light use discarded window glass. The notes describing the material of each named work establish this similarity.

The sentence compares the two works directly and states a feature explicitly attributed to each. The artist's broader interests and the works' different dates are unnecessary for this goal.

If you missed it: For the next comparison, point to a separate supporting note for each object before accepting a shared-feature claim.

Strategy in use: A statement about an artist's general interests cannot substitute for a note about a specific work.
