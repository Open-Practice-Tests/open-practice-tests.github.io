Pacing, Triage, and Question Order

  • Chapter 2

Spend time where a specific next action can improve an answer.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Build a flexible budget from the remaining clock and the work still ahead.
  • Distinguish productive reasoning from a stall, then defer without leaving an unanswered item.
  • Test question order in practice and recover from a slow stretch without abandoning accuracy.

2.1 Build a flexible time budget

A standard Reading and Writing module contains 27 questions and lasts 32 minutes. [1] The full-clock average is about 71 seconds per question, but that includes any review. A useful starting plan is to reserve about 3 minutes, leaving 29 minutes for first visits: 1,740 seconds divided by 27 is about 64 seconds. Neither average is a deadline for a particular item. A clear punctuation decision may take much less time; a difficult comparison may deserve more.

For a sequential route, try the remaining-clock checkpoints below in practice. A first visit ends when you select an answer and move on, even if that answer is provisional and marked for review. The checkpoints protect time for questions you have not reached. They do not certify that the first 9 or 18 answers are correct, and they do not require you to abandon a nearly completed solution to meet a number. If your route jumps around, count first-visited items instead of using the displayed question number.

At a checkpoint, ask what remains: unvisited questions, useful flagged checks, and any unanswered items. For a quick first-pass estimate, subtract your planned review reserve from the remaining seconds, then divide by the number of unvisited questions. With 11 minutes left, 8 unvisited questions, and a 3-minute reserve, the estimate is 480 divided by 8, or 60 seconds each on average. The flags still need attention within the reserve; the calculation has not made them disappear.

Adjust the plan to the work, not just the arithmetic. Several familiar editing items may allow you to recover time; an unfamiliar passage may require selective deferral. Do not spend every spare second simply because a formula permits it. The reserve is a proposed starting point and can shrink when necessary. If you use approved different timing, build and rehearse the plan from your actual Bluebook settings. Standard-time checkpoints should not replace your approved arrangement.

W2.1: A budget that includes the unfinished work

Maya has 14 minutes left in a standard 27-question module. She has first-visited 15 questions and selected an answer to each; 3 of those answers are flagged. She wants to preserve a 3-minute review reserve. She is about to open a question she has not visited.

Which interpretation gives Maya the most useful first-pass budget?

A. She has about 70 seconds per unvisited question because all 14 remaining minutes can be divided among the 12 unvisited items.

B. She has about 44 seconds per question because the 12 unvisited items and the 3 flagged items must receive equal shares of the first-pass time.

C. She has about 55 seconds per unvisited question on average, with the flagged checks to be prioritized within the planned reserve.

D. She has about 24 seconds per question because the remaining first-pass time must be divided among all 27 questions.

Practice this lesson: 2.05, 2.06, 2.07.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.1

Answer: C

Correct choice: She has about 55 seconds per unvisited question on average, with the flagged checks to be prioritized within the planned reserve.

  1. Count the unvisited questions: 27 - 15 = 12. The flagged questions already have provisional answers.
  2. Subtract the reserve: 14 - 3 = 11 minutes, or 660 seconds, for first visits.
  3. Divide: 660 / 12 = 55 seconds per unvisited question on average. This is a planning estimate, not a hard limit.
  4. Keep the 3 flags in the review plan. Maya may resolve only the most useful ones if the reserve proves insufficient.

Why A fails: Fourteen minutes divided by 12 is 70 seconds, but this allocation spends the review reserve before the flagged checks begin.

Why B fails: The 44-second result comes from 660 / 15. It combines unvisited and flagged work even though the scenario has already reserved separate review time; it also assumes equal time needs.

Why D fails: Dividing 660 seconds by all 27 questions counts already completed first visits again and understates the usable first-pass budget.

Key takeaway: Count remaining work once, and make the review reserve explicit.

Strategy in use: The chosen budget preserves Maya's stated reserve; a different reserve would require a new calculation.

2.2 Keep working or defer

The relevant question is not just how long you have spent. It is what the next few moments are likely to accomplish. Productive work changes the decision: you locate the actual subject, identify the comparison group in a table, or find a qualifier that separates two choices. A stall repeats the same activity without new evidence: reading the entire passage again, alternating between two answers, or hoping an unfamiliar word will suddenly become familiar.

Use a three-part check: name the remaining uncertainty, name an action that could resolve it, and judge whether the clock can support that action. For example, "I need to check whether the finding applies to one site or every site" suggests a focused reread. "I still dislike both answers" does not yet supply a plan. If there is time, one purposeful attempt may turn that vague uncertainty into a useful check. If it produces no progress, stop renewing the same unsuccessful attempt.

The time already spent is gone whether you continue or move on. Ninety seconds of productive work can justify a short final check; ninety seconds of circular rereading does not create a claim on another ninety seconds. Conversely, a fast initial answer can deserve a revisit if you notice a concrete defect. Decisions should respond to evidence and remaining work, not to a rigid elapsed-time rule or a desire to make the previous effort feel worthwhile.

When deferring, select the best-supported answer available, then mark the question if a useful revisit is possible and move on. Retain a compact check such as "compare the two authors' claims" or "check the table's units"; do not rewrite the passage. A provisional answer can still be wrong, but a review mark alone is not an answer. Later, rank marked questions by the usefulness and feasibility of the next check. The question that consumed the most time does not automatically deserve the first return.

W2.2: Longer than average, but still making progress

After 90 seconds on an inference question, Arun has eliminated two choices. He has just noticed that one remaining answer refers to all sampling sites, while the other refers only to the shaded sites. He knows which sentence describes the study's scope. The clock shows 13:40, and 9 questions remain unvisited. He intends to keep about 3 minutes for review.

Which next action is best justified by Arun's situation?

A. Check the identified sentence for the scope difference, make the decision if it resolves the issue, and defer if the check produces no progress.

B. Select a provisional answer and leave immediately because spending more than 90 seconds on any question creates an unacceptable pacing problem.

C. Reread the entire passage twice before deciding because a question that has taken this long requires a complete restart.

D. Use another 90 seconds on the question so that the time invested is sufficient to justify leaving it behind.

Practice this lesson: 2.08, 2.09, 2.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.2

Answer: A

Correct choice: Check the identified sentence for the scope difference, make the decision if it resolves the issue, and defer if the check produces no progress.

  1. The uncertainty is specific: all sites versus shaded sites. Arun has located a sentence that can settle it.
  2. The clock permits a brief check. After a 3-minute reserve, 10:40 remains for the current decision and 9 unvisited questions.
  3. Try the identified check, not an entire restart. If it resolves the scope difference, answer and move on.
  4. If the check does not clarify the answer, select a provisional choice and defer; the plan does not authorize open-ended work.

Why B fails: Deferral can protect time, but the reason given is a universal 90-second cutoff. Here a newly identified, potentially decisive check supports a brief continuation.

Why C fails: A complete reread may sometimes be useful, but two automatic rereads ignore the exact location of the remaining evidence and add unnecessary work.

Why D fails: Previously spent time cannot be recovered by spending the same amount again. Further time needs a useful action, not a matching investment.

Key takeaway: A short, decisive check can be worth doing even after a slow start.

Strategy in use: The available time and identified evidence make continuation reasonable here; either condition can change in another case.

Practice this lesson: 2.08, 2.09, 2.10.

2.3 Choose and test a question route

A useful default is to work forward, selecting provisional answers and deferring selectively when a question stalls. This route limits navigation and makes it easier to see how much of the module remains. It does not require equal time on every question. It also avoids deciding in advance that an entire skill is either easy or expendable. A familiar-looking grammar item can be difficult, while a reading question can have a clear, directly supported answer.

An alternative route may help a particular student, but it must solve an observed problem. For example, a student who repeatedly runs out of time before reaching editing questions might test completing identifiable editing items earlier, then returning to reading. The route needs a clear return point and a way to track unvisited questions. Recognize tasks from their stems and answer choices; do not assume a fixed question number marks the start of a grammar block. Simply jumping to a later displayed number does not mean you have completed the earlier work.

Test routes on fresh, reasonably comparable mixed material under the same timing. Keep other major conditions stable, and alternate which route you try first across practice sessions. Record correct answers, unanswered items, accuracy by skill, time to first-visit completion, and navigation problems. A route that finishes sooner but creates more careless errors may be a poor trade. A route that feels calmer deserves consideration, but that feeling should be checked against what actually happened.

One comparison is a clue, not a verdict: different forms may vary in difficulty, and one unfamiliar passage can affect a small sample. Repeating the same questions under a new route mixes the effect of order with memory. Selecting only your easiest questions makes the comparison even less useful. Prefer the simplest route that repeatedly supports accurate completion in your practice. If no alternative has shown a clear benefit, retain the familiar route for the test instead of making a last-minute experiment.

W2.3: A promising result that is not yet a conclusion

Lena uses a forward route on one fresh mixed practice module: 23 answers correct, no blanks, and first visits complete with 1 minute left. On a different fresh module, she tries editing questions first: 24 answers correct, no blanks, and first visits complete with 3 minutes left. The sets were selected to cover similar skills, but their difficulty is not known to be identical. She noticed no navigation error.

What is the most defensible next step?

A. Adopt editing first permanently because the increase of one correct answer proves that the route is more accurate.

B. Reject editing first because any route that requires navigation must reduce the time available for answering.

C. Repeat both modules in the opposite order and use the higher repeated score to determine the better route.

D. Treat editing first as a promising candidate and compare the routes again on fresh, reasonably comparable modules, recording accuracy and completion as well as time.

Practice this lesson: 2.11, 2.12, 2.13.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.3

Answer: D

Correct choice: Treat editing first as a promising candidate and compare the routes again on fresh, reasonably comparable modules, recording accuracy and completion as well as time.

  1. Editing first performed better on the two recorded measures in this comparison and produced no observed omission.
  2. The modules differ, so their difficulty may explain part or all of the difference. A one-answer change cannot isolate the route's effect.
  3. Test again with fresh mixed material, consistent timing, and the same measurements. Check skill accuracy and navigation costs as well.
  4. Use repeated evidence to decide whether the benefit is stable enough to justify changing the familiar route.

Why A fails: One additional correct answer on a different form is encouraging, but it does not prove that the route caused the difference or will keep doing so.

Why B fails: Navigation has a cost, yet an alternative route can still improve total performance. Lena's results justify further testing rather than automatic rejection.

Why C fails: Lena may remember the questions or explanations. Repeated-item scores would not cleanly measure how each route handles unfamiliar work.

Key takeaway: A useful route earns its place through fresh practice, not a slogan or one favorable score.

Strategy in use: This example supports another comparison, not a claim that editing first is generally preferable.

2.4 Recover when behind and finish the module

Being behind a checkpoint is information, not a verdict on the module. First replace "I am too slow" with a count: time left, unvisited questions, and unanswered items. Then identify the behavior to change. If repeated rereading caused the delay, shorten unproductive loops; if one difficult question caused it, do not let that exception turn into a rule for every remaining item. Keep enough reading to identify the task and the evidence. Skipping essential context can create errors without recovering useful time.

Recalculate once, then act. Suppose 6 minutes remain and 6 questions are unvisited. Preserving 2 minutes leaves 4 minutes, or 40 seconds per new question on average. That estimate signals the need for focused solving and selective provisional answers; it does not require six guesses or exactly 40 seconds on each item. If a strict reserve would leave almost no first-pass time, reduce it. The purpose of a reserve is to support completion and useful review, not to keep an untouched block of time while accessible questions remain unseen.

As time becomes scarce, check that each question has a selected answer. There is no penalty for guessing. [5] Use supported eliminations when available, but do not spend the final seconds inventing a reason to prefer a letter. Once selections are complete, prioritize checks with a realistic chance of changing the decision: an unchecked comparison row or a possibly missed word can be more useful than reconstructing an unfamiliar argument from the beginning. This is a qualitative judgment about remaining work, not an estimate of SAT point values.

Within the current module, you can return to questions; after leaving the module, you cannot return to it. [2] Finish that module's work before the boundary. If time remains, do not change a supported answer merely because it has been selected for a while. Review needs a specific question and a stopping point: "Does this option reverse the authors?" is actionable; "Read everything again until I feel certain" is not. When the module ends, carry forward your routine, not an attempt to solve a question you can no longer change.

W2.4: Seventy seconds and two unanswered questions

The clock shows 1:10. Jo has visited every question but accidentally left two without selected answers. Jo has narrowed each of those questions to two choices and can select a provisional answer to each quickly. Two other questions are flagged: one needs a check of a single table row, while the other would require rereading both texts and rebuilding their relationship.

Which order of actions best addresses the remaining work?

A. Rebuild the paired-text relationship first, since the question requiring more work should receive the largest share of the remaining time.

B. Select provisional answers for the two unanswered questions, verify that selections are complete, then use the remaining time for the specific table-row check.

C. Complete the table-row check first, then wait until the final few seconds to select answers to the two unanswered questions.

D. Select provisional answers for the two unanswered questions, then change one flagged answer without rereading to avoid leaving both uncertain selections unchanged.

Practice this lesson: 2.14, 2.15, 2.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W2.4

Answer: B

Correct choice: Select provisional answers for the two unanswered questions, verify that selections are complete, then use the remaining time for the specific table-row check.

  1. Two visited questions still lack selections. Jo can address these omissions quickly using the work already done.
  2. Answer them now, then verify completion rather than trusting that the review marks or earlier visits saved an answer.
  3. Of the flagged work, the single-row check has a specific, feasible next action. The paired-text reconstruction is more demanding under this clock.
  4. Change an answer only if the check supplies a reason. If time runs out, keep the best-supported selections already made.

Why A fails: The largest task does not automatically deserve priority. Starting it first risks leaving two questions unanswered and losing the opportunity for a focused check.

Why C fails: The table check is useful, but Jo can eliminate the immediate risk of two blanks before undertaking it. Waiting leaves answer selection vulnerable to the clock.

Why D fails: Uncertainty alone does not justify a change. Completing selections is sensible; changing a flagged answer without evidence is not a useful review action.

Key takeaway: Complete selections first when the clock is short, then revisit the most feasible unresolved check.

Strategy in use: The relative review priority comes from these stated tasks and time constraints, not from the skill labels themselves.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

• Use checkpoints to notice a developing time problem. They are approximate rehearsal targets, not individual question deadlines.

• Continue when you can name a useful next check and afford it; defer when more time is producing no new evidence.

• Select a provisional answer before moving on. A review mark identifies work to revisit; it does not answer the question.

• Keep a route only if fresh practice supports it after accounting for accuracy, omissions, and navigation cost.

• Near the end, ensure every question has a selected answer, then spend the remaining time on specific, feasible checks.

Begin with four short drills, one per lesson, then complete twelve multiple-choice questions, three per lesson. Labels distinguish SAT-format questions from strategy decisions. Give the evidence or stated condition that decides each item. Model drill responses are examples; another well-supported response may also work.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 2.01

The clock shows 16:00. You have first-visited 20 of 27 questions and selected an answer to each. Three are flagged. You would like to reserve 3 minutes for review.

Calculate the approximate average first-pass time available per unvisited question, then state one important limit of that estimate.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Remaining-work budget | Review lesson 2.1

Answer: There are 7 unvisited questions. The first-pass budget is 16 - 3 = 13 minutes, or 780 seconds. Dividing by 7 gives about 111 seconds per unvisited question on average. This is not a command to spend 111 seconds on each item; the flagged checks still compete for the 3-minute reserve.

The calculation separates unvisited work from review work and counts each once. Having more time per unvisited item does not make the flagged answers correct or ensure that all can be resolved.

If you missed it: On a fresh timed set, record the number of genuinely unvisited items at one checkpoint before calculating the average.

Strategy in use: A budget helps you allocate attention; it does not predict which question will be difficult.

Answer: Select the better-supported provisional answer, mark the question, and move on. If time permits a return, compare Text 1's actual claim with the claims attributed to it in the two remaining choices.

An honest route comparison | Review lesson 2.3

Answer: Use each route on two fresh modules under the same timing, alternating routes across sessions. Record correct answers, blanks, accuracy by skill, time to finish first visits, and navigation problems. Treat a small difference cautiously because module difficulty may vary.

Fresh mixed material reduces memory and self-selection effects. Multiple observations provide more information than a single favorable result, although they still cannot make the forms identical.

Strategy in use: The aim is a practical decision based on evidence, not a formal guarantee about which route caused every score difference.

Recover without abandoning the text | Review lesson 2.4

Answer: One reasonable plan is to reduce the reserve to 1 minute, leaving 3:30, or 210 seconds, for 5 unvisited questions: 42 seconds each on average. Stop circular rereading, select provisional answers when stalled, and protect the final answer-completion check.
