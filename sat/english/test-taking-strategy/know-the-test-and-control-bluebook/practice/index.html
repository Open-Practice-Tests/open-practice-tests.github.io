Question 5

  • Guide question 1.05
  • Foundation

Evan has three minutes remaining in Module 1 and has selected an answer to every question. One marked answer depends on a contrast he has not checked. He proposes checking it after he begins Module 2 so that he can use these three minutes to rest.

Which fact most directly requires Evan to revise this plan?

Why this answer

Respect the module boundary | Review lesson 1.1

Correct choice: He will not be able to return to Module 1 after leaving it.

Evan's planned check crosses a module boundary. If he wants to examine the unresolved contrast, he must do so while Module 1 is still accessible. The fact that an answer is already selected does not remove this timing constraint.

B: Marking an item does not lock its selected response within the active module.

C: The modules have separate time budgets; this claim does not justify postponing a Module 1 check.

D: A review mark does not turn a selected response into an unscored response.

If you missed it: Before a module closes, resolve any useful outstanding checks within that module's remaining time.

Strategy in use: The issue is when the check remains possible, not a requirement to spend every remaining second reviewing.

Question 6

  • Guide question 1.06
  • Transfer

When Bluebook's five-minute alert appears, Grace has four questions she has not yet examined and two earlier marked questions with provisional answers. She says, 'The alert means I should abandon the new questions and review only the marked ones.'

Which interpretation of the alert is most useful under these conditions?

Why this answer

Use a time alert as information | Review lesson 1.1

Correct choice: It reports the remaining time; she should use it to reassess the unfinished work rather than treat it as a required change of task.

The alert supplies timing information, not a mandated review phase. Grace should account for the four unseen questions as well as the two selected but unresolved responses. Automatically abandoning all new work would misuse the alert.

A: Students may continue answering within the active module. The alert does not restrict the final minutes to review.

B: Marks and answer selections are separate actions; removing marks cannot supply missing responses.

D: The alert announces time remaining in the current budget, not an extension of that budget.

If you missed it: At a clock check, identify unseen items, unanswered items, and useful marked checks before choosing the next action.

Strategy in use: A timer can prompt a decision; it cannot decide which question is worth your next minute.

Review lesson: 1.1 Module boundaries and the clock

Question 7

  • Guide question 1.07
  • Challenge

With 35 seconds left in Module 1, Talia has selected every answer. One marked item has a specific unresolved issue: she meant to choose the option limited to surviving manuscripts, but she may have selected the option about all manuscripts. She can find the two options immediately. Another marked item felt difficult, but she cannot name any defect in her selected answer. She proposes waiting until Module 2 to inspect both.

Which plan best uses the remaining access to Module 1 under these conditions?

Why this answer

Distinguish a final check from a new search | Review lesson 1.1

Correct choice: Check the manuscript item's selected scope now, correct it if needed, and keep the other answer unless time allows a specific additional check.

The manuscript item has a narrow, immediately accessible check that could reveal a selection error. The other item has only an unspecific feeling attached to it. Given 35 seconds and no later Module 1 access, the named scope check is the strongest first use of the time. Keeping the other response is conditional, not a claim that first answers are always correct.

A: Discomfort alone does not identify what to inspect. A full restart may consume the remaining time while the concrete selection concern goes unchecked.

B: Selected answers can still contain correctable errors. The manuscript concern names one that a brief check could resolve.

C: Equal time treats the checks as equally useful despite different evidence and startup costs. Restarting both passages ignores the immediately available distinction.

If you missed it: When marking an item, identify the exact word, relation, or selection you would check on return.

Strategy in use: This choice follows from the stated access and clarity of the check, not a universal rule to review scope questions first.

Question 8

  • Guide question 1.08
  • Transfer

During Module 2, Ben answers a difficult inference item and then sees a short punctuation item. He assumes the punctuation item appeared because his inference answer was wrong. He wants to return to the inference item and change his answer even though he has found no new evidence.

Which response best addresses Ben's reason for changing the answer?

Why this answer

Reject question-level routing speculation | Review lesson 1.2

Correct choice: The SAT does not adapt after each question within a module; the next item's appearance supplies no reason to change the inference answer.

Ben is treating a multistage adaptive test as though each answer selects the next question. That premise is wrong. He may revisit the inference if he identifies a genuine uncertainty, but the following item's length or apparent ease is not answer evidence.

A: The next item proves neither correctness nor incorrectness. It also cannot justify a blanket refusal to review.

C: Changing an earlier answer does not trigger a new sequence of later questions within the active module.

D: Counting punctuation items would still not reveal whether the inference answer follows from its passage.

If you missed it: Before changing an answer, name the new textual or grammatical evidence supporting the change.

Strategy in use: Distinguish information about the current answer from impressions about the test's design.

Review lesson: 1.2 Adaptation and scoring without speculation

Question 9

  • Guide question 1.09
  • Challenge

A student has 80 seconds left and a response selected for every question. A short editing item has one unresolved clause-boundary check that the student knows how to perform. A dense reading item has already received two unsuccessful rereads, and the student cannot identify a fresh approach. The student wants to spend all 80 seconds on the reading item because its difficulty must make it worth more points.

Which reasoning best supports a more defensible review decision?

Why this answer

Separate review value from imagined point value | Review lesson 1.2

Correct choice: The student's known clause check offers a concrete chance to improve an answer; apparent difficulty does not supply a reliable point value for allocating the time.

The student can compare the usefulness of the proposed checks without inventing scoring weights. One item has a specific available method; the other currently has no new approach. Starting with the clause check is defensible under these conditions, while a claim about exact points is not needed or supported.

A: The scoring premise is incorrect, and shortness alone does not determine the value of review.

B: Apparent difficulty does not reveal an exact scoring weight. Equal division also ignores the different prospects of the two checks.

C: Density does not establish that an item is a pretest question. The student's response should not depend on that guess.

If you missed it: Choose a review target by the specific correction you might make and the work needed to test it.

Strategy in use: No estimated SAT point payoff is required. The decision compares the available methods and their likely usefulness qualitatively.

Question 10

  • Guide question 1.10
  • Transfer

Two classmates count the same number of correct Reading and Writing answers on different official adaptive practice tests but receive different section scores. One concludes that every future test with that number correct should be assigned the higher of the two scores.

Which interpretation is best supported?

Why this answer

Interpret practice results without a universal conversion | Review lesson 1.2

Correct choice: Use each test's reported score and examine the actual errors; the same correct total need not map to one score across different adaptive forms.

Question characteristics contribute to adaptive scoring, so a count alone is not a universal conversion rule. The official score for each completed form and the student's concrete errors are more useful than replacing results with a homemade fixed mapping.

B: It assumes the very equivalence that the scoring model does not require.

C: Averaging two observations creates a number, not a validated conversion that applies to other forms.

D: Different scores at the same correct count do not by themselves show a routing error.

If you missed it: Record the reported practice score together with skill-level errors and process observations; keep the correct count as a separate description.

Strategy in use: This does not make correct answers unimportant. It limits what one aggregate count can tell you about scaled scores.

Question 11

  • Guide question 1.11
  • Foundation

Ari has crossed out options A, B, and D on a practice item and intends to answer C. The screen still shows no selected response. Ari plans to move on because C is the only option not crossed out.

What should Ari do before moving on?

Why this answer

Confirm the intended response is selected | Review lesson 1.3

Correct choice: Select C and confirm that it is the recorded response.

The scenario explicitly states that no response is selected. The eliminator records Ari's rejections, but Ari still needs to enter the intended answer. Selecting C resolves the mismatch between the decision and the recorded response.

A: A review mark is a reminder to return, not an answer selection.

B: Undoing eliminations does not ask the app to solve or answer the item.

D: It contradicts the stated response status and confuses elimination with selection.

If you missed it: Add a quick selection check to the end of your answering routine when using the eliminator.

Strategy in use: Interface fluency includes confirming what the app has recorded, not merely knowing which answer you intend.

Review lesson: 1.3 Purposeful tool use

Question 12

  • Guide question 1.12
  • Challenge

Dina crossed out option B because she read a finding as applying to every surveyed building. On a focused reread, she notices that the text says the finding applies only to buildings constructed before 1950. Option B preserves that limit; her selected option C omits it. She has enough time to compare both complete choices and sees no reason to question the passage's wording.

Which action best uses the tool and the new evidence?

Why this answer

Repair a faulty elimination | Review lesson 1.3

Correct choice: Undo B's elimination, compare B and C against the limited claim, and change the selection if that full comparison supports B.

Dina now knows that her reason for eliminating B rested on a misreading. Restoring B allows a legitimate comparison. The year-related limit gives her a decisive issue to investigate, but she should still check the rest of B rather than assume one matching phrase proves the whole answer.

A: Elimination is reversible precisely because a judgment can need correction. Preserving a known misreading does not protect accuracy.

C: An option may include the right date yet distort another part of the finding. The complete choice still needs verification.

D: Earlier confidence is not evidence that C preserves the passage's restriction. The new textual information directly challenges that selection.

If you missed it: When an elimination's reason fails, restore the option and evaluate its entire claim again.

Strategy in use: Be willing to reverse a tool action while remaining skeptical of a newly attractive answer. Both habits support careful review.

Question 13

  • Guide question 1.13
  • Transfer

When leaving a paired-text question temporarily, Luis has selected a provisional answer. He understands each author's main claim. His remaining uncertainty is whether the second author rejects the first author's conclusion or only questions the method used to reach it. He wants a brief note that will guide his return.

Which note would be most useful?

Why this answer

Make a return note actionable | Review lesson 1.3

Correct choice: Check whether Text 2 challenges conclusion or method.

Luis has identified a specific distinction that could change the answer. Option A preserves that distinction and tells him what to inspect. It avoids restarting from the topic or allowing discomfort to dictate a change.

B: It records difficulty but loses the precise issue Luis already identified. More confidence is not a defined evidence check.

C: Sharing a topic does not imply agreement and does not resolve conclusion versus method.

D: A possible error is a reason to check, not an instruction to switch letters without evidence.

If you missed it: Write review notes as questions that the passage can answer.

Strategy in use: Useful notes compress the next reasoning step rather than summarize your emotional reaction.

Question 14

  • Guide question 1.14
  • Transfer

A question asks which finding would most strongly support a researcher's claim that a particular coating slows corrosion under high humidity. Priya has identified the claim correctly. She is about to choose an option that repeats the claim but reports no observation about coated and uncoated materials.

Which next step best matches the task?

Why this answer

Distinguish a claim from its supporting evidence | Review lesson 1.4

Correct choice: Compare the findings for observations that would make slower corrosion under the stated humidity conditions more credible.

The task asks for a finding that supports a claim, not a paraphrase of the claim or a description of the researcher's purpose. Priya should look for relevant observations and preserve the high-humidity condition when assessing their effect.

A: Repeating a proposition does not independently supply evidence for it.

B: A fact about cost may be true while providing no support for the corrosion claim.

C: Purpose is a different task. A broad description of what the researcher is doing would not necessarily test the claim.

If you missed it: For evidence questions, state the claim and ask what observation would increase or decrease its support.

Strategy in use: Question recognition keeps you from rewarding an option simply because it sounds like the passage.

Question 15

  • Guide question 1.15
  • Transfer

An editing question offers four completions that differ mainly in punctuation. The text begins with an introductory dependent clause, followed by the subject and verb of the main clause. Mateo wants to pick the shortest-looking option because he remembers that concise writing is often effective.

Which approach most directly addresses the tested decision?

Why this answer

Verify an editing completion structurally | Review lesson 1.4

Correct choice: Identify the clause boundary, insert the candidate punctuation into the fixed sentence, and check that the complete structure is grammatical.

The stated answer differences point to a punctuation decision. The relevant representation is the relationship between the introductory dependent clause and the main clause, followed by a complete-insertion check. General preferences about brevity do not establish that structure.

A: Shortness and familiarity do not determine whether punctuation correctly connects the clauses.

C: A spoken pause is not a dependable test of the grammatical boundary.

D: The introductory clause is part of the structure being tested. Removing it from consideration can conceal the punctuation error.

If you missed it: On a punctuation item, name the structures on both sides before judging the mark between them.

Strategy in use: Inspecting answer differences helps identify the likely issue, but the fixed surrounding text supplies the rule's actual conditions.

Question 16

  • Guide question 1.16
  • Challenge

A passage first presents an early explanation for a change in bird migration, then describes measurements inconsistent with that explanation, and finally proposes a narrower alternative. The question asks for the passage's main purpose. Jules predicted 'defend the early explanation' after the first sentence. All four choices mention migration, and one uses several phrases from that opening sentence.

Which sequence would best help Jules choose an answer?

Why this answer

Revise a provisional representation | Review lesson 1.4

Correct choice: Revise the purpose map to include the challenge and alternative, then check which choice accurately describes the passage's overall movement.

Jules's prediction became outdated when the passage challenged the early explanation and offered a narrower alternative. The complete purpose map must capture that movement. A correct purpose answer may mention the early explanation as the view being reassessed, rather than as a position the passage defends.

A: It treats the initial prediction as a commitment and ignores the later evidence that changes the passage's direction.

B: The alternative matters in relation to the earlier explanation and contrary measurements. A summary of it alone may omit the passage's main rhetorical work.

D: A challenged explanation can be essential to describing the text's purpose. The defect would be misrepresenting its role, not merely mentioning it.

If you missed it: When a passage changes direction, revise your one-sentence representation before comparing purpose choices.

Strategy in use: Prediction helps only while it remains accountable to the whole passage. Keyword overlap cannot repair an incorrect role assignment.
