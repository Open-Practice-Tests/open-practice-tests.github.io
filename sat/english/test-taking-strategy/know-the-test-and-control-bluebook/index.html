1.1 Module boundaries and the clock

For the standard-time adaptive SAT, Reading and Writing has two modules of 27 questions and 32 minutes each: 54 questions in 64 minutes. There is no standard break between these two modules; the 10-minute break comes between Reading and Writing and Math. [1] Treat each module as its own work period. These figures describe the standard format; follow your actual Bluebook timing and approved accommodations if they differ.

You can move among questions and revise answers in the current module until its time expires. After leaving a module, you cannot return to it. [2] This boundary matters more than how much time remains in the whole test. Suppose you finish your first visit to every question with two minutes left. Those minutes are your opportunity to check that module's responses. A plan to repair a doubtful Module 1 answer during Module 2 depends on an opportunity that will not exist.

Keep three states separate: selected, marked, and supported. Selected means you have entered a response. Marked means you want to return. Supported means you can justify the response with the text or a rule. A question can be selected and marked at the same time. It can also be selected without being well supported. Before moving away from a difficult item, choose your provisional best-supported answer; mark it when you have a useful reason to revisit. Do not let a bookmark stand in for a response.

Use the clock to trigger a useful decision. A check might tell you to continue a productive comparison, defer a stalled item, or inspect unanswered questions. Repeatedly looking at the same countdown without changing your action only interrupts reading. Bluebook alerts you at five minutes remaining, when the timer can no longer be hidden. [3] Rehearse your own earlier checkpoints instead of waiting for that alert to discover unfinished work. Chapter 2 develops a flexible pacing plan; the first habit is simpler: know which module is open, what work remains, and what the next check will accomplish.

W1.1: The unfinished response behind a bookmark

With 45 seconds left in Module 1, Nia has reached every question. The question menu shows one unanswered item, Question 12, which is also marked for review. She remembers narrowing it to two options. All other questions have selected answers. She plans to start Module 2 and return to Question 12 during a quiet moment there.

Which revision to Nia's plan best addresses the immediate problem?

A. Remove Question 12's review mark, then use the remaining time to check a response she already supports.

B. Return to Question 12 now, select the best-supported remaining option, and make any further check within the time left in Module 1.

C. Keep Question 12 marked and use the start of Module 2 to decide between the two remaining options.

D. Stay on Question 12 until she can explain every option, even if she has not selected a response when time expires.

Practice this lesson: 1.05, 1.06, 1.07.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.1

Answer: B

Correct choice: Return to Question 12 now, select the best-supported remaining option, and make any further check within the time left in Module 1.

  1. Identify the immediate exposure: Question 12 has no selected answer. Being marked does not resolve that problem.
  2. Apply the navigation boundary: Module 2 will not provide access to Module 1's questions.
  3. Choose a supported provisional response while the item is still accessible. The two-option comparison gives Nia a starting point.
  4. Use any remaining seconds for a specific check, without making certainty a condition for entering an answer.

Why A fails: Removing a mark changes the reminder, not the unanswered status. It would leave the only blank unresolved while spending time elsewhere.

Why C fails: The proposed return crosses the module boundary. The bookmark does not preserve later access to a finished module.

Why D fails: A complete explanation would be useful in practice, but waiting to enter any response creates an avoidable blank at the deadline.

Key takeaway: Enter a response before a module closes; a bookmark is a reminder, not an answer.

Strategy in use: When time is short, separate recording your best current answer from continuing to investigate it. You can do the first without pretending the second is finished.

1.2 Adaptation and scoring without speculation

The SAT adapts between modules, not after each question. Module 1 performance determines the difficulty mix in Module 2, and scored work in both modules contributes to the section score. Each second-module route still contains a mixture of difficulties. [4] A difficult passage followed by a straightforward editing item is therefore not a message that your immediately preceding answer was wrong. It is a new task to solve.

Do not convert a feeling into a routing diagnosis. A vocabulary item may feel easy because you know that particular word; a short sentence may feel difficult because its structure is unfamiliar. Those reactions describe your experience of the items. They do not establish the module's overall difficulty mix or your final score. The useful response to an unusually difficult question is local: identify the task, look for the decisive evidence, and decide whether another focused check is worth the time.

Scores are not a simple total of identical point values. College Board explains that students with the same number correct can receive different scores because the characteristics of the questions matter. Two pretest questions in each module do not affect scores. [4] Neither fact gives you a dependable way to price a particular item while testing. Do not label an odd-looking question 'experimental' and abandon it, or spend extra minutes on a dense passage because you assume it is worth more. Choose work by your prospects of solving it and the time it needs.

There is no deduction for an incorrect answer, and official guidance favors answering as well as you can rather than leaving blanks. [5] This is permission to make a reasoned choice under uncertainty, not a claim that guesses earn equal points or that elimination guarantees success. Keep your working goal concrete: answer each question as well as the available evidence and time allow. After practice, use the official score report for that particular test and inspect the mistakes themselves. Avoid universal tables claiming that any fixed number of misses always produces the same score.

W1.2: Two easy questions do not identify a route

The first two questions Mira sees in Module 2 use familiar vocabulary. She concludes that she received the lower-difficulty route and that careful work can no longer improve her result. She has 29 minutes left and has not examined the remaining questions.

Which response best corrects Mira's reasoning and supports her next action?

A. The familiar words prove she is on the higher-difficulty route, so she should relax and work at her usual pace.

B. She should scan all remaining passages to estimate the route before deciding which questions deserve careful attention.

C. She should deliberately answer a difficult-looking question incorrectly so that the next question becomes easier.

D. Two familiar items do not establish the route or score; she should keep solving because her remaining scored responses still matter.

Practice this lesson: 1.08, 1.09, 1.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.2

Answer: D

Correct choice: Two familiar items do not establish the route or score; she should keep solving because her remaining scored responses still matter.

  1. Separate the observation from the inference: Mira recognizes two words, but that does not reveal the full difficulty mix.
  2. Recall that the test does not select the next question in response to each individual answer within the module.
  3. Reject the claim that the result is already fixed. Remaining scored work in Module 2 contributes to her section performance.
  4. Return attention to the next question rather than trying to diagnose a route from appearances.

Why A fails: It replaces one unsupported routing claim with another. Familiarity does not prove either route.

Why B fails: A route estimate from a scan would remain uncertain and would consume time without supplying evidence for any answer.

Why C fails: It assumes question-by-question adaptation, which this test does not use, and sacrifices a response without a benefit.

Key takeaway: Treat perceived difficulty as a signal about the current task, not as a score report.

Strategy in use: The correction is not forced optimism about a route. It is recognizing that the route guess is unsupported and does not help answer the next question.

1.3 Purposeful tool use

A tool earns its place when it reduces a specific burden. Bluebook offers Highlights & Notes, a Line Reader, an Option Eliminator, Mark for Review, a Question Menu, and zoom. The eliminator lets you cross out an option and undo that action. [6] Learn these controls in the Bluebook test preview before a timed rehearsal. Tool fluency means finding and using what you need without interrupting the decision you are trying to make.

Highlight the hinge, not the whole passage. Useful targets include a contrast, a limitation, a research claim, or the exact goal in a synthesis task. If a passage says a change occurred 'only in the coastal samples,' those words may prevent an answer from expanding the result to every sample. A short note such as 'coastal only' can serve the same purpose. Highlighting four complete sentences makes the crucial restriction no easier to find. Use the Line Reader or zoom if it helps you track text, and test that choice during practice.

Cross out an option when you can state its defect: 'reverses the two authors,' 'changes some to all,' or 'leaves a dependent clause alone.' A crossed-out choice records a judgment; it does not prove the judgment was correct. If you discover that you misread the passage, undo the elimination and reconsider. Likewise, crossing out three choices is not the same action as selecting the remaining response. Before moving on, confirm that the answer you intend is actually selected.

Use marks and notes to preserve a useful next step. 'Check what however contrasts' is more helpful than 'confusing.' Marking every moment of discomfort produces a queue with little information about what to do next. A supported answer can remain unmarked even if the passage felt difficult. A marked answer can remain selected even while one issue needs checking. When you return through the menu, perform the named check rather than rereading everything automatically. If tools take longer than the problem they solve, simplify: a brief mental label or a small notation on provided scratch paper may be sufficient.

W1.3: A note that preserves the actual issue

During practice, Omar reads a study reporting that a material reduced heat loss in dry conditions but produced no measurable reduction in humid conditions. He is deciding between an answer claiming reduced heat loss in dry conditions and one claiming reduced heat loss in both dry and humid conditions. He understands the rest of the passage but keeps losing track of this restriction when returning from the answer choices.

Which use of the tools most directly addresses Omar's difficulty?

A. Highlight the contrast between dry and humid conditions, then compare the scope of each remaining answer with that contrast.

B. Highlight every sentence so that all the study's information receives equal visual emphasis.

C. Cross out the longer of the remaining answers and select the shorter answer before rereading the restriction.

D. Mark the question without a note, then restart the entire passage on every return until one answer feels familiar.

Practice this lesson: 1.11, 1.12, 1.13.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.3

Answer: A

Correct choice: Highlight the contrast between dry and humid conditions, then compare the scope of each remaining answer with that contrast.

  1. Name the burden: Omar is losing one condition-specific restriction, not failing to understand every sentence.
  2. Preserve that restriction where he can find it quickly. The contrast, rather than the material's name, determines answer scope.
  3. Use the tool output in a comparison: an answer claiming reduced heat loss in both conditions conflicts with the reported lack of a measurable reduction in humid conditions.
  4. Select the supported option after checking its complete wording, rather than treating the highlight itself as a solution.

Why B fails: Equal emphasis on all sentences does not make the decisive limitation easier to relocate.

Why C fails: Length does not establish whether an answer preserves the study's conditions. Either a short or long answer could distort them.

Why D fails: The repeated restart does not preserve the restriction Omar already identified. Familiarity is not evidence that an answer is supported.

Key takeaway: Use a tool to preserve the information that distinguishes the answers.

Strategy in use: Omar still has to evaluate the options. The annotation reduces the chance of losing a key restriction; it does not replace reasoning.

1.4 Question recognition and a repeatable solving routine

Before processing details, identify the job. Reading and Writing includes short passages or passage pairs, normally followed by one multiple-choice question; its four domains test reading, analysis, editing, and revision. [7] A useful default is to read the question stem first, then read enough of the stimulus to understand the relevant claims and constraints. This is not permission to skip context. An inference may depend on two sentences working together; a transition may connect back beyond its immediate neighbor.

Turn the stem into a brief instruction. 'Most strongly supports' asks what evidence would strengthen a claim. 'Main purpose' asks what the text does. 'Conforms to the conventions' asks for a grammatical completion. A rhetorical-synthesis goal might require both identifying a shared feature and emphasizing a difference. These tasks can concern the same topic but require different evidence. Topic recognition tells you what the passage discusses; task recognition tells you what a correct answer must accomplish.

Use a four-part routine: name the task, build the needed representation, compare the choices, verify the winner. The representation may be a one-sentence claim, a map of two authors' positions, a subject and finite verb, or a list of required goal elements. Keep it small enough to use. For example, 'both value the archive; disagree about its completeness' can guide a cross-text comparison better than memorizing dates and names that the question does not test. Your first interpretation is provisional; revise it when the text gives you a reason.

Make verification task-specific. For a reading answer, locate the supporting words and test the choice's full scope. For editing, insert the chosen wording into the fixed sentence and check the resulting structure. For synthesis, check both factual accuracy and every part of the requested goal. A final reread that merely asks 'Does this sound right?' may miss the exact problem the item tests. If a check fails, return to the competing choices. If it succeeds and no concrete uncertainty remains, select the answer and move on. The routine should guide attention, not become four lengthy written summaries for every question.

W1.4: The same topic can support different tasks

Leah sees notes about two public gardens. The prompt asks her to emphasize a difference in how the gardens collect rainwater. She begins arranging every date in chronological order because both sets of notes contain opening dates. She has not yet compared the gardens' collection methods.

Which adjustment best aligns Leah's work with the task?

A. Finish arranging the dates, since every detail in the notes must appear in a correct synthesis answer.

B. Choose the sentence mentioning both gardens, since naming both is enough to establish the requested difference.

C. Identify each garden's rainwater-collection method, compare the relevant notes, and test which choice accurately emphasizes that difference.

D. Choose the most detailed sentence about either garden, since additional information makes a comparison more complete.

Practice this lesson: 1.14, 1.15, 1.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W1.4

Answer: C

Correct choice: Identify each garden's rainwater-collection method, compare the relevant notes, and test which choice accurately emphasizes that difference.

  1. Name both parts of the task: emphasize a difference, specifically in rainwater collection.
  2. Identify the needed information: the collection method used by each garden. Opening dates do not answer this stated comparison.
  3. Build a small representation of the two methods before evaluating how the options express their difference.
  4. Verify both the facts and the requested comparative emphasis in the chosen sentence.

Why A fails: Synthesis requires relevant selection, not inclusion of every note. Chronology does not address the specified difference in methods.

Why B fails: A sentence can name both gardens yet discuss a similarity or an unrelated difference. Both names alone do not fulfill the goal.

Why D fails: Detail about one garden can remain accurate while failing to compare the two collection methods. Quantity of information does not determine goal fulfillment.

Key takeaway: Let the requested operation determine which details deserve attention.

Strategy in use: Goal-first reading narrows the search. It still requires checking the relevant notes rather than selecting an option from its wording alone.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

• Treat the current module as the boundary for answering and review. Confirm that intended responses are selected.

• Do not infer a score, routing result, or item value from how a few questions feel.

• Give every annotation, elimination, and review mark a specific job; revise the tool action when its reasoning changes.

• Name the task, build a compact representation, compare the choices, and verify the winner with the kind of evidence the task requires.

Begin with four short drills, one per lesson, then complete twelve multiple-choice questions, three per lesson. Labels distinguish SAT-format questions from strategy decisions. Give the evidence or stated condition that decides each item. Model drill responses are examples; another well-supported response may also work.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 1.01

At the end of Module 1, a student says, 'Question 8 is bookmarked, so I can answer it during Module 2. Question 19 is selected, so I must be certain it is right.'

Correct both statements in two or three sentences. Distinguish an entered answer from a reason to revisit it.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Module boundary and response status | Review lesson 1.1

Answer: A bookmark does not preserve access after Module 1 ends or enter an answer. The student should select the best-supported response to Question 8 while the module remains open. A selected answer on Question 19 may still deserve review if there is a specific unresolved issue.

The first statement confuses a reminder with both a response and later access. The second confuses recording a choice with establishing its correctness. Separating these states makes the remaining work visible.

If you missed it: In the next practice module, check that each deferred item has a selected answer and a concrete reason for returning.

Strategy in use: You do not need certainty before selecting a provisional answer. You do need a reason for spending additional review time.

Drill 1.02

A student writes, 'Three Module 2 questions felt familiar. That proves my route, which means my score is already decided.'

Identify the valid observation, two unsupported inferences, and one useful next action.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Separate observation from score inference | Review lesson 1.2

Answer: The valid observation is that three questions felt familiar to this student. Familiarity does not prove which route the student received, and it does not establish a final score. The student should continue solving the remaining questions using their evidence and the available time.

Personal familiarity with a few topics does not describe the difficulty mix of an entire module. Remaining scored responses contribute to performance, so a route guess supplies no reason to stop working carefully.

If you missed it: Replace the thought 'What does this say about my score?' with 'What does this question ask me to establish?'

Strategy in use: This reset avoids an unsupported inference; it does not promise that the route or score will be favorable.

Drill 1.03

A passage states that an archive contains every surviving letter written by a painter to her sister but does not include letters written to other recipients. Two answer choices differ on whether the archive is complete for all the painter's correspondence or only for correspondence with her sister.

Write a note of eight words or fewer that preserves the decisive restriction. Then explain the check you would perform when returning to the choices.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 1.03

Purposeful annotation | Review lesson 1.3

Answer: Note: 'Surviving letters to sister only.' Check whether each choice limits completeness to that group rather than claiming completeness for all correspondence.

The note preserves both limits: the surviving letters and the particular recipient. A note such as 'complete archive' would drop the information that separates the choices.

If you missed it: When annotating a claim, include any small word or phrase that restricts who, when, where, or how broadly it applies.

Strategy in use: The eight-word limit is an exercise in compression, not a test-day rule.

Drill 1.04

You encounter three prompts: one asks for the main purpose of a text, one asks for the transition that most logically completes a passage, and one asks for a sentence emphasizing a similarity between two inventions.

For each prompt, name the job and one final verification check. Do not merely say 'reread.'

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 1.04

Task-specific verification | Review lesson 1.4

Answer: Purpose: identify what the text does, then check whether the choice describes that function rather than only its topic. Transition: identify the relationship between the relevant ideas, then test the inserted word against that relationship. Synthesis: identify a shared feature, then verify that the chosen sentence attributes that supported feature to both inventions.

All three tasks involve meaning, but their success conditions differ. A function, a logical relationship, and a shared feature require different representations and checks.

If you missed it: Before your next practice set, write one short verification question for each task type you expect to encounter.

Strategy in use: A useful check names what could be wrong with the answer; a general reread does not specify what to examine.
