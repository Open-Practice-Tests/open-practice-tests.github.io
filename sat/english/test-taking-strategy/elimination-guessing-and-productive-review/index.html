Review and reference

Make each remaining second serve a defensible decision.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Detect unsupported commitments inside otherwise plausible answers.
  • Resolve close choices through a specific difference and the relevant evidence.
  • Change or keep answers for identifiable reasons, then verify the result.
  • Close unanswered items and direct review toward checks that can improve a selection.

5.1 Expose half-right answers and scope changes

An answer can identify the right topic and still make the wrong claim about it. Read a contender as a set of commitments: who or what, did what, under which conditions, with what degree of certainty? If a passage says that some seedlings grew faster under one laboratory treatment, an answer claiming that the treatment improves all plants outdoors changes the population and setting. Familiar nouns do not repair those changes. A single unsupported commitment can make the whole answer unsuitable, even when the rest accurately summarizes the text.

Use a short defect label to direct your check: wrong group, reversed relationship, added cause, changed certainty, or wrong task. These labels are questions to investigate, not labels to attach because an answer looks suspicious. 'All' is appropriate if the text supports all members of the stated group. 'May' can still introduce an unsupported possibility. An answer does not become defensible merely by sounding cautious. Compare the exact subjects and conditions before judging the strength of its verb.

Separate what the passage establishes from what could also be true. An experiment showing that a material tolerates heat does not by itself establish that the material is inexpensive, easy to manufacture, or suitable for every engine. You need not prove that an unsupported choice is impossible in the world. You need to show that it goes beyond the evidence required by this question. Conversely, do not reject a sound paraphrase just because it replaces the passage's vocabulary with different words.

Make elimination brief enough to help your pace. Once you can name a decisive defect, cross out that choice and direct attention to the remaining contenders. Bluebook's option eliminator can be undone if you discover that your reason was mistaken. [6] A crossed-out choice is a record of a judgment, not a fact. If the final two both fail, reconsider the earliest elimination instead of forcing one to fit. The worked example demonstrates why evidence for survival under one condition cannot carry a claim about a much larger natural process.

W5.1: A successful test with a limited conclusion

To investigate whether the seeds of a coastal plant could survive exposure to seawater, researcher Lina Perez immersed a batch for seven days and then planted the seeds in fresh soil. Some germinated. Perez did not test whether the seeds float or measure the duration of ocean journeys between the plant's island habitats. The results therefore suggest that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

A. the seeds usually reach new islands after floating across the ocean for seven days.

B. the seeds would germinate only if they were planted immediately after seven days in seawater.

C. at least some seeds can remain viable after seven days in seawater, although the experiment does not establish that they disperse successfully between islands.

D. exposure to seawater is necessary for the seeds to germinate in fresh soil.

Practice this lesson: 5.05, 5.06, 5.07.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.1

Answer: C

Completed text: To investigate whether the seeds of a coastal plant could survive exposure to seawater, researcher Lina Perez immersed a batch for seven days and then planted the seeds in fresh soil. Some germinated. Perez did not test whether the seeds float or measure the duration of ocean journeys between the plant's island habitats. The results therefore suggest that at least some seeds can remain viable after seven days in seawater, although the experiment does not establish that they disperse successfully between islands.

  1. Separate the measured event from the larger question: some immersed seeds later germinated; actual transport between islands was not observed.
  2. C preserves both boundaries. 'At least some' matches the observed germination, and the final clause recognizes the untested requirements for natural dispersal.
  3. A is tempting because it connects the experiment to the island habitats. But it replaces survival with successful transport and turns the experimental duration into a journey duration.
  4. Check necessity language in B and D. The experiment did not compare planting delays or establish that immersion was required for germination.

Why A fails: Surviving immersion does not show that seeds float, reach another island, or usually complete a seven-day journey.

Why B fails: Nothing establishes that immediate planting after exactly seven days is the only condition permitting germination.

Why D fails: The passage reports germination after immersion; it does not establish that seeds cannot germinate without immersion.

Key takeaway: Preserve the measured outcome, the tested conditions, and the limits of the conclusion.

Strategy in use: The decisive elimination is about what was measured, not about preferring the longest or most cautious choice.

5.2 Settle the final two with a decisive difference

When two choices remain, stop asking which one sounds more convincing. State their difference in a form you can test. Does one explain a cause while the other merely report a sequence? Does one compare two objects while the other describe only one? Does one supply the main verb while the other leave only a participle? A useful comparison identifies the smallest consequential difference, then returns to the relevant evidence. Repeatedly reading both choices without naming that difference rarely creates new information.

For reading questions, compare each contender with the precise claim, relationship, or role requested. For editing, insert each into the complete sentence and test the disputed structure. For synthesis, translate the goal into a short checklist before comparing. If the student wants to compare materials and purposes, a choice that accurately compares materials alone is incomplete. Truth and task fulfillment are separate checks. The longest choice can fail by adding an unsupported detail; the shortest can fail by omitting a required part.

Keep the comparison small without making your reading too narrow. To decide between two inferences, you may need a qualification in the first sentence as well as the result in the last. To choose a transition, you may need the claim that a new example develops, not merely the immediately preceding noun. Read the part that can settle the difference, and widen that reading when a pronoun, exception, or causal link depends on earlier context. A prediction made before reading the choices remains provisional.

Sometimes you cannot settle the final two within a reasonable first-pass effort. Choose the answer with better support, mark the question if a specific later check seems useful, and move on. Having two uneliminated choices does not establish that they are equally likely to be correct; you may have stronger evidence for one, or may have eliminated the correct answer. On return, perform the missing check rather than restart the entire struggle. In the worked example, the decisive difference is whether both requested comparison dimensions appear.

Task

A useful final-two question

Reading

Which exact claim or role is supported by the passage?

Editing

What changes in the complete sentence when I insert each choice?

Transitions

Which relationship connects the relevant ideas in this direction?

Synthesis

Which choice fulfills every required part of the goal without changing a note?

W5.2: Both true, but only one comparison is complete

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

1. Artist Mara Sato completed the print series Salt Lines in 2018 and Grain Fields in 2021.

2. Salt Lines contains twelve linocuts depicting coastal tides through arrangements of parallel lines.

3. Grain Fields contains six woodcuts depicting farmland through groups of curved marks.

4. Both series appeared in a museum exhibition about Sato's work in 2024.

5. The exhibition also displayed sketches Sato made while planning the two series.

The student wants to compare the subject matter and printmaking techniques of Salt Lines and Grain Fields. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A. Mara Sato depicted coastal tides in the linocuts of Salt Lines and farmland in the woodcuts of Grain Fields.

B. Mara Sato used linocut printmaking for Salt Lines and woodcut printmaking for Grain Fields, two series displayed together in 2024.

C. Salt Lines depicts coastal tides, while Grain Fields depicts farmland; the two series contain twelve and six prints, respectively.

D. Mara Sato's Salt Lines consists of twelve linocuts depicting coastal tides through arrangements of parallel lines.

Practice this lesson: 5.08, 5.09, 5.10.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.2

Answer: A

Correct choice: Mara Sato depicted coastal tides in the linocuts of Salt Lines and farmland in the woodcuts of Grain Fields.

  1. Translate the goal into two required comparisons: subject matter and printmaking technique, with both series identified.
  2. Use the notes to make the pairs: Salt Lines = coastal tides + linocuts; Grain Fields = farmland + woodcuts.
  3. A states both pairs accurately. B is the close rival if you notice only 'printmaking techniques'; it gives no subject matter for either series.
  4. Check C against the second goal component. Its coastal-tides/farmland contrast is accurate, but print counts cannot substitute for techniques. D discusses only one series.

Why B fails: It compares techniques but omits the coastal-tides/farmland subject contrast; the exhibition date does not supply that missing part.

Why C fails: It compares subject matter and print counts, but the goal requests subject matter and techniques.

Why D fails: It accurately describes Salt Lines but supplies no corresponding information about Grain Fields.

Key takeaway: With a compound goal, compare contenders against each required component separately.

Strategy in use: If A and B were your final two, the check 'Does B name either subject?' resolves the choice without rereading every note.

Practice this lesson: 5.08, 5.09, 5.10.

5.3 Change an answer for a specific reason

An answer change should follow a changed understanding of the evidence or rule. Useful reasons include noticing a qualifier, correcting a reversed comparison, finding the sentence's actual subject, or recognizing that a chosen answer fulfills only half of the goal. 'I have looked at this too long' and 'this answer suddenly feels strange' identify discomfort, not a defect. Ask yourself: What did I miss, and how does it make the current answer worse than the replacement? You do not need a written explanation during the test; one precise mental sentence can be enough.

Give the replacement its own test. Finding a flaw in A does not automatically prove B. If you discover that A describes only one of two studies, check whether B actually compares the studies accurately rather than merely mentioning both. In editing, reconstruct the sentence with the new choice, including punctuation already outside the blank. A correction that fixes agreement but creates a fragment is not complete. The object is to improve the answer, not simply to move away from the first selection.

Keeping an answer can also be an active, evidence-based decision. Suppose your original choice says that a finding 'supports' a hypothesis and the text reports evidence consistent with it. A rival claiming that the finding 'proves' the hypothesis may overstate the conclusion. If a targeted reread confirms that distinction, keep the original choice and finish the review. Do not demand perfect certainty about every question before moving on. Once the relevant check supports the selection, repeated reopening can consume time needed elsewhere.

Review your changes after practice in both directions: wrong to right, right to wrong, and wrong to wrong. Record the reason you used at the moment, then ask whether that reason was valid. A fortunate switch based on letter patterns is still an unreliable process; an evidence-based change deserves inspection even when the replacement remains wrong. Look for the missing second check. The worked example shows a justified switch prompted by one previously overlooked word, although, which changes the status of an entire clause.

W5.3: The word a quick scan missed

The observatory's oldest glass plates record star positions from a century ago. Although digital scans let researchers study these images without touching the ______ originals still preserve faint marks that the scans omit.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A. plates the

B. plates. The

C. plates; the

D. plates, the

Practice this lesson: 5.11, 5.12, 5.13.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.3

Answer: D

Completed text: The observatory's oldest glass plates record star positions from a century ago. Although digital scans let researchers study these images without touching the plates, the originals still preserve faint marks that the scans omit.

  1. Suppose your first selection was C because 'digital scans let researchers study these images' looked like an independent clause.
  2. On review, include its opening word: 'Although digital scans let researchers study these images without touching the plates' is dependent. It cannot stand as the first independent clause of a semicolon construction.
  3. D places a comma after the introductory dependent clause. The main clause then reads 'the originals still preserve faint marks that the scans omit.'
  4. Reinsert D into the full sentence and verify both boundaries. The reason to change is the overlooked subordinating word, not a rule that second thoughts are better than first thoughts.

Why A fails: It omits the comma separating the introductory although clause from the main clause.

Why B fails: The period strands the introductory although clause as a sentence fragment.

Why C fails: The semicolon follows a dependent clause rather than an independent clause; the opening although controls its status.

Key takeaway: Name the newly noticed fact, reject the old choice for that fact, and verify the replacement.

Strategy in use: A plausible change from C to D is justified by 'Although.' If D was your first answer, the same check gives a reason to keep it.

Practice this lesson: 5.11, 5.12, 5.13.

5.4 Prioritize useful review and close the module

A review flag identifies unfinished work; it does not tell you how valuable a return will be. At the end of a module, first verify that an answer is selected for every question. College Board states that there is no penalty for guessing. [5] A guess can still be wrong, but leaving a question unanswered gives up the opportunity to answer it correctly. The flag itself is not a selected response. You can revisit questions in the current module, but you cannot return after leaving that module. [2]

Once every question has a selection, prioritize returns with a specific unresolved check and a reasonable time cost. 'Check whether I used the correct table row' is more actionable than 'this passage felt hard.' A suspected copied value or missed negation may be worth checking before a low-confidence vocabulary choice for which you have no new approach. Confidence matters as a signal, but the strongest review target is often the one with an identifiable, repairable defect. Do not invent point values for different questions to justify the order.

Use the clock to limit the scope of the return. If a check should take a brief reread, perform that check; if it expands into a complete restart, reconsider whether the remaining time supports it. Reserve time to confirm the selected answer after making a change. Do not remove a provisional answer while deciding what to replace it with. If the module is almost over and you cannot resolve a question, keep or select the best-supported remaining choice. With no usable evidence, select an available choice rather than leave the item blank.

Avoid two end-of-module traps. First, do not change letters to make an answer pattern look balanced; your selections on other questions do not establish the answer to this one. Second, do not interpret two crossed-out choices as proof of a 50-50 situation. Your eliminations and remaining evidence may be uneven or mistaken. Use any surviving evidence, then finish. The review order below is a training recommendation under stated constraints, not an official scoring formula or a guarantee that the most difficult question deserves the most time.

Current state

Useful next action

An item has no selected answer

Select a provisional answer before an optional extended review.

A specific defect is suspected

Check the exact claim, value, goal component, or sentence structure.

W5.4: A concrete correction beats a vague worry

A student has 1 minute 10 seconds left in the current module and has selected an answer for every question. Question 8 is flagged because two vocabulary choices still feel plausible after two rereads; the student has no new way to compare them. For Question 17, the student has just noticed that the selected answer uses the 2022 row of a table, although the prompt asks about 2023. Checking the correct row should take about 15 seconds. Question 23 is flagged for a punctuation check that should take about 30 seconds.

Which review plan best uses the information available?

A. Begin with Question 8 because it has been uncertain longest, then use whatever time remains to inspect the other flagged questions.

B. Check Question 17 against the 2023 row, verify any replacement, and then use the remaining time for the specific punctuation check on Question 23.

C. Check Question 23 first because grammar questions should receive review priority regardless of what the other flags represent.

D. Keep every current answer because a selected response should be changed only when the student feels completely certain.

Practice this lesson: 5.14, 5.15, 5.16.

Show worked solutionHide worked solution for W5.4

Answer: B

Correct choice: Check Question 17 against the 2023 row, verify any replacement, and then use the remaining time for the specific punctuation check on Question 23.

  1. There are no blank items, so the next priority is a useful review rather than filling missing selections.
  2. Question 17 has an identified mismatch between the prompt's year and the row used. It offers a short, concrete check that could correct a specific error.
  3. After that check, Question 23 offers another defined task. The estimated 15 and 30 seconds total 45 seconds, leaving some of the 70-second reserve for navigation and confirmation; the student must still watch the actual clock.
  4. Question 8 is uncertain, but repeated rereading without a new distinction gives it a weaker claim on this limited review time.

Why A fails: The age of the uncertainty does not supply a new method for resolving it; this plan delays a short check of a known row mismatch.

Why C fails: The punctuation check is useful, but a universal grammar-first rule ignores the more specific and shorter identified error on Question 17.

Why D fails: Complete certainty is not required for a justified correction; the row mismatch is concrete evidence that deserves checking.

Key takeaway: Prioritize a repairable defect and an explicit check, then verify the selected response.

Strategy in use: This order follows the scenario's evidence and estimated time costs. A different pattern of blanks, suspected errors, or remaining time could justify a different order.

Independent practice

Keep these checks in mind

• A half-right answer can fail through one changed group, condition, relationship, or degree of certainty.

• For the final two, name the deciding difference and test it against the passage, sentence, data, or synthesis goal.

• A justified answer change requires a defect in the current choice and support for the replacement; keeping an answer also deserves evidence.

• First ensure that every item has a selected answer, then prioritize short, specific checks of suspected errors.

• Crossed-out choices, subjective confidence, and earlier answer-letter patterns do not determine correctness.

Begin with four short drills, one per lesson, then complete twelve multiple-choice questions, three per lesson. Labels distinguish SAT-format questions from strategy decisions. Give the evidence or stated condition that decides each item. Model drill responses are examples; another well-supported response may also work.

These four written drills are ungraded. Write your reasoning before opening each model answer.

Drill 5.01

A passage reports that 14 of 20 clay samples from one excavation contained traces of a blue pigment. It gives no results from other excavations. A proposed answer says, 'Most pottery at the settlement was painted blue.'

Identify two changes in scope or meaning, then write a narrower claim supported by the evidence.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 5.01

Scope audit | Review lesson 5.1

Answer: The answer expands 20 tested samples from one excavation to most pottery at the entire settlement. It also turns traces of a pigment into a claim about pottery having been painted blue. A supported claim is: 'Most of the tested clay samples from this excavation contained traces of a blue pigment.'

Fourteen is more than half of twenty, so 'most' is supported for the tested samples. The problem is the group it modifies and the added claim about use of the pigment, not the word 'most' by itself.

If you missed it: Underline the noun group governed by each quantity word; verify the verb separately.

Strategy in use: Constructed-response training; this is not an SAT question format.

Drill 5.02

A student must compare how two shelters protect animals from heat. Shelter A uses a reflective roof to reduce solar heating. Shelter B uses open sides to increase airflow. Choice X describes the reflective roof and says that both shelters were built in 2021. Choice Y describes the reflective roof and the open sides, explaining each cooling mechanism. All those facts appear in the notes.

Write the smallest useful final-two question, answer it, and identify the better choice.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

State the decisive difference | Review lesson 5.2

Answer: Does the choice explain the heat-protection mechanism of both shelters? X explains only A's mechanism; a shared construction year does not explain B's mechanism. Y explains both, so Y better fulfills the goal.

Both choices are factually supported. The requested comparison, rather than factual truth alone, distinguishes them.

Drill 5.03

A student initially chooses a semicolon after 'Because the inks faded during storage' in the sentence 'Because the inks faded during storage; the researchers used ultraviolet images to examine the writing.' On review, the student considers replacing the semicolon with a comma.

Explain whether the change is justified. Name the exact word or structure that decides it, and reconstruct the sentence.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 5.03

Give a reason to change or keep | Review lesson 5.3

Answer: The change is justified because 'Because' makes the opening clause dependent. A semicolon cannot join it to the main clause as though both were independent. Corrected sentence: 'Because the inks faded during storage, the researchers used ultraviolet images to examine the writing.'

The comma marks the end of the introductory dependent clause. The replacement has also been checked: 'the researchers used ultraviolet images to examine the writing' supplies the main clause.

If you missed it: Before confirming a punctuation change, include the clause opener and identify the main subject and finite verb.

Strategy in use: Constructed-response training; this is not an SAT question format.

Drill 5.04

You have 45 seconds left. Every item has a selected answer except Question 25, where you have already narrowed the choices but have not clicked either. You also suspect that your answer to Question 12 reverses the direction of a comparison; checking the two relevant sentences should take about 20 seconds. Question 7 remains uncertain after repeated rereading, with no new distinction identified.

Write a short sequence for using the remaining time, including what you will do if the planned check takes longer than expected.

Write a brief answer and identify the words that justify it.

Show model answerHide model answer for drill 5.04

Write a review sequence | Review lesson 5.4

Answer: Select the better-supported remaining choice for Question 25 now. Check the direction of the comparison on Question 12 and verify any replacement. Watch the clock; if that check stops producing useful evidence, keep the best-supported selection and confirm that every item is answered. Do not spend the remaining seconds rereading Question 7 without a new test.

This sequence closes the blank, addresses a specific possible error, and protects the final selections. The 20-second estimate guides the plan but does not authorize exceeding the available time.

If you missed it: When flagging an item, remember the precise question a revisit should answer.

Strategy in use: Constructed-response training; this is not an SAT question format.
